## 1. Why everyone needs POSH awareness

Everyone has the right to a workplace where they feel safe, respected and able to do their best work. Sexual harassment takes that away. It harms health, confidence and careers, it damages teams, and in India it is also unlawful and creates serious duties for the employer.

**This module is for all of us because**

- Anyone can experience sexual harassment, and anyone can see or hear about it
- Anyone can cause harm without meaning to, if they do not understand where the lines are
- A good response from a colleague, in the first minutes, makes a big difference to the person affected
- The law expects every employee to be aware, and expects the employer to train and inform them

**What this module will help you do**

- Recognise sexual harassment, including the less obvious and online forms
- Know your rights, and the options open to you if something happens
- Know how to be a good colleague when you see or are told about it
- Understand how the Internal Committee works and what to expect from a complaint
- Understand the rules on confidentiality, retaliation and false complaints

**The three rules to remember**

1. **Unwelcome is what matters.** It does not matter if the person meant it as a joke or a compliment. What matters is that it is unwelcome to the person receiving it.
2. **You can always speak up, and you will be protected.** Retaliation for raising a concern is itself a serious breach.
3. **Do not wait.** Early action protects people and makes it easier to resolve things. Timelines apply to formal complaints (chapter 11).

**A note on language.** The POSH Act protects women at work, and the law speaks of the "aggrieved woman". ## 2. The POSH Act in plain words

**Where the law comes from.** In 1997, in a case called Vishaka v State of Rajasthan, the Supreme Court set guidelines for preventing sexual harassment at work, because no law existed. In 2013 Parliament passed the POSH Act, which turned those guidelines into law. Its Rules (2013) add the detail on procedure.

**What the Act does**

- Defines sexual harassment at the workplace and says it is prohibited
- Requires employers to prevent it, and to provide a safe working environment
- Requires employers with 10 or more employees to set up an **Internal Committee (IC)** to receive and inquire into complaints
- Sets a process, time limits and possible outcomes for complaints
- Protects the confidentiality of everyone involved, and protects people from retaliation
- Sets penalties for employers who do not comply

**Who is covered**

| Group | Covered? |
| --- | --- |
| Permanent and regular employees | Yes |
| Temporary, ad hoc, daily wage and contract workers | Yes |
| Probationers, trainees, apprentices and interns | Yes |
| Freelancers and consultants working with us | Yes, in practice: treat them as covered, and ask the IC how the policy applies to them |
| Employees of clients, vendors and visitors | The Act can apply where the incident connects to our workplace or work. Report it and the IC will advise |
| Senior leaders and directors | Yes. Seniority never gives an exemption |

The Act's definition of "employee" is wide, and includes people employed directly or through an agent or contractor, with or without the knowledge of the principal employer, for pay or not.

**A few key terms**

- **Aggrieved woman:** the woman who says she was sexually harassed at work. The Act protects women of any age, employed or not.
- **Respondent:** the person the complaint is about.
- **Internal Committee (IC):** the committee set up by the employer to handle complaints.
- **Local Committee (LC):** a committee set up by the district authorities. It handles complaints from workplaces with fewer than 10 employees, and complaints against the employer.
- **Employer:** the person or organisation responsible for the workplace, including its management and head.

**Penalties for employers.** An employer who does not comply (for example, by failing to set up an IC or to act on its recommendations) can be fined up to ₹50,000. Repeat offences can bring a higher penalty and the cancellation of a licence or registration. This is one reason the law takes training and records seriously.

## 3. What counts as sexual harassment

**The legal definition.** The Act says sexual harassment includes any one or more of these **unwelcome** acts or behaviour, whether directly or by implication:

1. Physical contact and advances
2. A demand or request for sexual favours
3. Making sexually coloured remarks
4. Showing pornography
5. Any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature

**Circumstances the Act treats as harassment**

- An implied or explicit promise of preferential treatment at work
- An implied or explicit threat of detrimental treatment at work
- A threat about the person's present or future employment status
- Interference with work, or creating an intimidating, offensive or hostile work environment
- Humiliating treatment that is likely to affect the person's health or safety

**Examples**

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Unwanted touching, hugging, patting or brushing against someone; blocking the way; standing too close; unwelcome massage |
| Verbal | Sexual jokes; comments on someone's body, clothes or looks; sexual remarks about a person's private life; repeated invitations after a "no"; asking about sex life |
| Non-verbal | Staring or leering; sexual gestures; following or stalking; sending or showing explicit images or videos |
| Written and digital | Sexual messages, emails, chats, memes or "reactions"; unwanted late-night messages; sharing intimate images; pressure over video calls |
| Quid pro quo ("this for that") | A manager hints at promotion, a good review or a project in return for a date or sexual favour, or threatens a bad outcome for refusal |
| Hostile environment | Offensive posters or screensavers; constant sexual banter that others cannot escape; a culture where complaints are mocked |

**Intent is not the test.** "I was only joking" and "I meant it as a compliment" are not defences. What counts is whether the behaviour was unwelcome and how it affected the person. The test is what a reasonable person would find unwelcome, and what the person affected experienced.

**One serious act or a pattern.** A single serious incident, such as unwanted touching, can be sexual harassment. Minor behaviours repeated over time can build a hostile environment. You do not need to wait for "enough" incidents.

**Not covered by "sexual harassment" but still not acceptable.** Bullying, shouting, discrimination based on caste, religion, disability or other grounds, and general rudeness are not the same as sexual harassment. They may fall under other policies, and the IC or HR can advise where to take them [reference to other policies].

**Criminal law as well.** Some behaviour, such as sexual assault, stalking, voyeurism and obscene messages, is also a criminal offence. A person can choose to complain to the police as well as, or instead of, using the Internal Committee. The IC can help them understand the options.

## 4. Our workplace includes more than the office

Under the Act, "workplace" is defined widely. It covers any place a person goes to because of their work, so harassment can be covered wherever it happens.

**Places that count**

- Our offices and any shared or co-working space we use
- **Client offices, stores and sites** we visit for meetings, shoots or deliveries
- Transport provided by the organisation, or used to travel for work
- Business trips, conferences, training, workshops and off-sites
- **Your home, when working remotely**, and any place used for work calls
- Work events and celebrations, including parties and dinners, whether held in or out of the office
- **Digital spaces:** work email, chat, video calls, project boards, shared documents and social media used for work

**Remote and digital work**

Video calls, chat and messaging create the same duties as a meeting room. Be aware of:

- Messages outside working hours that are personal, suggestive or pressuring
- Comments about someone's appearance or home during a video call
- Screenshots, recordings or images shared without consent
- Private or "after hours" group chats that exclude people or carry sexual jokes
- Stickers, GIFs, emojis and memes. A suggestive picture is harassment even if it was "just a reaction".
- Friend requests, direct messages and comments on personal social media, when they follow from work and are unwelcome

**Work events and alcohol.** Workplace rules apply at off-site events and parties. Alcohol is never an excuse for behaviour, and the organiser should plan for safe travel home.

**When it happens at a client's place.** If you are harassed by a client or someone at a client site, the organisation still has a duty to you. Report it as you would any other incident (chapter 14).

## 5. Consent, respect and professional boundaries

**Consent at work.** Consent means a clear, freely given yes. At work, flirtation and advances can easily be unwelcome, because people may feel unable to say no.

- **Silence, a smile or a lack of objection is not consent.** People often say nothing out of fear, embarrassment or politeness.
- **A past yes is not a future yes.** Consent can be withdrawn at any time.
- **Power changes everything.** When someone has influence over your pay, your projects, your references or your job, a "yes" may not be free. For this reason, advances from a senior person toward a more junior one are especially risky.
- **A "no" must be respected the first time.** Repeating a request after a no is harassment.

**Signs that behaviour may be unwelcome**

- The person goes quiet, changes the subject, laughs awkwardly or looks away
- They avoid you, or avoid being alone with you
- They say "please stop", "I am not comfortable" or "that is not funny"
- They begin to skip meetings, stop speaking up, or change their schedule or their route

**Before you say or do anything, ask yourself**

- Would I say or do this in front of my manager, the person's family or the whole team?
- Would I be comfortable if it were recorded and shown to the Internal Committee?
- Is this person free to say no to me, with no effect on their work?
- Am I certain that it is wanted, or am I only assuming?

**Professional boundaries to keep**

- Keep comments on appearance out of work, even the positive ones
- Keep one-to-one meetings in open, professional settings, and offer a colleague's company if someone seems uneasy
- Do not ask personal or sexual questions, and do not share intimate stories to get a reaction
- Do not send personal messages late at night or on weekends unless the work truly needs it
- Do not touch a colleague unless you are sure it is welcome. A handshake is usually fine, anything else needs care
- Do not comment on or share photos of colleagues without permission

**Relationships at work.** A consensual relationship between adults is not harassment. But relationships between a manager and someone who reports to them create a conflict of interest and a risk of pressure. Declare them as the policy requires [policy reference], so that decisions on reviews, pay and promotion can be handled fairly. A relationship that ends can lead to unwelcome contact. Continued unwelcome contact after a breakup is harassment.

## 6. Worked scenarios: recognising harassment and grey areas

Use these to test your understanding. In each case, ask: Is it unwelcome? Is it of a sexual nature, or does it create an intimidating or hostile environment? Is power involved?

| Scenario | Is it sexual harassment? |
| --- | --- |
| A manager tells a team member her review "would go better if she were friendlier" and suggests dinner at his place | Yes. This links a work outcome to personal favours (quid pro quo) |
| A colleague regularly comments on a teammate's body in the team chat, and others laugh | Yes. Sexually coloured remarks and a hostile environment, even if the person has not complained |
| A colleague shares a sexually suggestive meme in the team chat, then says it was a joke | Yes, it is likely to be unwelcome and creates a hostile environment. Intent does not decide it |
| Two colleagues have been dating for months and exchange affectionate messages | Not harassment, where both welcome it. Follow the policy on declaring relationships if one reports to the other |
| A colleague invites a coworker for coffee once, hears "no thanks", and accepts it | Not harassment. Asking once and respecting a no is fine |
| The same colleague keeps asking every week after being told no | Yes. Repeated unwelcome advances after a refusal |
| A senior leader gives a team member an unexpected hug at the end of a long project. She stiffens and steps back | Possibly, and it is a warning sign. The hug was unwelcome to her. Seniority raises the risk. The leader should apologise, and not repeat it |
| A client sends a freelancer private late-night messages complimenting her photos and asking to meet | Yes. A client is part of the work context. Report it |
| During a video call, a team lead comments on someone's bedroom and asks about her partner | Likely yes, if unwelcome. Personal remarks in a remote setting still count |
| An employee posts a rude photo of a colleague on a private social media account, outside work hours | Possibly. If the post is about a colleague and it affects them at work, it can come under the Act. Report it |
| Someone says "I hate Mondays" and a colleague replies "me too, let me help you with that" in a flirty way | The reply may be unwelcome. The key is how it was received and whether it continues |
| A manager gives a firm but fair performance warning to a woman who is upset by it | Not sexual harassment. Fair management is not harassment, but the way it is delivered should still be respectful |

**Grey areas.** If you are unsure whether something is acceptable, the safe course is to stop, and ask. If you are not sure whether something that happened to you counts, you can still speak to the Internal Committee for confidential advice. You do not need to be certain before you ask.

## 7. If you experience sexual harassment: your options

**First, know this**

- It is not your fault, whatever you wore, said or did, and wherever you were
- You do not need to prove it before you speak up
- You can choose how to proceed. There are informal and formal routes
- You will be protected from retaliation, and your confidentiality will be protected

**In the moment, if it is safe**

- Say clearly "Stop. That is not okay", if you feel able to. Silence does not mean you agreed.
- Move away, end the call, leave the room, or ask a colleague to join you
- Do not blame yourself if you froze or could not respond. Freezing is a common reaction.

**Afterwards: keep a record**

Write down what happened as soon as you can, while your memory is fresh.

- Date, time and place (or platform)
- What was said or done, in as much detail as you can recall
- Who was present or may have seen or heard it
- How you responded, and how it affected you
- Any earlier incidents, with approximate dates

Save evidence safely: screenshots, emails, chat messages, photos or voice notes. Keep copies somewhere private that you control. Do not delete anything, even if it is hard to look at.

**Your options**

| Option | What it involves | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Talk to someone you trust | A friend, family member, colleague or counsellor | Support matters. You are not required to take any further step |
| Tell the person to stop | Directly, or in writing, or through a trusted colleague | You are not obliged to do this. It is your choice, and it does not replace a complaint |
| Seek advice from the Internal Committee | A confidential conversation to understand your options | Asking for advice does not commit you to a formal complaint |
| Make a written complaint to the IC | The formal route (chapter 11) | A time limit applies. The IC will help you put it in writing if you need help |
| Ask for interim relief | Such as a change of reporting line, seating or a period of leave, while an inquiry takes place | Available once a complaint is made. See chapter 11 |
| Complain to the police | For criminal acts such as assault, stalking or voyeurism | You can do this as well as, or instead of, going to the IC |
| Use outside support | A counsellor, helpline or legal adviser | The organisation can share contacts [list of support contacts and EAP] |

**Timing.** A formal complaint to the IC should be made **within three months** of the incident, or of the last incident if there was a series. The IC can extend this by up to a further three months if it is satisfied that circumstances stopped you from complaining in time. Do not let the deadline stop you from asking questions or seeking advice early.

**If the person is your manager or a senior.** You can still go to the IC, which is independent of the reporting line. The IC must not be influenced by seniority. If the complaint is against the employer (the head of the business), the Local Committee can receive it.

**You will not lose your job for speaking up.** Any step taken against you for raising a concern, such as being sidelined, given worse projects or dismissed, is retaliation and can itself lead to action.

## 8. If you witness harassment: be an active bystander

You do not need to be a hero to help. Small actions can stop harassment and show people they are supported. Use the **5 D's**.

| Action | What to do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | Say clearly that the behaviour is not okay | "That comment is not appropriate. Please stop." |
| Distract | Interrupt without confronting | Ask the person a work question, spill a drink, start a loud conversation, or call them away |
| Delegate | Ask someone with authority or more confidence to step in | Ask a manager, an HR member or an IC member to help |
| Delay | Check in with the person afterwards | "I saw what happened. Are you okay? Can I help you report it?" |
| Document | Write down what you saw, and offer it if the person wants it | Date, time, what was said, who was there |

**Dos**

- Prioritise the safety and wishes of the person targeted. Ask what they want, and respect it.
- Offer practical support: sit with them, walk them out, help them write things down
- Report what you saw to the IC, even if the person is reluctant, if the behaviour is serious or may happen to others [follow the policy on third-party reports]
- Challenge the culture: do not laugh at sexual jokes, and do not forward offensive content

**Don'ts**

- Do not confront the person in a way that makes things worse or unsafe
- Do not spread the story, speculate or gossip
- Do not pressure the person to complain, or to stay silent
- Do not take photos or recordings of the person affected without their consent

**Group chats and online spaces.** If something offensive is posted, do not react with an emoji or a laugh. Tell the poster privately that it is not okay, tell the group, or report it. Screenshot it first. Staying silent signals approval.

## 9. If someone tells you: receiving a disclosure

A colleague may tell you about something that happened to them, because they trust you. How you respond matters a great deal. This applies to every colleague, and especially to managers and team leads.

**What to do in the conversation**

1. **Listen and believe.** Give them your full attention, in private, without interrupting. Do not question whether it really happened.
2. **Say the right things.** "Thank you for telling me." "I am sorry this happened." "It is not your fault." "I will help you find the right support."
3. **Do not promise secrecy you cannot keep.** Explain honestly: "I will keep this as private as I can. As a manager, I may need to tell the IC. I will not tell anyone else without telling you first." [check against your policy: managers may have a duty to report]
4. **Do not investigate.** Do not interview the other person, gather other witnesses or confront anyone. That is the Internal Committee's job. Your involvement could harm the process.
5. **Explain the options.** Share chapter 7: advice from the IC, informal steps, a formal complaint, support services.
6. **Respect their choices.** It is their decision how to proceed, unless the situation is dangerous or the policy requires you to report.
7. **Offer practical help.** Help them write down what happened, find the IC contact, or reach support. Offer to go with them to the IC.
8. **Check in later.** A short message a few days later shows that you care.

**What not to say**

- "Are you sure? He is such a nice guy."
- "Maybe you misunderstood. It was just a joke."
- "What were you wearing?" or "Why did you go there?"
- "You should not make a fuss. It could hurt your career."
- "Let me deal with it myself." (Unless it is a minor matter and they asked for it.)

**Manager checklist when you receive a disclosure**

- [ ] I listened in private, without judging or questioning whether it happened
- [ ] I told them it is not their fault and thanked them for telling me
- [ ] I explained the options, including the Internal Committee, and who they can talk to
- [ ] I did not investigate, confront anyone or tell colleagues
- [ ] I reported to the Internal Committee as the policy requires, and told the person I was doing so
- [ ] I wrote down only the facts I was told, with date and time, and kept the note confidential
- [ ] I took steps to prevent further harm, such as adjusting schedules, if the person asked
- [ ] I checked in with the person a few days later

## 10. The Internal Committee: who it is and what it does

**What the IC is.** The Internal Committee is a committee that every employer with 10 or more employees must set up, in each office or administrative unit. It is the place to take advice and complaints, and it conducts inquiries.

**Who sits on it.** By law, the IC has at least four members:

| Member | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Presiding Officer | A senior woman employee. If none is available, one is nominated from another office, unit or related organisation |
| At least two employee members | Preferably committed to the cause of women, or with experience in social work or legal knowledge |
| One external member | From an NGO or association committed to women's cause, or a person familiar with issues of sexual harassment. This brings independence |

At least half of the members must be women. Members normally serve for up to three years. A member must step down if they are involved in a complaint, or if their independence is in doubt.

## 11. The complaint and inquiry process, step by step

Here is how a formal complaint works under the Act and its Rules. Your organisation's policy may add detail. [Check the process against the policy]

### 11.1 The steps

1. **Written complaint.** The aggrieved woman gives the IC a written complaint, with supporting documents and the names of witnesses, if any. If she cannot write it herself, the Presiding Officer or a member must help her. If she cannot complain herself because of a physical or mental incapacity or death, another person such as a relative, friend or colleague can complain with her written consent, as the Act provides.
2. **Time limit.** Within **3 months** of the incident (or of the last incident in a series). The IC can extend this by up to 3 months, with written reasons.
3. **Conciliation (optional).** Before any inquiry, and only if she asks, the IC can try to settle the matter between the parties. It must not be based on money. If a settlement is reached, the IC records it and passes it to the employer to act on, and no further inquiry takes place.
4. **Inquiry.** If there is no conciliation, or it fails, the IC begins an inquiry. It gives the respondent a copy of the complaint, and he or she must reply within 10 working days. Both sides are heard, can bring evidence and witnesses, and are given a fair chance to respond. The inquiry follows the principles of natural justice. Both parties can ask for copies of the findings.
5. **Interim relief.** While the inquiry is pending, the IC can recommend steps to protect the aggrieved woman. For example, moving her or the respondent to another seat, team or location, granting her up to three months of paid leave on top of normal leave, or stopping the respondent from assessing her work.
6. **Time limit for the inquiry.** The inquiry should be completed within **90 days**.
7. **Report.** Within **10 days** of completing the inquiry, the IC sends its findings and recommendations to the employer. Both sides receive a copy.
8. **Action by the employer.** The employer must act on the recommendations within **60 days**.
9. **Appeal.** Either side can appeal to the appropriate court or tribunal within **90 days** of the recommendations.

### 11.2 Possible outcomes

| IC finding | What follows |
| --- | --- |
| The allegation is not proved | No action against the respondent. If the complaint was made in good faith, the complainant is not penalised |
| The allegation is proved | The IC recommends action under the organisation's service rules, or under the Act's default list below |
| The complaint was knowingly false or malicious | Action can be recommended against the complainant. Not being able to prove a complaint is not the same as it being malicious (chapter 12) |

**Actions the Act and Rules contemplate for a proved case**

- A written apology
- A warning, reprimand or censure
- Withholding of a promotion, pay rise or increments
- Termination from service
- Counselling or community service
- Deduction from the respondent's salary, or payment by him or her, as compensation to the aggrieved woman

**How compensation is decided.** The IC considers factors such as the mental trauma, pain, suffering and emotional distress caused, the loss of career opportunity, her medical expenses, the respondent's income and financial status, and whether the amount can be paid in one sum or in instalments.

### 11.3 What you can expect

- To be treated with respect and without judgement
- To be able to bring a support person to meetings, if the policy allows it [confirm]
- To receive updates on progress and timing
- To have your identity and the details of the matter kept confidential
- To be protected from retaliation during and after the process
- To be told the outcome in writing

**If you are the respondent.** You are entitled to be informed, to respond, to present evidence and to a fair process. You must not contact, pressure or retaliate against the complainant or witnesses. Doing so is a separate breach. You can seek support, and you should cooperate fully.

**If you are a witness.** You may be asked to give a statement. Tell the truth, keep the matter confidential, and know that you are protected from retaliation.

## 12. Confidentiality, no retaliation and false complaints

### 12.1 Confidentiality

Section 16 of the Act forbids publishing or making known the **identity or address** of the aggrieved woman, the respondent and the witnesses, and the details of the complaint, the inquiry and the recommendations, to the public, the press or the media. Our policy goes further and treats any sharing with people who do not need to know as a breach [confirm against policy].

- Do not discuss a case with colleagues, in group chats or on social media
- Do not try to find out who made a complaint
- Do not name the people involved in training, examples or lessons learned
- The IC can share information only with those who need it for the process, and only as the law allows

**Breaking confidentiality** is a breach of the Act and can lead to a penalty under the organisation's service rules.

### 12.2 Retaliation

Retaliation means any bad treatment as a result of raising a concern, taking part in an inquiry, or supporting someone who has.

- Sidelining, excluding or isolating the person
- Worse projects, shifts, ratings or pay
- Threats, gossip, social media attacks or pressure to withdraw
- Dismissal, demotion or non-renewal of a contract

**Retaliation is itself serious misconduct**, and can lead to disciplinary action even if the original complaint is not proved. If you think you are being retaliated against, tell the IC straight away.

### 12.3 False and malicious complaints

The Act allows action against someone who knowingly makes a false or malicious complaint, or gives false evidence or forged documents. But two points matter:

- **Not proving a complaint is not the same as making a false one.** Many true complaints cannot be proved, because there were no witnesses or the evidence is limited. A complaint that is not proved does not lead to action against the complainant.
- **Action is for bad faith only,** and only after an inquiry establishes that the complaint was malicious. This protects people from false accusations, while making sure real complaints are not discouraged.

### 12.4 Records

The IC keeps records securely and for the period required by the policy and the law [retention period]. Records are available only to those who need them.

## 13. What the organisation and its managers must do

- Provide a safe working environment, including for people who come into contact with the workplace

- Help the aggrieved woman make a complaint to the police, if she wants to

- Treat sexual harassment as misconduct under the service rules, and take action
- Monitor the timely submission of reports by the IC

- The IC's annual report is prepared and filed as required

## 14. Harassment by clients, vendors and other outsiders

Harassment does not always come from a colleague. Staff, especially those who deal with clients, can be targeted by clients, customers, suppliers, partners, freelancers or visitors.

**Why it matters**

- The Act's definition of the workplace covers places we visit for work, and the employer's duty to keep people safe does not end at the office door
- A client's seniority or the size of an account is never a reason to put up with harassment

**What to do**

1. **Protect yourself.** Leave the situation if you can. End the call. Ask a colleague to step in.
2. **Keep a record.** Save messages, emails, call logs and notes of what happened.
3. **Report it** to your manager or directly to the IC, as you prefer.
4. **Do not handle it alone.** The organisation decides how to deal with the client, not you.

**What the organisation will do** [Check against policy]

- Take the report seriously and protect the person, and not require them to keep working with the person
- Raise the matter with the client at the right level, and put clear boundaries in place
- Change staffing on the account, if the person wants that, without disadvantage to them
- Where needed, end the work or the relationship with the client or vendor
- Support any complaint made to the IC or the police

**Managers and sales.** Never ask a colleague to "be nice" or "handle" a client who behaves inappropriately, in order to protect revenue. Doing so makes the organisation responsible for the harm.

**Meetings and events with clients.** Plan for safety: meet in public or professional settings, avoid being alone with a client late in the evening, keep alcohol limited, and share your location or schedule with a colleague when you travel for work.

**If you are the one dealing with a vendor or freelancer.** If one of our freelancers or vendors harasses someone, the same process applies. The IC will advise on contractual steps.

## 15. Building a respectful culture

Rules and committees matter, but culture decides whether people feel safe to speak up. A good culture is built by everyone, every day.

**Signs of a healthy culture**

- People can say "that is not okay" and be thanked for it
- Leaders and managers behave well, and are held to the same rules
- Concerns are taken seriously and handled fairly, with no fear of retaliation
- Different people are heard in meetings, and credit is shared
- Everyone knows who the IC members are and how to reach them
- Jokes and banter are inclusive

**Signs of a risky culture**

- "Boys will be boys" or "it is just banter"
- Complaints are mocked, or complainants are called oversensitive
- Powerful people are never challenged
- Most people do not know the policy or who is on the IC
- Women, or other groups, are left out of key roles and decisions
- Social events centre on alcohol, with late nights and pressure to attend

**Everyday habits that help**

- **Language.** Use respectful, professional language. Avoid sexual jokes, nicknames, "darling" or "baby", and comments on looks.
- **Inclusion.** Make sure everyone has a voice in meetings, in client calls and in social plans.
- **Fairness.** Decide assignments, pay and promotion on the work, not on personal closeness.
- **Events.** Make attendance optional, offer non-alcoholic options and plan safe transport.
- **Flexible reporting.** Offer several ways to report, including a woman contact and an external member.
- **Feedback.** Ask people regularly how safe they feel. Act on the answers.
- **Learning.** Review what happened after any incident, with no blame, and improve.

**For leaders.** What you tolerate becomes the standard. Speak about this topic openly, take part in training, back the IC with time and resources, and respond fast to early warning signs, such as repeated complaints about one person.

**Inclusion beyond gender.** Respect applies to everyone: people of all genders and sexual orientations, all ages, castes, religions, abilities and backgrounds. Harassment aimed at someone for who they are, including sexual-orientation and gender-identity-based harassment, damages people in the same way. Report it, and the IC or HR will guide you to the right policy.

## 16. Employee questions and sample answers

These are answers anyone in the organisation can use to explain the basics. They are general information and not legal advice. For anything specific, ask the Internal Committee, HR or a legal adviser.

**1. Does the POSH Act apply to our company?**
Yes, if we employ women, which includes us. An employer with 10 or more employees must set up an Internal Committee. Even smaller employers have duties, and the Local Committee at district level handles those cases. Our policy applies to everyone, and to all workplaces we use, including remote work and client sites.

**2. What exactly is sexual harassment?**
Unwelcome behaviour of a sexual nature. It includes unwanted physical contact or advances, a demand for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature. It also includes promises or threats linked to work in return for sexual favours, and conduct that creates a hostile or intimidating environment.

**3. What if the person says they were only joking?**
Intent does not decide it. What matters is that the behaviour was unwelcome to the person who received it, and how it affected them. A joke can still be harassment. If someone says it bothers them, stop.

**4. Does it have to happen in the office?**
No. It covers any place connected to work: client sites, business trips, events, work transport, work calls and chats, and working from home. A message sent late at night about work-related contact can also count.

**5. Can a man complain, or can harassment be aimed at men or other genders?**
The Act is written to protect women, so the formal Internal Committee process under the Act is for women. Harassment of anyone is wrong, and our policy [confirm against policy] takes complaints from people of all genders and handles them fairly. Speak to the IC or HR, and they will tell you the right route.

**6. What if the person who harassed me is senior to me, or a client?**
You can still complain. The Internal Committee is independent of the reporting line, and seniority does not protect anyone. For clients and vendors, the organisation still has a duty to protect you, and will deal with the client. You will not be penalised for reporting.

**7. Do I have to complain in writing?**
A formal complaint to the Internal Committee must be in writing, but you can ask the Presiding Officer or a member to help you write it. You can also speak to the IC for confidential advice first, without making a formal complaint.

**8. How long do I have to complain?**
Within three months of the incident, or of the last incident in a series. The IC can extend this by up to another three months for good reasons. Speak up earlier if you can, because records and memories are fresher.

**9. What happens after I make a complaint?**
The IC may offer conciliation, if you ask for it. If not, or if that fails, it holds an inquiry, hearing both sides and any witnesses. It should finish within 90 days and send its report to the employer within 10 days. The employer must act within 60 days. You can be offered interim relief while the inquiry runs.

**10. Will my complaint be kept confidential?**
Yes. The law forbids revealing the identity of the people involved, and the details of the complaint and inquiry. Information is shared only with those who need it for the process. Breaking confidentiality is itself a breach.

**11. Could I lose my job or be treated badly for complaining?**
No. Retaliation is prohibited and is serious misconduct. If you experience anything that feels like punishment for speaking up, tell the IC straight away.

**12. What if I cannot prove it?**
You can still complain. Many genuine complaints lack witnesses or documents. If the IC does not find the allegation proved, you will not be penalised, unless the inquiry finds that it was knowingly false or malicious, which is a high bar and different from being unable to prove it.

**13. What is conciliation, and do I have to accept it?**
Conciliation is a way to settle the matter before a formal inquiry, and it can happen only if you ask for it. It cannot be based on a money settlement. You never have to agree to it, and you can choose a full inquiry instead.

**14. What if a colleague tells me they were harassed?**
Listen, believe them, and tell them it is not their fault. Do not investigate or promise to keep everything secret. Explain their options, and help them reach the Internal Committee. If you are a manager, you may have a duty to report, so be honest with them about that (chapter 9).

**15. I saw something inappropriate but it was not aimed at me. Should I report it?**
Yes, you can. Check in with the person affected first, if it is safe, and support what they want. If the behaviour is serious or may happen to others, you can report it to the IC, even if the person is reluctant, as the policy allows. Staying silent can make the behaviour worse.

**16. Is flirting at work allowed?**
Consensual interest between adults is not harassment. But at work, flirting can easily be unwelcome, especially where one person has power over the other. If you are told no, or the person seems uncomfortable, stop at once. Repeated advances after a no is harassment.

**17. Can I be punished for a mistake or for something I did not mean to do?**
Intent is not the test, but fairness is. If someone tells you that you made them uncomfortable, stop, listen and apologise. A first, minor, unintended issue that is dealt with quickly is handled very differently from repeated or serious behaviour. If a complaint is made against you, you are entitled to a fair hearing.

**18. What are the consequences for someone who is found to have harassed another person?**
That depends on the seriousness. Possible actions include a written apology, a warning or reprimand, withholding a promotion or pay rise, counselling, community service, compensation and, in serious cases, termination. Criminal law may apply as well.

**19. Does this apply to remote work and online messages?**
Yes. Chats, emails, video calls and social media used for work all count, and so do personal messages that follow from a work relationship. Sexual jokes, GIFs, unwanted late-night messages and comments on someone's home or appearance can all be harassment.

**20. Does this apply to interns, freelancers and contract staff?**
Yes. The Act's definition of employee covers regular, temporary, contract and daily wage workers, probationers, trainees, apprentices and interns, with or without pay. Freelancers and consultants should be treated as covered, and can report to the IC.

**21. What should I do at a work party or business trip where alcohol is served?**
The same rules apply as in the office. Alcohol is never an excuse. Plan how you will get home, stay with colleagues, and leave if you feel uncomfortable. If someone is harassed, support them and report it afterwards.

**22. Who can I talk to if I am unsure whether something counts?**
The Internal Committee, in confidence: [contact]. You can also talk to HR, a manager you trust, or an outside counsellor [support contacts]. Asking for advice does not commit you to making a complaint.

**23. Does the IC have to be told about every case, even minor ones?**
Concerns can be raised informally with the IC or HR, and small matters are often resolved with a quiet word and an apology. But anything serious, repeated or involving power should go to the IC. If in doubt, ask. The IC will advise you.

**24. What if a complaint turns out to be false?**
Action can be taken if an inquiry finds that a complaint was knowingly false or malicious, or that evidence was forged. This is rare, and it is different from a complaint that simply cannot be proved. Real complaints should never be discouraged.

## 1. Why everyone needs health, safety and wellbeing basics

Health and safety is not only a facilities job or an HR job. An office looks safe until someone props open a fire door, leaves a charger cable across a walkway, or works nine months in pain because nobody asked. A safe and healthy workplace is made of small daily habits by every person in it.

The organisation has a duty of care to the people who work for it: to provide a reasonably safe place and way of working, to explain the risks, and to act on problems that are reported. You have a part too: to look after yourself and others, to follow safety instructions, and to speak up about hazards. The exact legal duties in India depend on the kind of workplace, the size of the organisation and the state, so they are not set out here. [Confirm with HR or a legal adviser which laws and registrations apply to our offices.]

After this module you will be able to:

- Spot and report everyday hazards in the office, at home and while travelling
- Know what to do in a fire, a medical emergency, an earthquake, a flood, a power failure or a security threat
- Set up a desk, at the office or at home, that does not hurt you
- Look after your mental health, notice warning signs in others and talk to a colleague kindly
- Know what support the company offers and how to ask for it
- Report an accident, injury or near miss quickly and without fear of blame

**Rules to remember**

1. If you see a hazard, report it. A near miss is a free warning.
2. In an emergency, protect life first. Laptops, files and client work come second.
3. Know your nearest two exits and the assembly point before you need them.
4. Pain, exhaustion and low mood are signals, not weaknesses. Act early.
5. Staying home when you are properly unwell is the right thing to do, not a failing.
6. Nobody is blamed for reporting in good faith.
7. Managers support and signpost. They do not diagnose.

## 2. A safe office

Most workplace injuries are ordinary: a slip, a trip, a burn from a kettle, a finger trapped in a door. The office is safe when people keep it that way.

### Fire safety

- Know where the fire exits, fire extinguishers, alarm call points and assembly point are. Walk the route once in your first week.
- Never block, lock or prop open a fire door or exit route. Keep stairwells and corridors clear of boxes, bicycles and delivery parcels.
- Do not overload extension boards or leave heaters, kettles or chargers running unattended.
- Do not cook in the pantry beyond what the space is designed for. Keep flammable items such as spray cans and cleaning fluids away from heat.
- Treat every alarm as real. Do not wait to see if it is a drill.
- Extinguishers are for small fires only, and only if you have been trained and your exit is behind you. Otherwise leave. [Fire safety training: yearly, by building management or an approved provider.]

### Exits and assembly points

Every floor should have a posted evacuation plan showing the exits and the assembly point. Learn two routes from your desk, in case one is blocked. At the assembly point, the fire marshal counts people, so stay there and do not go back for belongings. Visitors, interns and freelancers must be accounted for too: whoever invited a visitor is responsible for them in an evacuation.

### Electrical safety

- Plug laptops and equipment directly into sockets where possible. Do not daisy-chain extension boards.
- Do not use damaged cables, cracked plugs or sockets that spark or feel hot. Unplug if safe, tag it, and report it.
- Keep drinks away from sockets and keyboards. Keep liquids off the floor near cables.
- Do not fix electrical faults yourself. Report them to [the facilities contact].
- Use only the charger made for the device, or one of good quality. Poor chargers are a common fire and shock risk.

### Slips, trips and falls

| Hazard | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Wet floor after rain or mopping | Use warning signs, wipe up spills at once, and walk slowly |
| Loose cables across walkways | Tape or route them along walls and report persistent cases |
| Open drawers, bags on the floor | Close drawers, store bags under the desk |
| Poor lighting on stairs or in corridors | Report broken lights the same day |
| Reaching high shelves | Use a proper step stool, never a swivel chair |
| Worn mats and uneven floor tiles | Report them to facilities |

### Lifts and stairs

- Use the lift correctly: let people out first, do not force the doors, and do not use a lift in a fire. Use the stairs.
- If you are stuck in a lift, use the alarm or intercom and stay calm. Do not try to climb out or prise the doors.
- Hold the handrail on stairs and do not carry loads that block your view.
- Keep to the side so others can pass. Do not run.

### First aid

A first-aid box should be easy to reach, and its contents should be checked regularly. Trained first-aiders should be named on a notice. [First-aid boxes checked: monthly, by facilities.] If someone is hurt, call the first-aider, then follow chapter 3 if it is serious. Only trained people should do more than basic care. If you are not trained, stay with the person, keep them calm and call for help.

### Safe use of equipment

- Read instructions before using any unfamiliar equipment, such as a projector cart, a standing desk, a paper cutter or a camera rig.
- Lift with your legs, keep the load close to your body, and ask for help with heavy boxes or monitors.
- Use ladders and steps only when someone is nearby.
- For studio or shoot equipment, check stands and cables, and secure lights against falling.
- Do not use equipment you are not trained for, and report anything broken.

Whoever the office lead assigns each month can use this checklist. Anyone can use it to check their own area.

## 3. Emergencies and what to do

In an emergency, people who have rehearsed in their head act faster. Read this chapter now, and keep the contact table below saved on your phone.

**The universal order**

1. Protect life: yours first, then others if it is safe.
2. Raise the alarm and call for help.
3. Follow the evacuation or shelter instructions.
4. Go to the assembly point, report in, and wait.
5. Do not re-enter until you are told it is safe.

### Fire

1. Shout "Fire" and press the nearest alarm call point.
2. Leave by the nearest safe exit. Do not use the lift.
3. Close doors behind you if you can do so without delay.
4. Stay low if there is smoke. Cover your nose and mouth with cloth if you can.
5. Go to the assembly point and check in with the fire marshal.
6. Call 112 or the fire number [confirm local numbers].

Do not go back for a laptop, a phone or a bag. If a door feels hot, do not open it.

### Medical emergency

1. Check it is safe for you to approach.
2. Call for the first-aider and call 112 or an ambulance number if the person is unresponsive, has chest pain, trouble breathing, heavy bleeding, a seizure or seems seriously unwell.
3. Do not move the person unless they are in danger.
4. Send someone to the gate or lift to guide the ambulance.
5. Note what happened and when, and give this to the first-aider and the responders.

Do not give food, drink or medicines to an unwell person unless a trained person tells you to. This is general information, not medical advice.

### Earthquake

- Drop, cover and hold on: get under a sturdy desk or table, protect your head and neck, and stay until the shaking stops.
- Stay away from windows, glass and heavy shelves.
- Do not run outside during the shaking, and do not use the lift.
- When it stops, evacuate by the stairs if the building or advice says to, and expect aftershocks.
- Follow building management and official guidance. Some areas are more at risk than others.

### Flooding and heavy rain

- If water is entering the building, move to a higher floor or leave if told to. Turn off and avoid electrical equipment if you can do so without touching wet areas.
- Do not walk or drive through floodwater. It may be deeper than it looks and may hide open drains or live wires.
- If roads are flooded, stay put or work from home. No deadline is worth being stranded. See chapter 7.
- Tell your manager and the office lead where you are.

### Power failure

- Stay calm. Emergency lights should come on in common areas.
- Save your work if you can. Do not run through dark areas; use a phone torch.
- Switch off sensitive equipment at the socket so a surge does not damage it when power returns.
- Check with facilities about backup power and how long the outage may last. See Business Continuity for Everyone for work arrangements.

### A security threat or intruder

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A stranger without a pass is walking around | Politely ask who they are meeting and escort them to reception. If you feel unsafe, do not approach. Call building security |
| A visitor refuses to leave or is aggressive | Do not argue or block them. Move away, call security, and alert colleagues |
| A person who may be armed or violent | Leave if there is a safe route. Otherwise lock or barricade a room, silence phones and stay out of sight. Call 112 when it is safe |
| A suspicious bag or parcel | Do not touch it. Move away and tell security |
| Tailgating at the door | Do not hold the door for people you do not know; ask them to badge in |

Never put your own safety at risk to protect property. Report the event afterwards through the process in chapter 14.

## 4. Ergonomics and desk work

Ergonomics is about fitting the work to the person. It matters because most of us sit and use screens for hours, and discomfort that is ignored can become a long-term problem.

### Posture and setup

- Sit with your back supported, your feet flat on the floor or a footrest, and your knees at about the height of your hips.
- Keep your elbows near your body, at about a right angle, with your forearms level.
- Keep your wrists straight when typing and using the mouse.
- Keep your shoulders relaxed, not hunched.
- The best posture is the next posture: change position often.

### Screen height and distance

- The top of the screen should be at or just below eye level, about an arm's length away.
- Tilt the screen slightly back and avoid glare from windows or lights.
- If you use a laptop for long periods, use a stand and a separate keyboard and mouse so the screen is at eye level and your hands are at the right height.
- Use the same distance for text: increase the font size rather than leaning in.

### Eye strain and the 20-20-20 habit

Long screen time can leave eyes tired, dry or sore, and can cause headaches. Try the **20-20-20 habit**: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 metres) away for at least 20 seconds. Blink often, and keep the room light balanced with the screen. If eye discomfort or headaches continue, see an eye specialist. This is general advice, not a diagnosis.

### Breaks and movement

- Take a short break from your seat at least once an hour: stand, stretch, walk, get water.
- Do not eat lunch at your desk every day. A real break helps concentration.
- Use a timer or a reminder app. [Optional: team-wide reminder in the chat tool.]
- Vary tasks. Alternate calls, reading and typing if you can.

### Repetitive strain

Repetitive strain means pain or tingling in the hands, wrists, arms, neck or shoulders from repeated movement or fixed postures. Signs to take seriously include pain that returns, tingling, numbness, weakness and stiffness that does not ease. Do not push through it. Tell your manager, ask for a desk assessment [from HR or the office lead], and see a doctor if it continues. Only a doctor can tell you what is wrong.

**Setup check for the office desk**

| Part of the setup | Aim |
| --- | --- |
| Chair | Adjustable height, supports the lower back |
| Screen | Top at eye level or slightly lower, arm's length away |
| Keyboard and mouse | Close to you, wrists straight |
| Feet | Flat on floor or footrest |
| Lighting | No glare on the screen |
| Phone | Use a headset or speaker, not the shoulder |
| Breaks | A short break every hour |

## 5. Working from home safely and ergonomically

Working from home brings comfort and flexibility, and also its own risks: poor furniture, loose cables, isolation and no clear end to the day. The organisation's duty of care can reach into your home workspace for work, but you are the one who can change the setup. [Confirm with HR how the policy applies to home working.]

### A home workstation that does not hurt

- Use a proper chair if you can. If not, use a firm cushion for back support and a box or books as a footrest.
- Raise a laptop on a stand or a stack of books and use a separate keyboard and mouse.
- Avoid the sofa or bed for long work. If you must, take frequent breaks.
- Sit near natural light but avoid glare on the screen.
- Keep the walkway clear of cables, bags and pets' toys.
- Use a surge protector and do not overload sockets. Do not leave chargers under pillows or on beds.

### Home safety

- Check that your electrical wiring is safe. If sockets spark or get hot, stop using them.
- Keep a charged phone, a torch and a first-aid kit at home.
- Know where the fire extinguisher or exit is in your building.
- Keep water bottles and food away from the equipment.

### Isolation and boundaries

- Keep contact with colleagues: video calls, a short daily check-in, a chat that is not only about tasks.
- Set clear work hours and say when you are offline.
- Have a place to put the laptop away at the end of the day.
- If you share a home with others, agree quiet times and let them know when you are in a meeting.

### Data and security at home

Follow the guidance in Cyber Security Basics for Everyone: lock your screen, use approved tools, and keep your family away from work devices.

## 6. Commuting, working late and travelling for work

Your journey to and from work, and any travel for work, can be the riskiest part of the day. Plan it.

### Commuting

- Plan your route and tell someone when you leave if you travel at night or alone.
- Use pavements and crossings. Do not use headphones at a volume that blocks out traffic.
- Wear a helmet on a two-wheeler and a seat belt in a car. Follow traffic rules. Do not use a phone while driving.
- In heavy rain, check the route, and consider working from home if the roads are bad. See chapter 7.
- If you drive or ride, do not do so when tired, unwell or after alcohol.

### Working late

Late working should be occasional. If it is regular, the workload needs to change, not the person. See chapter 9.

- Tell your manager and security that you are staying late. [Late working: after [8 pm], tell [the security desk].]
- Do not stay alone in an empty office when you can avoid it.
- Make sure the lights, lifts and exits are working and know how to leave safely.
- Arrange a safe way home before you start. [Company cab for working after [9 pm], as per the Expense and Travel Policy.]

### The safety of colleagues travelling at night

- If someone leaves late, check that they have got home. A message when they arrive is enough.
- Encourage colleagues to share a cab only if everyone is comfortable. Do not pressure anyone.

- Managers: do not set expectations that lead to unsafe travel. [Late-night cab for anyone leaving after [9 pm]; ask [HR or the office lead].]

### Travelling to client sites

- Share the address and your arrival time with a colleague. Check how you will get in.
- Confirm that the client's site has safe access, and know their emergency exits and rules.
- Wear suitable clothing and carry water in hot weather.
- Do not enter an unsafe site, and do not agree to work in unsafe conditions to please a client.
- Check in with your manager when you arrive and when you leave.

### Travel away from home

- Keep copies of your ticket, hotel booking and emergency contacts.
- Check local conditions such as weather, strikes and health advice before you go.
- Keep your valuables with you and your devices locked.
- Keep the company emergency contact in your phone. [Travel support line: [phone].]

## 7. Heat, air quality, monsoon and seasonal health

India has strong seasons, and each one affects health and work.

**Seasonal guide**

| Season or issue | Common risks | Sensible habits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat and summer | Heat exhaustion, dehydration, sunburn | Drink water often, avoid strong sun at midday, wear light clothing, and take breaks in cool places |
| Poor air quality, pollution or smoke | Cough, itchy eyes, breathing problems | Check local air quality, limit outdoor time on bad days, and consider a well-fitting mask or working from home |
| Monsoon | Flooding, waterlogged roads, slippery floors, mosquito-borne illness | Plan travel early, avoid floodwater, keep dry shoes at the office, and remove standing water |
| Winter and cold snaps | Colds, flu, cold hands, fog on roads | Dress in layers and leave extra time to travel |
| Festivals and crowds | Crowding, firecracker smoke, travel delays | Plan journeys, keep to safe places and avoid heavy crowds |

### Heat

Signs that someone is too hot include heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps and confusion. Move them to a cool place, give water in sips if they are alert, loosen clothing, and call for help or 112 if they are confused, faint or very unwell. Do not wait. This is general information, not medical advice.

### Air quality

In some places and seasons, air quality can be poor. If it is, use indoor air where possible, reduce strenuous outdoor activity, and talk to your manager if you have a condition that is made worse by pollution. [The company may permit working from home on severe air quality days: [yes or no].]

### Monsoon

- Check weather alerts for your area and travel plans the evening before.
- Do not walk through water near electrical poles or open drains.
- Keep a spare set of dry clothes or shoes at work.
- Remove standing water at home to cut mosquito breeding. Use repellent or nets if advised locally. If you have a fever with other worrying signs, see a doctor promptly; mosquito-borne illness should be assessed by a doctor.
- Do not feel obliged to come in if roads are unsafe. Tell your manager and work remotely.

## 8. Infectious illness and hygiene

Infections spread in offices, in shared spaces, on shared devices and in lifts. A few habits cut the risk.

### Everyday hygiene

- Wash your hands with soap and water for about 20 seconds, especially before eating and after using the toilet or coughing.
- Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or your elbow. Throw tissues away.
- Clean shared surfaces such as meeting room tables, door handles and shared keyboards.
- Do not share cups, bottles or cutlery.
- Keep the pantry clean. Throw away old food. Store food covered.

### Sick days and when to stay home

Staying home when you are properly unwell protects you and others, and is not a mark of weakness. Consider staying home if you:

- Have a fever
- Are vomiting or have diarrhoea
- Have a heavy cough or breathing problems
- Have an illness that is contagious, such as the flu, chickenpox or a stomach bug, until you are no longer infectious
- Are too unwell to work safely or to travel

The details of sick leave, notice, medical certificates and how many days are allowed are in the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone. [Sick leave: [number] days a year; certificate needed after [3] days.] If you are unsure, ask your manager or HR.

**What to do when you are unwell**

1. Tell your manager early, by call or message, and say whether you can work in any form.
2. Hand over urgent work. See Business Continuity for Everyone.
3. Rest. Do not work through illness out of guilt.
4. See a doctor if you are worried, and follow their advice.
5. Tell HR if you have a notifiable or serious illness, or if you need a longer break. [HR contact].

### Managers and sick colleagues

If a colleague comes in clearly unwell, you can suggest they go home. Do so kindly and without drama. Do not ask for medical details beyond what is needed, and keep what you are told private.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A colleague arrives coughing heavily and has a fever | Ask quietly whether they should be at home. Offer remote work if they are well enough, otherwise rest |
| A colleague reports a contagious illness | Thank them. Tell the team there is a case without naming them, if the team needs to know. Ask facilities to clean shared spaces |
| An employee is sick but fears missing a client launch | Reassure them and arrange cover. The launch is a team responsibility |

## 9. Mental health and wellbeing

Mental health is part of health. Everyone has days of pressure or low mood. This chapter is general information, not medical advice, and it is not a substitute for professional help.

### Stress, anxiety and burnout

- **Stress** is the response to pressure. A little can help, but too much for too long harms.
- **Anxiety** is persistent worry or fear that is hard to control.
- **Burnout** is a state of exhaustion from long-term, unmanaged work stress, often with cynicism and a sense of ineffectiveness.

You cannot tell what a person is going through from the outside, and you should not try to label anyone. Be curious, not clinical.

### Warning signs

**In yourself**

- Trouble sleeping or sleeping much more
- Feeling tired all the time, or on edge
- Loss of interest in work or in things you enjoy
- Dread on Sunday evenings or before meetings
- More headaches, stomach upsets or colds
- Drinking more, or relying on substances to cope
- Feeling hopeless or that others would be better off without you

**In others**

- A change in behaviour: quieter, more irritable, withdrawn
- Missing deadlines, making unusual mistakes
- Absences, lateness or presenteeism
- Visible tiredness, tearfulness or panic
- Working long hours with no break

These signs can have many causes and do not prove anything. They are a reason to ask kindly.

### How to talk to a colleague

1. Choose a private and unhurried time and place.
2. Start gently: "I have noticed you seem a bit stretched lately. How are you doing?"
3. Listen. Do not interrupt, fix, or compare with your own problems.
4. Do not diagnose or give medical advice. Say "That sounds hard."
5. Ask what would help. Offer practical support.
6. Point to support: the employee assistance programme, counselling, a doctor or HR. See chapter 10.
7. Keep what you hear private unless there is a risk to safety.
8. Check in again a few days later.

If someone talks about harming themselves or ending their life, take it seriously. Stay with them, stay calm, do not promise to keep it secret, and help them reach a doctor, a helpline or emergency services right away. In an emergency call 112. [Confirm the number is current.]

### How managers support without diagnosing

Managers are not therapists. Their job is to notice, ask, listen, adjust the work where they can, and point to the right support.

- Do ask: "What would help?" Do not guess: "Do you have depression?"
- Do adjust deadlines, priorities or hours where you can. Do not remove all responsibility without asking.
- Do keep conversations confidential and write down only what is needed.
- Do not discuss a person's health with the team. Do not make decisions based on assumptions about their condition.
- Involve HR when you need advice, or the person needs formal support. [HR contact].

### Healthy workloads and boundaries

Overwork is a leading cause of burnout and it is often a workload and planning issue, not a personal failing.

- Raise unrealistic deadlines early, with options (reduce scope, move date, add people).
- Agree working hours and response times. Chats late at night should be rare and for real urgency.
- Plan leave and use it. See Business Continuity for Everyone for handover.
- Managers: check the workload before adding more, and notice who is always the one who stays late.

### Switching off

- Turn off notifications outside working hours, or use scheduled send.
- Keep a clear end to the day: close the laptop, take a walk.
- Take your leave and do not check email on holiday unless it is a true emergency.
- Spend time away from screens: movement, friends, hobbies.

### Sleep

Sleep is not a luxury. Try to keep regular sleep and wake times, avoid heavy meals and caffeine late, and keep screens out of the last hour if possible. If sleep problems last for weeks, see a doctor. [General advice, not medical advice.]

## 11. Substance use and alcohol at work and at events

This chapter is about safety and respect, not judgement.

### At work

- Do not work, drive or operate equipment while under the influence of alcohol or drugs. It is unsafe for you and others.
- Illegal drugs are not permitted at work or work events. Laws on alcohol differ by state, and some states restrict or ban it. [Confirm with HR or a legal adviser what applies in our locations.]
- If you take prescribed medicine that makes you drowsy, tell your manager only what is needed for safety, and ask for a change if necessary.

### At work events

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A team dinner or party with alcohol | Drinking is optional. Never pressure anyone. Offer non-alcoholic drinks and food |
| A colleague seems too intoxicated | Do not let them drive. Arrange a safe ride with a colleague and, if needed, call 112 |
| A client event with drinks | Stay professional. Remember you represent the company |
| An event in a state with alcohol restrictions | Check the rules first. [Event approvals: ask [HR]] |
| Someone is uncomfortable around alcohol | Respect their choice. Do not ask why |

### If you are struggling

Substance use can be a health issue, not just a discipline matter. If you or someone you know is struggling, support is available through the employee assistance programme or a doctor. Managers: if you notice a safety concern, speak privately, focus on behaviour and safety, and involve HR. Do not accuse or diagnose.

## 12. Accessibility and reasonable adjustments

A workplace is only safe and fair if everyone can use it. People may need adjustments because of a disability, a long-term condition, an injury, pregnancy, or another reason.

**What an adjustment can look like**

- A desk, chair, keyboard or screen change
- Flexible hours or working from home on some days
- Step-free access, a ground-floor desk, or a reserved seat near an exit
- Screen reader software, captions, or documents in accessible formats
- A quiet space, or extra breaks
- A buddy during evacuations, for someone who cannot use stairs

### How to ask

1. Tell your manager or HR what you need, not necessarily the reason. You do not have to share a diagnosis unless needed.
2. Agree a trial of the change and a date to review it.
3. Keep a record of what was agreed. [HR keeps this, confidentially.]

### What managers must do

- Listen, and do not assume what a person can or cannot do.
- Keep information private.
- Ask HR for help if you are unsure.
- Plan for evacuation: each person who needs help should have a personal emergency plan. [Fire marshal assigns a buddy.]

Indian law protects the rights of persons with disabilities, and the details of what is required of employers change and depend on the type and size of the organisation. [Confirm with HR or a legal adviser.]

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A designer with a wrist injury cannot use a mouse for weeks | Offer voice input, a trackball or temporary task changes, and review after [2 weeks] |
| A new joiner uses a wheelchair, and the meeting room is upstairs | Move meetings to an accessible room and tell facilities |
| An employee asks to keep their medical condition private | Respect it and share only what is needed with the people who must act |

## 13. Violence, bullying and harassment

Everyone has the right to feel safe at work. Violence, threats, bullying and harassment are not acceptable, whether in person, online, at events, with clients or with colleagues.

### What these are

- **Violence**: physical attack, threats, intimidation or damage to property
- **Bullying**: repeated unreasonable behaviour that humiliates, undermines or isolates someone
- **Harassment**: unwanted conduct that creates an intimidating or hostile environment, including conduct based on gender, caste, religion, disability or other personal characteristics
- **Sexual harassment**: unwelcome sexual conduct. See POSH Awareness for Everyone for the legal framework and the Internal Committee

### What to do

1. Get to safety if you are in danger. Call 112 in an emergency.
2. Write down what happened, with dates and witnesses, as soon as you can.
3. Tell someone you trust: your manager, HR or [the grievance contact].
4. Use the right route: sexual harassment goes to the Internal Committee [name, contact], and other concerns to the grievance process. See POSH Awareness for Everyone and Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling).
5. Expect no retaliation. Retaliation is a serious breach.

### If you see it

- Check on the person afterwards and offer to support them.
- Report it, or encourage them to. You do not have to confront the person.
- If it is safe, say something at the time: "That is not okay."

### Clients and customers

Aggressive or abusive behaviour from a client is not part of the job. Staff can end a call, ask for respectful behaviour, and escalate to their manager. The company will support staff in refusing abuse. [Manager action: [name]]. See also the Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone.

| A senior colleague keeps mocking a junior in a team chat | Support the junior, keep screenshots, and report to HR or the grievance contact |
| A visitor makes a threatening remark in the office | Move away, tell security, and report the incident |

## 15. Returning to work and a manager's duties

### Returning to work after illness or injury

Coming back should be planned, not rushed.

1. Keep in touch in the way you both agree, without pressure to work.
2. Ask for the doctor's advice on what you can and cannot do, if you have it. You need to share only what is needed for adjustments.
3. Have a return conversation with your manager or HR. Agree the first few weeks: hours, tasks, breaks and adjustments.
4. Start gradually if you need to. [A phased return of [2 to 4 weeks] may be agreed with HR.]
5. Review after a short time and adjust.

Managers should welcome the person back, not ask for medical detail, and not expect them to catch up on everything at once. Keep what you know confidential. See the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone for leave and pay details. [Leave rules: confirm with HR.]

### A manager's duties

Managers have a particular responsibility for the health and safety of their teams.

- Know the emergency plan and the contacts, and make sure new joiners know them.
- Make sure workloads are realistic and that people take breaks and leave.
- Respond to reports of hazards, injuries and near misses the same day, and pass them to the safety contact.
- Check in on remote staff, those working late, and those who travel.
- Support a person who is struggling. Notice, ask, listen, adjust, signpost. Do not diagnose.
- Allow reasonable adjustments and keep them confidential.
- Never ask someone to work unsafely, drive when tired, or travel at night without safe transport.
- Join and act on the monthly safety walk. [Safety walk done by: [office lead] monthly.]

## 16. Employee questions and sample answers

These are answers a helpful colleague or manager could give. Adapt them to your situation, and speak to HR for anything specific.

**1. Do I have to report a small injury?**
Yes, please report it, even if it seems minor. It takes a minute and it can show a hazard that others might face. Tell your manager and the safety contact the same day.

**2. What is a near miss and why does it matter?**
A near miss is something that could have caused harm but did not, such as nearly tripping on a cable. It matters because fixing it prevents a real accident later. Report it without worrying about blame.

**3. What number do I call in an emergency?**
In India, 112 is the single emergency number. Separate numbers for police, fire and ambulance also exist in many places. Save 112 and the numbers in the emergency contact table. [Confirm local numbers.]

**4. Should I try to put out a small fire?**
Only if you are trained, the fire is small, and you have a clear exit behind you. Otherwise raise the alarm and leave. Your safety is more important than any equipment.

**5. Can I go back for my laptop during an evacuation?**
No. Leave it. Laptops and files can be replaced and people cannot. Wait at the assembly point until the fire marshal says it is safe.

**6. How often should I take breaks from my screen?**
Stand up and move at least once an hour, and use the 20-20-20 habit for your eyes: every 20 minutes look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. If you have eye pain or headaches that continue, see an eye specialist.

**7. My chair hurts my back. What can I do?**
Tell your manager and ask for a desk assessment. In the meantime, try back support, a footrest and more breaks. If pain continues, see a doctor.

**8. Can I ask for equipment to work from home?**
Ask your manager or HR what the company provides. [Allowance or equipment loan: [as per the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone].] Even without it, small changes like a laptop stand and a separate keyboard can help.

**9. I travel home late. What does the company expect?**
You should not have to take risks to get home. Use an approved cab, share your trip, and tell your manager when you are staying late. [Late-night cab rules: [as per the Expense and Travel Policy].]

**10. What if roads are flooded and I cannot reach the office?**
Do not travel through floodwater. Tell your manager and work remotely if you can, or take the day as agreed. No deadline is worth your safety.

**11. When should I stay home sick?**
When you have a fever, are vomiting, have a heavy cough or a contagious illness, or are too unwell to work safely. Tell your manager early and hand over what is urgent. Leave rules are in the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone.

**12. Will I be judged for taking sick leave?**
You should not be. Taking leave when you are unwell is a normal use of a benefit. If you feel pressure, speak to HR.

**13. How do I know if I am burnt out?**
Only a professional can say, but signs include constant tiredness, dread about work, cynicism and feeling you cannot cope. If this sounds like you, talk to someone you trust and consider the employee assistance programme or a doctor.

**14. A colleague seems very low. What should I say?**
Find a private moment and say what you have noticed, kindly: "You seem a bit down lately. Are you okay?" Listen, do not give advice, and point them to support. If you fear for their safety, get help straight away.

**15. Is the counselling really confidential?**
Counselling through the employee assistance programme is designed to be confidential, within the limits set by the provider. [Confirm limits with the provider.] Your manager will not be told what you discuss.

**16. Is there a mental health helpline in India?**
Tele-MANAS (14416) is a government helpline for mental health support. [Confirm the number is current.] In an emergency call 112.

**17. Can I drink at a company event?**
It is up to you, and it is also fine not to. Stay responsible, never drive after drinking, and respect others' choices. Rules differ by state. [Confirm with HR.]

**18. Who do I tell if I need a workplace adjustment?**
Tell your manager or HR. You do not need to give a diagnosis unless it is needed. They will agree changes and a review date with you, and keep it confidential.

**19. What if a client is abusive to me?**
You do not have to put up with it. End the call politely, write down what happened, and tell your manager. The account owner will handle the client.

**20. I have been unwell for a long time and I am coming back. What happens?**
Speak to HR and your manager before your return. They will agree a plan for hours, tasks and adjustments, and review it. You do not need to share more medical detail than is needed.

# Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools

This module is for everyone in the organisation: developers, designers, marketers, writers, project managers, finance, admin, sales, leaders, interns and freelancers. It explains how to use company devices, accounts, software, email, the internet, cloud storage and client systems responsibly, and how to use AI tools safely, including what may and may never be put into them. It is written for non-specialists and uses situations from a digital agency that builds and grows online stores. It is general information and does not replace the organisation's own written acceptable use policy, the contracts we have signed with clients, or applicable law.

Draft of 2026-10-30. Names, contact details, time limits, entitlements and policy references in this module are proposed defaults shown in [square brackets]. Edit them to match the organisation's real policy, and have the module checked by a qualified adviser where it touches law.

## 1. Why everyone needs clear rules for IT and AI

Our work runs on laptops, accounts, shared drives, ad platforms, store admins and, more and more, AI tools. These make us faster. They also hold clients' customer data, plans and passwords, so a small slip can cost a client a great deal.

**Purpose of this policy-style module**

- Protect clients, colleagues and the company from harm and legal trouble
- Make it clear what is allowed, so that people do not have to guess
- Let people use good tools, including AI, with confidence and without hiding anything

**Scope.** These rules apply to everyone who uses company systems or works on company or client matters: employees, interns, freelancers, contractors and vendors' staff with access. They apply on company devices, on personal devices used for work, and on any account used for work, at the office, at home or while travelling.

**What this module will help you do**

- Know what you may do with company and personal devices
- Use approved software and ask for new tools the right way
- Use email, chat, the internet and cloud storage professionally
- Use client systems and accounts only for authorised work
- Use AI tools safely, check their output, and respect copyright, people and consent
- Report mistakes quickly and know what happens if rules are broken

**The rules to remember**

1. **Work data goes only to approved places and tools.** If a tool is not approved, client data does not go in.
2. **You are responsible for what you send out,** including anything an AI tool wrote for you.
3. **Ask early, report fast.** A question before you start or a report straight after a mistake costs far less than a cover-up.

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 9 for the everyday IT rules. Read chapters 10 to 12 carefully before using any AI tool for work, as that is where most new risks arise. Chapter 13 explains reporting, consequences and a manager's extra duties. For password, phishing and device security basics, see Cyber Security Basics for Everyone, which this module does not repeat. For personal data and confidentiality, see Data Privacy and Confidentiality for Everyone. Use the questions in chapter 14 and the glossary in chapter 15 for quick reference. If you are unsure, ask [IT contact] before you act.

## 2. Company devices and personal devices

### Company devices

A company device is a laptop, phone, tablet or other equipment the organisation owns and gives you. It is provided for work.

**Do**

- Keep the device updated, locked with a strong screen lock, and storage encrypted as set up by [IT contact]
- Use it for work and the limited personal use in chapter 7
- Keep it with you or in a safe place, not in an unattended car or on a cafe table
- Report loss or theft to [IT contact] straight away, whatever the time

**Do not**

- Disable security tools such as antivirus, updates or disk encryption
- Install software that is not approved (chapter 4)
- Let family, friends or other people use it
- Lend it, sell it or hand it back without [IT contact] wiping it properly
- Leave work signed in on a shared or public computer

### Personal devices (BYOD)

BYOD means "bring your own device": using your own phone, laptop or tablet for work. We allow it only as follows. [confirm BYOD policy with the owner]

| Item | Proposed default |
| --- | --- |
| Email and chat on a personal phone | Allowed, if the phone has a screen lock, current updates and [the approved work profile or management app] |
| Client files on a personal laptop | Not allowed by default. Work in the approved cloud tools in a browser instead, and do not download |
| Client customer data or credentials on a personal device | Not allowed |
| Saving work files to a personal device's gallery, downloads or personal cloud backup | Not allowed |
| Rooted, jailbroken or unsupported devices | Not allowed |
| Shared family devices | Not allowed for work |
| Leaving the company | Work profile or accounts removed. Personal data is not touched |

**Why the line is firm.** On a personal laptop we cannot control who else uses it, what else is installed, or whether it backs up client data to a personal cloud. If the device is lost, we cannot wipe it. Client contracts often forbid client data on personal devices.

**Your privacy on a personal device.** The company manages only the work profile or work accounts. It does not read your personal messages or photos. [confirm with a legal adviser and IT contact]

## 3. Accounts and sign-in

Every account you use for work is a door into client data. Rules about strong passwords, a password manager, two-step sign-in, phishing and device security are in Cyber Security Basics for Everyone. This chapter adds only the acceptable use rules.

**Do**

- Use your own named account for every tool. Your actions are traced to you
- Use the [approved password manager] for work credentials
- Use work email for work accounts, and personal email for personal ones
- Sign out of accounts on devices you do not own

**Do not**

- Share your password, one-time codes or approval prompts with anyone, including IT, a manager or a client
- Use a work password anywhere personal, or a personal password for work
- Sign into work tools with a personal social media or Google account where company login is available
- Let someone else work under your login "just for a day"
- Store passwords in notes, spreadsheets, chat or on sticky notes

### Shared and generic accounts

Some tools only offer one login, such as a client's old store admin or a social media page. Shared accounts hide who did what and are hard to secure, so we allow them only as a last resort.

| Rule | Detail |

| If a shared account is unavoidable | Get approval from [IT contact] and the project lead, keep the login in the [approved password manager] shared vault, and record who has access |
| Never use "admin@" or similar generic accounts for convenience | Use named accounts with the right role |
| When someone leaves the project | Change the shared password the same day and remove their access |
| Two-step sign-in | Use it on shared accounts through a device or number controlled by the company [confirm with IT contact] |

## 4. Approved software, licensing and requesting new tools

### Why only approved software

Every tool we use can hold data, install code on a device, or connect to client systems. Approval means someone has checked its security, its terms, what it does with data and its licence cost. Unapproved tools are a leading cause of leaks, malware and surprise bills.

**What counts as software**

- Apps and programs on laptops and phones
- Browser extensions and plugins
- Online tools and websites you sign up to with a work email
- Plugins and apps installed on client stores or ad accounts
- AI tools (more in chapters 10 to 12)

### Use the approved list

[IT contact] keeps the list of approved tools at [approved tools list]. If a tool is on it, use it for the stated purposes. If it is not, do not use it with company or client data until it is approved.

### Licensing and no piracy

- Use software only under a valid licence that covers your use. Company licences are for company work
- Never use cracked, pirated or "free full version" software, fonts, themes, plugins, stock images, music or templates. They break the law, often carry malware, and put the client's store at risk
- Do not copy licensed software to personal devices or share licence keys
- Check licence terms for fonts, stock images and music before using them in client work, including commercial use
- Open-source code and themes have licences too. Some require credit or require you to share changes. See chapter 12

### How to request a new tool

1. **Check the approved list first.** An equivalent tool may exist.
2. **Send a request to [IT contact]** at [it-requests@company] with: the tool name, what you will use it for, what data it will touch, who needs access, the cost and the client involved, if any.
3. **Wait for a decision.** The proposed target is [5 working days]. Urgent client needs can be flagged.
4. **Use dummy or public data only** while it is being assessed, and never client data.
5. **If the client requires the tool,** tell [IT contact] and the project lead, as it may need to be on the client's own account.

## 5. Email, messaging and internet use

### Email and messaging etiquette

Company email and chat represent the organisation. Messages can be forwarded, kept and used in disputes, so write as if a client may read it.

**Do**

- Be respectful, clear and professional, and keep to work in work channels
- Check the recipients, especially auto-completed addresses and reply-all
- Use bcc for external group emails so addresses are not shown
- Use the right channel: email for formal and external matters, chat for quick internal questions, a project tool for tasks and decisions
- Keep client conversations in the approved channels, and record decisions in writing
- Respect working hours. Do not expect replies at night unless the role requires it [confirm with HR policy]
- Use a professional signature with [the standard signature template]

**Do not**

- Send harassing, discriminatory, threatening, abusive or sexually suggestive messages, jokes or images. See Code of Conduct and Ethics and POSH Awareness for Everyone
- Send chain messages, spam or personal mass mailings
- Use work email to run a personal business or to sign up for personal shopping, dating or similar services
- Put passwords or full customer lists in email or chat
- Forward client emails to personal accounts
- Speak for the company on social media from a work or personal account unless authorised (see Social Media and Public Communication)
- Use screenshots of private chats in public without consent

### Internet use

The company provides internet for work. Reasonable, brief personal browsing is fine (chapter 7).

**Do not use company devices or networks to**

- Visit sites that are illegal, sexually explicit, hateful, or promote violence or gambling
- Download pirated films, music, games or software, or use file-sharing and torrent sites
- Run cryptocurrency mining, VPNs not approved by [IT contact], or tools to bypass company security or filters
- Attack, probe or scan any network or system, or try to get past access controls
- Access sites for the purpose of harassment or to post anonymously about colleagues or clients

If you reach a harmful site by accident, close it and tell [IT contact]. An honest accident is not a problem.

## 7. Personal use and monitoring

### Personal use limits

We trust people to use judgement. Limited personal use of company devices and internet is acceptable when it:

- Is brief and does not interfere with your work or others' work
- Does not use much bandwidth or storage
- Does not break any rule in this module, the law or the Code of Conduct and Ethics
- Does not involve running a business, paid side work, campaigning or fundraising
- Does not store personal files that would mix with work data
- Is not on a client's systems or accounts, ever

Treat personal use as a privilege that can be limited if it is abused.

### What the company may monitor and why

The company may monitor use of its systems. Reasons include security, finding and stopping misuse, protecting client data, keeping systems working, meeting legal and contractual duties, and investigating complaints.

**What may be monitored (proposed defaults)**

- Company email, chat and files on company systems
- Internet traffic and sites visited on company networks and devices
- Login activity, device status and installed software
- Use of AI tools and cloud services provided by the company

**How monitoring is meant to work**

- It is limited to what is needed for the stated purposes
- Access to the content of someone's messages needs a reason and the approval of [senior approver] and is handled by [IT contact] or HR, not casually
- Monitoring data is kept only as long as needed [retention period] and protected
- It is not used to snoop on private life, personal accounts or personal devices outside the work profile

**Important.** Monitoring rules must follow Indian law, including data protection and employment rules, and be explained clearly to staff. The owner must confirm the policy wording, notice and consent approach, and what is lawful, with a legal adviser. [confirm with a legal adviser]

**Have no expectation of privacy** in personal messages, files or browsing that you choose to do on company systems. If something is truly private, do it on your own device and your own network, outside work time.

## 10. AI tools: approved, unapproved and what may go in

AI tools, such as chatbots, writing assistants, image and video generators, meeting note-takers and code assistants, can save hours. They also send what you type to another company, may keep it, and sometimes use it to train models. How they are used at work needs clear rules.

### Approved and unapproved tools

| Category | Meaning | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approved AI tools | Reviewed by [IT contact] and listed at [approved AI tools list], with stated uses and data limits | Use them for the stated uses. Respect the data limits |
| Unapproved AI tools | Not reviewed, or reviewed and declined | Do not use for company or client work. Do not enter any company or client data |
| "Shadow AI" | Using unapproved AI tools or personal AI accounts for work, often with good intentions | Do not do it. If you already did, tell [IT contact] without fear, so risks can be managed |

**Personal AI accounts.** A free personal account on a public chatbot is outside the company's control. Work content must not go into it, even if you think it is harmless.

**Why approval matters**

- Many free tools may keep your inputs, use them to improve models, or allow staff to review them
- Terms differ, and change. A business plan with data protection terms is different from a free consumer account
- Clients increasingly forbid or restrict AI use on their data in their contracts
- AI features are appearing inside tools we already use, so check the settings and approval status

### What may and may never go into an AI tool

The rule depends on the tool. In an **approved** tool you may use public and internal information within its stated limits. In an **unapproved** tool, you may only use public information or content you wrote yourself that has no client or company data.

| Type of information | Approved AI tool | Unapproved AI tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public content (published pages, public product descriptions) | Allowed | Allowed, with care |
| Internal low-risk content (a generic brief that names no client) | Allowed within limits | Not allowed |
| Client confidential information (strategy, pricing, unreleased work, performance figures) | Only if the tool is approved for client data and the client's contract allows it [check each contract] | Never |
| Customer lists, order exports, emails, phone numbers, any personal data | Never, unless the tool is specifically approved for personal data and the client has agreed in writing | Never |
| Credentials, API keys, access tokens, private keys | Never | Never |
| Unreleased campaigns, creatives, product launches | Only if approved for that client and project | Never |
| Contracts, NDAs, legal advice, financial statements | Never, unless approved and cleared by [legal contact] | Never |
| Children's data or health or financial information | Never | Never |

If you are not sure, the safe answer is no. Ask [IT contact] or [privacy contact].

### Prompt hygiene

Good prompts get better results and keep data safe.

- **Describe, do not paste.** Say "a skincare brand selling mostly to women aged 25 to 40" instead of pasting the client brief
- **Remove names and numbers.** Replace client names, people's names, emails, phone numbers and amounts with placeholders such as [CLIENT] and [CUSTOMER]
- **Use sample or fake data** for testing, such as a dummy customer list of ten invented rows
- **Do not paste links** to private documents. Some tools can read them
- **Do not upload files** unless the file is public or the tool is approved for that type of data
- **Check the settings.** Turn off chat history or training on your data where the tool allows, and use a work account
- **Keep a record** of important prompts and outputs in the project file when the output is used in client work
- **Do not trust instructions inside pasted content.** Text on a web page or in a document can try to manipulate an AI tool. Review what it does before you act

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A colleague pastes a client's customer list into a free chatbot to "group people by city" | Do not do this. Personal data in an unapproved tool is a possible breach. Report to [privacy contact] and [IT contact]. See Data Privacy and Confidentiality for Everyone |

## 11. Checking AI output, ownership, disclosure and bias

### Check AI output before it reaches a client

AI tools produce fluent text that can be wrong. They can invent facts, quotes, statistics, links, product claims and legal statements ("hallucinations"). You remain responsible for everything you send, whoever or whatever wrote the first draft.

**Before AI output goes to a client or the public**

- [ ] I checked every fact, figure, date and name against a reliable source
- [ ] I checked that quotes, studies and links exist and say what the tool claims
- [ ] I checked product, health, finance, legal and pricing claims against what the client has approved
- [ ] I read it for tone, brand voice and cultural fit for India and the target audience
- [ ] I checked for copied text or images that look like someone else's work
- [ ] I checked for bias or stereotypes (see below)
- [ ] I removed generic filler and made sure it says something specific and true
- [ ] A second person has reviewed it where the stakes are high

### Copyright and ownership of AI output

The law on copyright and AI is unsettled in many countries, including India, and is changing. Do not assume that AI output is free to use, owned by us, or protected from copying by others. [confirm with a legal adviser]

**Practical rules**

- Treat AI output as a draft, and add real human work to it
- Do not ask AI to imitate a named living artist, writer, photographer or brand style
- Do not use AI tools to reproduce copyrighted text, images, music or code that you do not have rights to
- Do not put AI-generated work in client deliverables if the contract or the client forbids it
- Read the tool's terms on ownership and commercial use. Free tiers often limit them
- If an image looks like a real brand or celebrity, do not use it
- Keep records of how important deliverables were made

### Disclosing AI use to clients

Clients increasingly care whether AI was used on their work. Be honest.

- Follow the contract. If it requires disclosure or approval, do it
- Where the contract is silent, follow [the company's AI disclosure default], for example telling the client when AI was used in a meaningful part of a deliverable [confirm with the owner]
- Never claim work is fully human-made when it is not, or pass off AI output as the client's own research
- If a client asks, answer truthfully, and tell the project lead

### Bias and fairness

AI tools learn from data that reflects human bias, so they can produce stereotypes about gender, caste, religion, region, age, body type and skin colour.

- Check images and copy for stereotypes, for example only showing certain people as leaders, cooks or customers
- Do not use AI to make decisions about people, such as hiring, firing, credit or customer eligibility
- Do not use AI to guess personal traits of customers such as religion, health, caste or sexual orientation
- Use diverse, respectful inputs and review the results. Ask a colleague if unsure

### Scenario table

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| An AI tool gives a statistic for a client's blog: "73% of Indian shoppers prefer COD" | Do not publish it until you find the original source and confirm the number and date. If you cannot, remove it |
| A writer submits a blog post that is mostly AI text, with no checking | Ask them to rework and fact-check it, and follow the client's AI disclosure rules before delivery |
| A designer uses an AI image tool that produces a logo near-identical to a known brand | Do not use it. Create an original design or ask the client |
| The client asks "did you use AI for this report?" | Answer honestly and tell the project lead |
| A recruiter wants to use an AI tool to rank applicants' CVs | Do not do this without HR, [IT contact] and legal approval. Hiring decisions need human judgement and fairness checks |

## 12. AI images, voices, meetings, code and deepfakes

### AI images and voices in ads

AI-generated images, video and voice can speed up creative work. They also create special risks.

- **Real people.** Do not generate or edit images, video or voices of a real person, such as a celebrity, a founder, a customer or a colleague, without their written permission. Do not use AI to create a testimonial from a person who did not give it
- **Fake reviews and endorsements.** Do not use AI to create reviews, testimonials, "customer photos" or before-and-after results that did not happen. This can mislead customers and break advertising and consumer rules [confirm with a legal adviser]
- **Realistic products.** Do not use AI images to show a product's results or features that it does not have
- **Platform rules.** Ad platforms and marketplaces have policies on AI-generated and synthetic media, including labelling. Check them before launch
- **Client approval.** Get the client's written approval for AI-generated assets, and tell them which parts are AI
- **Labelling.** Label AI-generated or altered content where the platform, law, client or good practice requires it

### Deepfakes

A deepfake is realistic fake media of a real person's face or voice. They are used for fraud, harassment and misinformation, and creating or sharing them can be illegal in India. [confirm with a legal adviser]

- Never create a deepfake of any person, as a joke or otherwise
- Do not share one you receive. Report it to [IT contact] or your manager
- Be alert to scams: a voice note or video call from "the boss" asking for money or credentials may be fake. Verify through another channel before acting. See Cyber Security Basics for Everyone

### AI meeting recorders and transcription bots

AI note-takers can join a call, record, transcribe and summarise it. They are useful, and they capture everything said, including client secrets and personal details.

**Before using one**

1. **Ask for consent.** Tell everyone on the call before it starts, and get a clear yes. If anyone objects, switch it off
2. **Check the client's rules.** Many clients forbid recording or third-party bots on their calls. Ask first
3. **Use only approved tools,** with a work account and the right settings. Do not use personal bot accounts
4. **Decide where the recording goes.** Store transcripts in the approved client folder. Delete them when no longer needed
5. **Do not record** sensitive meetings, such as HR matters, disciplinary conversations, legal advice, or anything with personal or financial details, unless approved
6. **Review the summary** before circulating. Summaries can miss, misstate or add things

### Code assistants and licences

Code assistants suggest and write code. They are very helpful, with extra cautions.

- Use only approved code assistants, with the privacy settings that [IT contact] sets
- Do not paste a client's proprietary code, keys, customer data or database dumps into unapproved tools
- Review all generated code for security (for example injection and exposed secrets), performance and accessibility. Test it before it goes live
- AI-generated code may resemble open-source code with licences that have conditions. Check for licence notices, and avoid pasting large blocks you cannot trace [confirm with a legal adviser]
- Do not let AI tools run commands or change a live client store without human review and a backup
- Record in commit messages or notes where AI helped, if the client or company requires it

### Scenario table

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| An AI note-taker joins a client call without being invited because a colleague's account is linked to it | Ask the colleague to remove it at once. Tell the client the bot was not intended, and apologise. Report to [IT contact] and the project lead |
| Your team wants to record a client strategy call and have AI summarise it | Ask the client for consent, check their rules, use only the approved tool, and store the transcript in the client folder |
| A marketer wants to use an AI-generated "customer" photo with a review for a landing page | Do not. It would be a fake testimonial. Use real customer content with permission |
| Someone in the team chat shares a funny AI-made video of a colleague | Ask them to remove it. Creating or sharing fake media of a colleague without consent is not acceptable. Report if it continues |
| A developer pastes a client's whole theme file into an AI tool that is not approved | Report to [IT contact]. Proprietary code and possibly keys have been exposed |
| A free AI voice tool offers to clone the founder's voice for an ad | Do not use it without the founder's written permission and the client's approval, and use only approved tools |

## 13. Reporting, consequences and a manager's extra duties

### Reporting misuse and mistakes

Everyone makes mistakes. The aim is to find out quickly and fix it.

**Report straight away if**

- You put data in a tool that was not approved, or sent it to the wrong person
- You lost a device, a drive or a paper file
- You clicked a suspicious link or approved a prompt you did not expect
- You installed something that you now doubt
- You see a colleague, vendor or freelancer misusing systems or data
- A client complains about how we used their systems or tools
- An AI tool produced content that is harmful, biased or may infringe someone's rights

**How to report**

1. Stop using the tool or device if it is safe to do so. Do not delete evidence
2. Tell [IT contact] at [it-security@company], and [privacy contact] if personal data is involved, within [1 hour] of noticing
3. Tell your manager or the project lead
4. Write down what happened, when, what data and which tool
5. Help with follow-up

Reports made honestly and quickly are treated as a positive step, and will not be used against you. Concealing an incident, deleting evidence or blaming others is treated seriously. If you do not feel able to report through these routes, see Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling).

### Consequences

Breaking these rules can harm clients, colleagues and the company. The response depends on severity, intent and history, and follows the HR process. [confirm with HR]

| Type | Examples | Likely response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honest mistake, reported quickly | Wrong recipient, an unapproved tool tried once | Coaching, fix the issue, learn |
| Careless or repeated breach | Repeatedly ignoring the approved tool list, sharing a password | Written warning, retraining, access limits |
| Serious breach | Pasting client personal data into an unapproved AI tool, using client accounts for personal gain, hiding an incident | Formal disciplinary action, up to termination or ending a contract, and notice to the client |
| Illegal acts | Piracy, fraud, deepfake abuse, data theft | All of the above, plus involvement of authorities and legal action where appropriate |

### A manager's extra duties

Managers and project leads set the tone and are responsible for their teams' compliance.

- Make sure everyone in the team, including interns and freelancers, has read this module and knows the approved tool list
- Make sure access is requested for named people only, with the least access needed, and removed on time
- Check client contracts for rules on AI, devices, data location and confidentiality, and brief the team [check each contract]
- Do not pressure people to use unapproved tools to meet a deadline. Raise it with [IT contact] instead
- Welcome questions and reports without blame
- Review shared and generic accounts regularly and keep a list
- Act at once on reports, and escalate to [IT contact] and [privacy contact] the same day
- Do not read an employee's private messages or ask IT to do so outside the approval process
- Tell the team promptly when a client changes their rules
- Lead by example, including in how you use AI tools

**Manager's checklist at project start**

- [ ] The team knows which tools are approved for this client
- [ ] Client rules on AI, recording and devices are written down and shared
- [ ] Access is requested by name, with an end date
- [ ] The freelancers have signed confidentiality terms
- [ ] The team knows who to report to

## 14. Employee questions and sample answers

These are questions colleagues often ask. Use the answers as a guide, and ask [IT contact] when your situation is different.

**1. May I use my personal laptop for work if mine is being repaired?**
Use the approved cloud tools in a browser, and do not download client files. If you need more, ask [IT contact] for a loan laptop. Client contracts often forbid client data on personal devices.

**2. Can I read my work email on my personal phone?**
Usually yes, if your phone has a screen lock, current updates and the approved work profile or app. Tell [IT contact] when you change phones, and report loss at once.

**3. How do I get a new tool approved?**
Check the approved list, then send a request to [IT contact] with the tool, purpose, data involved, users and cost. A decision is targeted within [5 working days]. Do not use client data in the tool meanwhile.

**4. Is a free browser extension really a problem?**
It can be. Some extensions can read everything you view, including client admin pages and customer data. Install only approved ones, and ask [IT contact] if you find one useful.

**5. Can I install a cracked or free version of paid software to save the client's budget?**
No. Pirated software is illegal, often carries malware, and puts the client's store at risk. Ask [IT contact] for a licensed option or an approved alternative.

**6. Can I share a login with a freelancer to save time?**
No. Ask for a named account with limited access and an end date. If the system only has one login, get approval from [IT contact] and the project lead first.

**7. Can I use ChatGPT or similar tools for work?**
Only tools on the [approved AI tools list], within their stated limits. A personal account on a public chatbot is not approved for work, and no client or company data goes into it.

**8. What may I never put into an AI tool?**
Credentials and keys, customer lists and personal data, children's or health or financial data, client confidential material, unreleased campaigns and contracts, unless a specific tool is approved for that purpose. When in doubt, do not.

**9. I already pasted something I should not have into a chatbot. What now?**
Tell [IT contact] and [privacy contact] straight away. Do not delete the chat before they advise. Prompt reports let them contain the damage and, if needed, tell the client in time.

**10. Do I have to check AI output if it looks right?**
Yes. AI can invent facts, quotes and links in a very confident tone. You are responsible for what goes to the client, so check facts and figures and have a second person review high-stakes work.

**11. Do we own the content that AI creates for us?**
It is unclear. The law on AI and copyright is still developing, and tool terms differ. Add real human work, avoid copying known styles or works, and ask [legal contact] about important assets. [confirm with a legal adviser]

**12. Do I have to tell the client I used AI?**
Follow the contract first. If it is silent, follow [the company's AI disclosure default]. Never claim human-only work when AI did a meaningful part, and answer honestly if asked.

**13. Can I use AI to make a customer review or testimonial photo?**
No. Fake reviews and endorsements mislead customers and can break advertising and consumer rules. Use real content from real customers with permission.

**14. Can an AI note-taker join our client call?**
Only with everyone's clear consent, the client's agreement, and an approved tool. If anyone is unsure, switch it off. Do not record HR, legal or sensitive discussions.

**15. May I use a code assistant on a client's store code?**
Only an approved one, and not with secrets or customer data in the code. Review and test everything it writes, and watch for licence issues. Never let it change a live store without review.

**16. What do you monitor, and can you read my chats?**
The company may monitor use of its systems for security, misuse and legal reasons, as set out in chapter 7. Reading message content needs a reason and approval, and is not done casually. Ask HR if you want to know more. [confirm with a legal adviser]

**17. How much personal use of my work laptop is okay?**
Brief and reasonable, such as checking a bill or a news article, if it does not affect work, break rules or touch client systems. Keep personal files and logins off the work device where you can.

**18. Can I use a USB drive to move a big file?**
Avoid it. Ask [IT contact] for an approved large-file route or an encrypted drive. Never plug in an unknown drive.

**19. May I work from a cafe or while travelling?**
Yes, if you protect the screen, avoid public wi-fi or use the approved VPN, and avoid client calls and restricted data. For work from another country, ask first.

**20. Can I use a client's tools for another client's work?**
No. Client systems and subscriptions are only for that client's authorised work.

**21. What happens if I report my own mistake?**
Honest, prompt reports are treated as a positive step, usually leading to a fix and a lesson, not punishment. Hiding a mistake is what causes serious consequences.

**22. I am a manager and my team wants to use a new AI tool for a deadline. What do I do?**
Do not allow it until it is approved. Raise it with [IT contact], who can fast-track urgent requests. Meanwhile, suggest approved tools or manual work.

**Knowledge check**

2. What is a better choice than sharing a login with a freelancer?
3. Why should you not install a free browser extension that wants to "read and change data on all websites"?

**Answer guide**

1. The company cannot control who else uses the device or what else is installed, and cannot wipe it if it is lost. Client contracts often forbid client data on personal devices, and personal cloud backups may copy the files.
2. Ask for a named account with limited access and an end date, for example a staff or collaborator invite from the client.

4. Shadow AI is using unapproved AI tools or personal AI accounts for work. If you have done it, tell the IT contact without fear, so the risks can be managed.
5. Any three of: customer lists or personal data, credentials and keys, client confidential information, unreleased campaigns, contracts, and children's, health or financial data.
6. AI can invent facts, quotes, figures and links, and you remain responsible for what you send, so facts and claims must be checked and high-stakes work reviewed by a second person.
7. A fake testimonial misleads customers, can break advertising and consumer rules, and damages the client's trust. Use real content from real customers with permission.
8. Tell everyone and get clear consent, check the client's rules, use only an approved tool with a work account, and decide where the recording will be stored and deleted.
9. Report it to the IT contact straight away so the key can be rotated, and do not wait or try to hide it.
10. Any two of: making sure the team knows the approved tools and client rules, requesting named least-access with end dates, not pressuring people to use unapproved tools, welcoming reports without blame, reviewing shared accounts, and acting on reports the same day.

# SEO Basics
This module gives project managers enough SEO knowledge to follow what SEO specialists are saying, ask them the right questions, and answer common client questions without overpromising. It is written for non-specialists and uses examples from online stores. It does not describe our internal process; see the main onboarding module for that.

## 1. Why project managers need SEO basics

You do not need to do SEO. You need to understand it well enough to do four things:

- Follow a conversation with an SEO specialist and notice when something is unclear or unrealistic
- Explain to a client, in plain words, what is being done and why
- Set honest expectations on timing and results
- Spot risks early, such as a plan with no baseline, or a link tactic that could cause a penalty

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 10 once. Keep chapter 11 and chapter 12 (the client questions) open when you prepare for client calls. Use the glossary in chapter 13 when a specialist uses a term you do not know. Asking "what does that mean?" in a meeting is always fine.

## 2. How search works

Search engines such as Google do three jobs, and each one can fail on its own.

1. **Crawling.** Automated programs (often called bots or crawlers) follow links to find pages.
2. **Indexing.** The search engine reads each page it found and stores it in a huge database called the index. A page that is not indexed cannot appear in search results.
3. **Ranking.** When someone searches, the engine picks pages from the index and orders them by how well they match the search and how useful and trustworthy they look.

**What this means in practice**

- If a page is not showing up at all, the problem is usually crawling or indexing (technical SEO).
- If a page is indexed but sits on page five, the problem is usually relevance, content quality or authority (on-page, content, off-page).
- Nobody outside Google knows the full list of ranking factors, and they change. Specialists work from tested practice and evidence, not from a published formula.

**Organic versus paid.** Organic results are the unpaid listings. Paid results are ads. SEO affects organic results only. Ads are a separate service with their own team and timeline.

## 3. The types of SEO

SEO work falls into groups. Most of our services map to one of them.

| Type | What it covers | Plain-language example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical SEO | Whether search engines can crawl, index and understand the site; speed and site health | Fixing broken pages, speeding up the store, making sure important pages are indexed |
| On-page SEO | The content and setup of individual pages | Writing a clear page title, improving headings and product descriptions |
| E-commerce SEO | On-page and technical work applied to collections, products and store structure | Optimising a collection page for "men's running shoes" and its product pages |
| Off-page SEO | Signals from outside the site, mainly links from other sites | Getting a relevant blog or publication to link to the store |
| Content SEO | Articles and guides that target what people search for | A buying guide that answers "how to choose a running shoe" |

A healthy plan usually needs all of them. Great content on a site that cannot be indexed will not rank. A fast, clean site with weak content and no links usually stays quiet.

## 4. Keywords and search intent

**Keywords** are the words and phrases people type into search. Keyword research finds which phrases people use, how often, and how hard it is to rank for them.

**Search intent** is what the person actually wants when they type those words. There are four common types:

| Intent | What the person wants | Example search | Best page type |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Informational | To learn something | how to clean white sneakers | Blog post or guide |
| Navigational | To reach a specific site | nike official store | Brand or home page |
| Commercial | To compare before buying | best running shoes for flat feet | Buying guide, comparison, collection page |
| Transactional | To buy now | buy asics gel kayano size 9 | Product or collection page |

**Why intent matters.** A page can match the words but fail on intent. A long blog post will struggle to rank for a transactional search, where people want a product page. Specialists check what already ranks for a phrase to see which kind of page Google prefers.

**Terms you will hear**

- **Search volume:** roughly how many times a month a phrase is searched
- **Keyword difficulty:** a tool's estimate of how hard it is to rank, based on the competition
- **Long-tail keyword:** a longer, more specific phrase (such as "waterproof trail running shoes women"). It has fewer searches but is easier to rank for and often closer to a purchase.
- **Keyword mapping:** deciding which page targets which keyword, so pages do not compete with each other

**Tip for PMs.** Ask the specialist to show the keyword list with volume and the page each keyword is mapped to. That list is the plan.

## 5. Technical SEO basics

Technical SEO makes sure search engines can reach, read and trust the site. A specialist runs a **site audit** with a crawling tool to find problems and then ranks them by importance.

**Common things an audit looks at**

- **Indexing:** which pages are in Google's index and which are blocked or missing
- **Sitemap:** a file listing the pages you want found. Shopify creates one automatically.
- **robots.txt:** a file that tells crawlers which areas to avoid
- **Redirects and errors:** a broken page (404 error) frustrates users and wastes crawling. When a page moves, a 301 redirect sends visitors and search engines to the new address.
- **Duplicate content:** several pages with the same or very similar content. A canonical tag tells search engines which version is the main one.
- **Page speed and Core Web Vitals:** Google's measures of how fast and stable a page feels. They are loading speed (LCP), responsiveness (INP) and layout stability (CLS). Google's guidance for a good result is LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP within 200 milliseconds and CLS of 0.1 or lower.
- **Mobile friendliness:** most shoppers browse on phones, and Google judges pages mainly by their mobile version
- **Structured data (schema):** extra code that describes a page, for example product price, availability and reviews, so search results can show richer listings
- **HTTPS:** a secure connection, now standard

**Shopify notes.** Shopify handles many basics, such as the sitemap and secure connections. Typical store problems are too many apps slowing the site, duplicate or thin collection pages, large images, and broken links after products are removed.

**Questions worth asking a specialist**

- Which issues are the most important, and why?
- Which fixes need a developer and which do not?
- How will we confirm each fix worked?

## 6. On-page and e-commerce SEO

On-page SEO is about making each page clear to both people and search engines.

| Element | What it is | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title tag | The headline shown in search results and the browser tab | One of the strongest signals of what a page is about, and it affects clicks |
| Meta description | The short summary shown under the title in search | Not a direct ranking factor, but a good one raises the click rate |
| Headings (H1, H2, H3) | The page's structure; the H1 is the main heading | Helps readers and search engines understand the topic |
| Page copy | The text on the page | Needs to answer what the searcher wants, in natural language |
| Images and alt text | Pictures and the short text description of each | Helps accessibility and image search |
| URL | The page address | Short and readable is better |
| Internal links | Links between pages of the same site | Spreads authority and helps people and crawlers find pages |

**E-commerce specifics**

- **Collection (category) pages** usually target the broader, higher-volume searches and often matter most for traffic.
- **Product pages** target specific, closer-to-purchase searches and need unique descriptions, not copied supplier text.
- **Product variants and filters** can create many near-duplicate URLs. Specialists manage this so search engines focus on the right pages.
- **Reviews, price, availability and shipping information** help both shoppers and search listings.

**Keyword stuffing** means repeating a phrase unnaturally. It does not help and can hurt. Good on-page work reads naturally.

## 7. Off-page SEO and backlinks

A **backlink** is a link from another website to yours. Search engines treat quality links as a vote of trust, so links from relevant, respected sites carry weight.

**What makes a link valuable**

- It comes from a relevant, real website with genuine readers
- It is placed naturally inside useful content
- The site is trusted in its field
- The words used for the link (anchor text) look natural and are not forced

**Common ways links are earned.** Publishing content worth citing, outreach to relevant sites, guest articles, digital PR and brand mentions, and listing in genuine business directories.

**What to watch for**

- Buying links or joining link-exchange schemes can break Google's rules and lead to a penalty.
- Many cheap, irrelevant links are worse than a few good ones.
- Links take time. A common mistake is expecting rankings to move within days of a link being placed.
- Specialists track **referring domains** (the number of different sites linking to you), because ten links from one site matter less than ten different relevant sites.

**Tip for PMs.** Ask for a log of every link built: the site, the page, the date and the anchor text. If a specialist cannot show one, that is a risk.

## 8. Content and blogs

Content helps SEO in two ways: it targets searches that product pages cannot, and it earns links and trust.

**How content fits an online store**

- **Informational posts** (guides, how-tos) reach people early in their buying journey
- **Comparison and buying guides** reach people close to a decision
- **Supporting content** links to relevant collection and product pages, which passes visitors and authority to them

**What good SEO content looks like**

- It answers the search better than what already ranks
- It is accurate, clearly written and useful to the reader
- It has a clear structure with headings
- It links to related pages on the site
- It is updated when information changes

**What to avoid.** Thin or copied content, articles written only for search engines, and publishing large amounts with no check on quality. Writing with AI tools is allowed, but unreviewed, generic content rarely performs and can cause quality problems.

**Timing.** New content usually takes weeks or months to build traffic. Judge a blog by a trend over several months, not by a single week.

## 9. Tools and reports

You do not need to master these, but you should know what each is for.

| Tool | What it does | What you can ask for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google Search Console | Free tool from Google showing how the site appears in search: impressions, clicks, average position, indexing problems | A screenshot or export of search performance and any indexing issues |
| Google Analytics (GA4) | Shows what visitors do on the site: sessions, engagement, conversions and revenue | Organic traffic and organic conversions over time |
| Crawling tools (for example Screaming Frog) | Scan the site like a search engine and list technical problems | The audit export and the prioritised fix list |
| SEO suites (for example Ahrefs, Semrush) | Keyword research, rank tracking, competitor and backlink data | Keyword lists, ranking reports, backlink reports |
| PageSpeed Insights | Google's speed and Core Web Vitals check | Scores before and after speed work |

**Reading a basic SEO report.** A good report shows: the starting point (baseline), what changed, the results against the baseline over time, the work done this period, and the plan for the next period. If a report has no baseline, progress cannot be shown.

**Data differences.** Search Console, GA4 and third-party tools often show different numbers because they measure in different ways. This is normal. Always compare the same tool over time instead of mixing sources.

## 10. Metrics: what the numbers mean

| Metric | Meaning | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impressions | How many times a page appeared in search results | Search Console |
| Clicks | How many times people clicked through from search | Search Console |
| Click-through rate (CTR) | Clicks divided by impressions | Search Console |
| Average position | The average ranking position across searches (1 is the top) | Search Console |
| Organic sessions | Visits that came from unpaid search | GA4 |
| Conversions | Desired actions, such as purchases or sign-ups | GA4 |
| Organic revenue | Sales credited to organic search | GA4 or the store's analytics |
| Referring domains | Number of different sites linking to the site | SEO tools |
| Indexed pages | Number of pages in Google's index | Search Console |

**How to read them together**

- **Impressions rising, clicks flat:** the site is being seen more but not clicked. Titles and descriptions may need work.
- **Clicks rising, conversions flat:** the traffic arrives but does not buy. The problem may be the page, the offer or the search intent match, not SEO itself.
- **Rankings improving, traffic flat:** the improved keywords may have low search volume.
- **Average position can mislead.** It averages many searches. A new page ranking for many obscure phrases can lower it even while traffic grows.

**Leading and lagging signals.** Early signals (more indexed pages, more impressions, better rankings for target pages) show up first. Traffic comes next, and sales usually come last. When a client asks "where are the sales?", point to the earlier signals and explain that they come first.

## 11. Talking to clients: expectations and red flags

**What we can say with confidence**

- SEO is a long-term channel. Results build over months, not days.
- We can control the work: audits, fixes, content, links and reports.
- We cannot control Google's decisions, competitors' actions or algorithm updates.
- Small stores and new sites usually take longer than established ones.

**What we must never say**

- A guaranteed ranking, such as "you will be number one" or "on page one in three months". Google itself warns against anyone who guarantees rankings.
- A guaranteed amount of traffic or revenue
- That a tactic is "secret" or "a loophole"
- A promise about a Google update's effects

**Better ways to say it**

- Instead of "You will rank in three months", say "We will fix the main issues and publish the planned pages in the first months, and we expect to see early signs in impressions and rankings before traffic and sales."
- Instead of "We need more time", say "Here are the three early signals we are tracking and what we have seen so far."

**Red flags in SEO plans, internally or from outside vendors**

- No baseline and no reporting plan
- Large numbers of links promised quickly or for a very low price
- Keyword lists with no mapping to pages
- Work described only in vague terms
- Copying competitors' content or using automated pages
- Reports that show activity only and no results

**When you are unsure.** It is better to say "I will check with the specialist and come back to you by tomorrow" than to guess.

## 12. Client questions and sample answers

These are answers a project manager can give, in plain language. Adapt the wording to the client, and check with the specialist for anything specific to the client's site.

**1. What is SEO, in simple words?**
SEO is the work of making your website easier for search engines to find, understand and trust, so that it shows up when people search for what you sell. It includes fixing technical problems, improving your pages, creating helpful content and earning links from other sites.

**2. How long before we see results?**
Early signs, such as more pages indexed and more search impressions, can appear within the first few weeks. Rankings and traffic usually build over several months. Sales come last. The exact timing depends on how competitive your market is, how old your site is, and its current condition.

**3. Can you guarantee page one or the number one spot?**
No, and be careful of anyone who does. Rankings depend on factors we do not control. What we guarantee is the work: the audit, the fixes, the content and the reporting. We will show you the results against your starting point every month.

**4. Why are we not ranking yet?**
There are usually a few reasons: the pages are not yet indexed or optimised, competitors have stronger sites and more links, or the work has not had time to take effect. We will check where you stand for your target keywords and show you what is being done about each gap.

**5. Our traffic dropped. What happened?**
Traffic can drop for many reasons, including seasonality, a Google update, a technical problem, lost rankings or tracking issues. The first step is to confirm the drop is real in more than one source, then compare the dates with any site changes or updates. I will ask the specialist to investigate and report back with the cause and a plan.

**6. What is the difference between SEO and ads?**
Ads are paid and stop when you stop paying. SEO is unpaid search listings and builds over time, so the benefit tends to last. Ads give faster visibility, and SEO usually gives better long-term value. Many stores use both.

**7. What are keywords and why do they matter?**
Keywords are the words people type into search. We choose the ones that match what you sell and what customers look for, and then make sure the right pages target them. Choosing the wrong keywords means attracting visitors who will not buy.

**8. What is a backlink and why do you build them?**
A backlink is a link from another website to yours. Search engines see good links as a sign of trust. We aim for links from relevant, real sites, because a few quality links are worth much more than many poor ones. Links also take time to have an effect.

**9. Why does the blog matter for a store?**
Blog posts reach people who are researching before they buy, and they earn links and trust that help your whole site. They also let us link to your products and collections. They work best when they are useful and part of a plan, and they usually take a few months to build traffic.

**10. Why is my site speed important?**
A slow site frustrates visitors, so fewer buy, and Google measures page experience. Speed is rarely the only thing that decides ranking, but poor speed can hold back both rankings and sales. Large images and too many apps are the usual causes in online stores.

**11. Do you do social media or Google Ads as part of SEO?**
No, those are separate services. Social media and ads can bring visitors and awareness, and they work well alongside SEO. They do not directly improve organic rankings.

**12. Can we do SEO ourselves or in-house?**
Yes, many businesses do the basics themselves. The value of a specialist team is the experience, the tools, the time and the speed. We can also work alongside your own team. The right choice depends on your time and budget.

**13. Why does the SEO report show different numbers from Google Analytics or my own tracking?**
Each tool counts differently. Search Console reports how you appear in search, GA4 reports what visitors do on your site, and third-party tools use their own estimates. The numbers rarely match exactly. What matters is the trend from the same tool over time.

**14. What is a Google algorithm update?**
Google regularly changes how it ranks pages. Large updates can move rankings up or down across many sites. Sites that are useful, trustworthy and well maintained generally hold up best. If an update affects your site, we check the cause and adjust the plan.

**15. Is it true that Google penalises some SEO work?**
Yes, if the work breaks Google's rules, such as buying links, hiding text or copying content at scale. We follow safe, long-term practices so that your results do not depend on risky tactics.

**16. How many keywords can you rank for?**
It depends on your site, your market and the work involved. We focus on the keywords that matter most to your business first, and we expand as your site grows. The number of keywords matters less than their relevance and the traffic and sales they bring.

**17. Why do you need access to Search Console, Analytics and our store?**
We need real data to find problems and measure progress. Search Console shows how Google sees your site, and Analytics shows how visitors behave. We use access only for the agreed work, and we ask you to add us as a user so you keep control of your accounts.

**18. Can you move our site to Shopify or change our design without losing rankings?**
It can be done safely with proper planning. The main risk is losing rankings through broken or missing redirects and changed page addresses. We plan the redirects, check them, and monitor results after the change. Some short-term movement can still happen.

**19. Do reviews and product descriptions affect SEO?**
Yes, indirectly. Unique, helpful product descriptions give search engines and shoppers more to work with than copied supplier text. Genuine reviews add fresh content and build trust, and they can appear in search listings in some cases.

**20. Why can't you just copy what a competitor ranks for?**
Competitor research is useful for ideas, but copying content does not work well. Search engines look for original, helpful pages, and a copy cannot beat the page it was copied from. We use competitor insight to find gaps and to make your pages better, not the same.

**21. What do you need from us to get the best results?**
Quick access and approvals, accurate product information, help with brand and product knowledge, and patience. Delays on the client side are one of the most common reasons results take longer.

**22. How do I know the work is actually being done?**
We share a plan at the start, a regular progress update, and a monthly report showing the work completed and the results against your baseline. You can always ask us to explain any item in more detail.

## 13. Glossary

**Glossary**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Algorithm | The rules a search engine uses to decide how to rank pages |
| Alt text | A short description of an image, used by screen readers and image search |
| Anchor text | The clickable words of a link |
| Backlink | A link from another website to yours |
| Baseline | The starting numbers recorded before work begins |
| Canonical tag | A tag telling search engines which version of similar pages is the main one |
| Click-through rate (CTR) | Clicks divided by impressions |
| Core Web Vitals | Google's measures of page loading, responsiveness and stability |
| Crawling | Search engine bots finding pages by following links |
| Crawl budget | How much attention a search engine gives to crawling a site |
| Domain authority | A third-party score estimating a site's strength; it is not a Google metric |
| Duplicate content | The same or very similar content on more than one page |
| Index | The search engine's database of pages |
| Indexing | Adding a page to the search engine's index |
| Internal link | A link from one page to another page on the same site |
| Keyword | A word or phrase people type into search |
| Keyword difficulty | An estimate of how hard it is to rank for a keyword |
| Long-tail keyword | A longer, more specific search phrase |
| Meta description | The summary shown under a result's title in search |
| Nofollow | A link attribute telling search engines not to pass trust through that link |
| Organic traffic | Visitors who arrive from unpaid search results |
| Referring domain | A unique website that links to yours |
| Redirect (301) | A permanent forward from an old address to a new one |
| Rich results | Search listings enhanced with extra details such as price or reviews |
| Schema (structured data) | Code that describes a page's content to search engines |
| Search intent | What the searcher actually wants |
| SERP | Search engine results page |
| Sitemap | A file listing the pages you want search engines to find |
| Title tag | The headline of a page shown in search results |

**Knowledge check**

1. What are the three jobs a search engine does, and which one has failed if a page does not appear at all?
2. Name the four types of search intent and give a search example of each.
3. What is the difference between on-page and off-page SEO?
4. A client asks if we can guarantee page one in three months. How do you respond?
5. Impressions are rising, but clicks are flat. What might be the cause?
6. Why do Search Console and Google Analytics show different numbers?
7. What are Core Web Vitals, and why do they matter to an online store?
8. What should you ask a specialist who says they have built 100 links this month?
9. Why does a report without a baseline cause problems?
10. A client says traffic dropped this month. What are your first two steps?

**Answer guide**

1. Crawling, indexing and ranking. If a page does not appear at all, the problem is usually crawling or indexing.
2. Informational (how to clean white sneakers), navigational (nike official store), commercial (best running shoes for flat feet) and transactional (buy asics gel kayano size 9).
3. On-page SEO is the content and setup of your own pages. Off-page SEO is signals from outside the site, mainly links from other websites.
4. No honest guarantee is possible. Explain what we control (the work and the reporting), what we do not control, and what early signs to expect.
5. The site is being seen more but the titles and descriptions may not attract clicks, or the impressions may be coming from low-position results.
6. They measure different things in different ways, so the numbers rarely match. Compare trends within the same tool.
7. They are Google's measures of loading speed, responsiveness and layout stability. A slow or unstable store frustrates shoppers and can hold back rankings and sales.
8. Ask for the link log (sites, pages, dates, anchor text), how relevance and quality were checked, and whether any links were paid for or part of a scheme.
9. Without a starting point, there is no way to show what changed or whether the work is working.
10. Confirm the drop is real in more than one source, then compare the dates against site changes, seasonality and Google updates, and ask the specialist to investigate.

# Speaking Up: Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling

This module is for everyone in the organisation: developers, designers, marketers, writers, project managers, finance, admin, sales, leaders, interns and freelancers. It explains how to raise a concern or a complaint at work, which route to use for which kind of problem, what happens after you speak up, how you are protected, and what is expected of managers who hear a concern. It is written for non-specialists in plain language and uses examples from a digital agency and its clients. It is general information and not legal advice, and it does not replace the organisation's own written policies on whistleblowing and grievances.

## 1. Why everyone needs to know how to speak up

Most problems at work are small when they are first noticed. A wrong number in a client report, a manager who keeps shifting deadlines, a rule that is quietly ignored, a colleague who makes people uncomfortable. Left alone, small problems grow. The people closest to the work usually see them first, which is why a company depends on those people speaking up.

Speaking up can feel risky. People worry about being seen as difficult, about making things worse, or about not being believed. This module exists so that you know the routes, know what will happen, and know that you are protected when you act honestly.

**This module is for all of us because**

- Anyone can see something that is wrong, or be affected by something unfair
- Anyone can be the person a worried colleague confides in
- Clients trust an agency with their stores, customer data, ad budgets and brand. That trust only survives if problems are raised early
- A fair process protects the person raising a concern and also the person it is about

**What this module will help you do**

- Choose the right route for your problem, so it reaches the right people quickly
- Raise a concern clearly, with the facts that help someone act on it
- Understand what happens next, and what you can expect to hear back
- Know what confidentiality, anonymity and protection from retaliation really mean, including their limits
- Use the formal grievance procedure if an informal conversation does not solve a personal workplace problem
- Handle it well if a colleague raises a concern with you, especially if you manage people

**The three rules to remember**

1. **Raise it early and raise it honestly.** You do not need proof or certainty. You need a genuine belief that something is wrong.
2. **Do not investigate yourself.** Report what you saw and heard. Trained people look into it fairly.
3. **Retaliation is never acceptable.** Nobody may punish you for raising a concern in good faith, and you must never punish anyone else for doing so.

**The culture we want.** We want a workplace where a junior designer can tell a director that a claim in an ad is not accurate, where a developer can say "I think we have exposed customer data" without fear, and where a person with a personal complaint is listened to rather than managed out. Speaking up is part of doing a good job, not a betrayal of the team.

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 6 once, as everyone needs them. Chapters 7 to 10 explain confidentiality, protection and fairness, and are worth reading before you ever need them. Chapters 11 and 12 cover the formal grievance procedure and what to do if you are unhappy with an outcome. Managers must read chapter 13 carefully. Chapters 14 and 15 cover going outside the company and the legal background in India. Use the questions in chapter 16 and the glossary in chapter 17 for quick reference. If you are unsure which route to use, ask [the ethics contact]. Asking is always allowed.

## 2. Which route for which problem

Two words cause most of the confusion, so start here.

- A **grievance** is a personal complaint about your own working situation: how you are treated, paid, scheduled or managed.
- **Whistleblowing** (also called a protected disclosure or raising a concern) is reporting wrongdoing that affects the company, clients, colleagues or the public, whether or not it affects you personally.

Some problems have their own dedicated route, and those routes take priority because they have special rules and trained people.

| Type of problem | Examples | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal workplace grievance | Pay or deductions, workload, shift or deadline pressure, management style, an unfair rating, a dispute with a colleague, a change to your role | Grievance route: your manager first, then [HR contact], then the formal procedure (chapter 11) |
| Whistleblowing about wrongdoing | Fraud, false invoices, bribery or kickbacks, misuse of customer or personal data, safety risks, misreporting results to a client, breaches of law or contract, hiding errors | A whistleblowing channel (chapter 4): [ethics contact], a senior leader, or the [anonymous channel] |
| Sexual harassment | Unwelcome sexual remarks, touching, messages, pressure, stalking, in person or online | The Internal Committee, as set out in POSH Awareness for Everyone: [IC contact] |
| Security incident | Phishing clicked, lost laptop, leaked password, suspicious access to a client store | The security contact, as set out in Cyber Security Basics for Everyone: [security contact], straight away |
| Discrimination | Being treated worse because of gender, caste, religion, region, disability, age, orientation or similar | [HR contact] or [ethics contact]. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone |
| Bullying and harassment (not sexual) | Repeated humiliation, shouting, exclusion, threats, spreading rumours | [HR contact] or [ethics contact], or the formal grievance procedure |

**When a problem fits more than one route.** Many real problems do. A manager who gives one person worse work because of their religion is both a grievance and a discrimination concern. A manager who asks you to backdate a client invoice is both a pressure issue and possible fraud. Do not worry about picking perfectly. Pick the nearest route and say what you think the other angle is. [HR contact] and [ethics contact] pass matters to each other, and you will be told if your concern has been moved.

**A quick test.**

1. Is it mainly about how *I* am treated at work? Probably a grievance.
2. Is it about something that would be wrong even if it never touched me? Probably whistleblowing.
3. Is it sexual in nature? Go to the Internal Committee.
4. Is data or a system at risk right now? Call the security contact first, then talk about the rest later.

**Urgent situations.** If someone is in physical danger, call the emergency services first ([112] is the national emergency number in India, confirm for your location) and then tell [HR contact] or your manager.

### Examples of sorting

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You are asked to work every weekend for two months with no time off | This is a workload grievance. Talk to your manager, then [HR contact] |
| A colleague tells you they inflate the "conversion" figures in a client report so the campaign looks better | Whistleblowing: misreporting to a client. Use a whistleblowing channel |
| A senior makes comments on a teammate's body in the team chat | This may be sexual harassment. Direct to the Internal Committee, or encourage the teammate to |

## 3. What counts as a good-faith concern

You only need to be **honest and reasonable**. You do not need to be right.

**A good-faith concern means**

- You believe the information you are giving is true, or at least that it is worth checking
- You are raising it to get something put right, not to hurt someone or gain an advantage
- You are not making it up, exaggerating it, or hiding facts that would change the picture

**You are still protected if**

- You turn out to be mistaken
- There is an innocent explanation you did not know about
- The matter cannot be proved
- You were wrong about which policy or law applies

**Suspicion versus proof.** Report what you saw, heard or noticed. Say what you do not know. Say "I think", "I noticed" or "it looks like" if that is the truth. Do not claim certainty you do not have. Nobody expects you to have the evidence of a detective. That is the job of the people who handle the report.

**What is worth raising**

- Something that is illegal, unethical or against company policy
- Something that puts people, data, money or reputation at risk
- Something that looks like it is being hidden or covered up
- Unfair treatment that you or someone else is experiencing

**What may be better raised another way**

- A disagreement about strategy, design or creative direction. Raise it in the project discussion or with your lead
- A preference about how the team works. Raise it in a retrospective or with your manager
- A request for a pay review. Use the process in the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone

If you are unsure, raise it anyway and say you are unsure. The people receiving it will tell you if another route fits better.

## 4. Channels and how to choose

You have several ways to raise a concern. No route is "the correct one" for every case. Choose the one where you feel safest and where the concern will reach someone able to act.

| Channel | Best for | Things to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your direct manager | Everyday grievances and small concerns | Quickest. May not suit you if the manager is part of the problem |
| [HR contact] | Personal grievances, bullying, discrimination, pay and conditions | Trained in fair process. Can explain options |
| A senior leader of your choice, such as [department head] | When your manager is involved or you want a leader to hear it | Leaders are obliged to pass formal concerns on to the right place |
| [Ethics contact or ethics committee] | Whistleblowing, serious or sensitive matters | Independent of day to day line management |
| [Anonymous channel, for example an external hotline or web form] | When you fear being identified, or want to ask a question first | Hard to follow up. See chapter 7 on limits of anonymity |
| [Audit committee chair or director, for matters about senior leaders] | When the concern is about a senior leader or the ethics contact | See chapter 12 |

**How to choose**

1. **Ask yourself who is involved.** Never take a concern to someone it is about, or someone who reports closely to them.
2. **Think about how serious it is.** The more serious or sensitive, the more it belongs with [ethics contact] rather than a line manager.
3. **Think about what you need.** If you want a personal problem fixed, go to your manager or [HR contact]. If you want wrongdoing investigated, use a whistleblowing channel.
4. **Think about time.** If harm could happen soon, use the fastest route that reaches someone with authority.
5. **If you are torn, ask for advice.** You can contact [ethics contact] to ask "what route should I use?" without making a formal report.

**Written or spoken.** You may raise a concern in person, by phone, by email, through a form or by letter. For serious matters, a written note helps, even if it is short, because it keeps the facts straight. If you speak first, you can say "I would like this recorded as a formal concern" and the person must then follow the process.

**Bringing someone with you.** For grievance meetings and hearings you may bring a colleague as a companion [confirm against policy]. For whistleblowing conversations, you can ask whether a support person may attend.

## 5. How to make a report

A good report is clear, factual and short enough to act on. You can make a poor first report and add to it later. Do not wait until you have everything.

**What to include**

- **What happened.** Describe the facts in order, without opinion if you can
- **When and where.** Dates, times, project or client name, the channel or system involved
- **Who.** Names or roles of those involved, and of anyone who saw or knows
- **How you know.** Did you see it, hear it, find it in a document, or were you told?
- **Why you are concerned.** The rule, law, contract or value you think is affected
- **What you have already done.** For example, whether you spoke to the person or to a manager
- **What you would like to happen.** Optional, but helpful

**Evidence**

- Keep original messages, emails, screenshots or documents that you **already have lawful access to** as part of your job
- Note down dates and details soon after an event, while they are fresh
- Do not delete or change anything
- Say where further evidence may be found, such as a shared drive folder or an ad account report

**Do not investigate yourself**

- Do not search other people's accounts, files or devices
- Do not log in to systems you are not authorised to use
- Do not copy large quantities of confidential or client data to personal devices or private email. This can itself breach data rules (see Data Privacy and Confidentiality)
- Do not secretly record people, or confront the person you suspect
- Do not discuss it with colleagues to "check your facts". This can spoil an investigation and harm reputations

**Before you submit, check**

- [ ] I have written down what I saw or know, in order
- [ ] I have separated facts from my opinion
- [ ] I have included dates, names and places
- [ ] I have kept (not changed or deleted) the evidence I already have
- [ ] I have not accessed anything I should not
- [ ] I have chosen a channel that does not involve the person I am worried about
- [ ] I have said whether I want my identity kept confidential

### A model short report

> Subject: Concern about figures in the [Client name] monthly report
>
> On [date] I noticed that the revenue figure in the report sent to the client (page 3) does not match the figure in the store's own analytics export (saved in [shared drive folder]). The report is higher by about [amount]. I do not know whether this was a mistake or intentional. I was told by [name] that the higher figure was "what the client wants to see". I have not spoken to the client. I would like this checked, and I would prefer that my name is kept confidential.

Notice what the report does: it states facts, says what is unknown, and does not accuse.

## 6. What happens next

After you raise a concern, a clear process should begin. The exact times below are proposed defaults.

| Stage | What happens | Typical time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Acknowledgement | You receive a written confirmation that your concern was received, and a named contact | Within [3 working days] |
| 2. Initial assessment | The receiver decides whether the concern falls under this route, whether it needs urgent action, and whether it can be handled informally | Within [7 working days] |
| 3. Investigation | A fair and impartial person or panel gathers facts, speaks to people and reviews records | Usually completed within [30 working days], longer for complex cases, with updates |
| 4. Outcome | A decision is reached on what was found and what action, if any, is needed | After the investigation |
| 5. Feedback to you | You are told that the matter has been dealt with and, where possible, the general outcome | Within [5 working days] of the outcome |
| 6. Closing | The case is closed and actions are followed up | After actions are done |
| 7. Record keeping | A confidential record is kept, with restricted access | For [the period set in the retention policy] |

**Fair and impartial.** The investigator must not be involved in the matter, must not report to the person concerned, and must keep an open mind. Where there is a conflict of interest, a different investigator is appointed, from inside or outside the company.

**What you can expect**

- To be treated with respect and not blamed for raising a concern
- To be told the next step and who your contact is
- To receive updates, even when the update is "still in progress"
- To be asked questions, perhaps more than once. This is normal and does not mean you are doubted

**What you may not be told**

- Details of disciplinary action against another person. This is private to them
- Confidential information about other people
- Findings that could reveal identities or harm the process

You may be told "it has been dealt with" without detail. That does not mean nothing happened.

**Possible outcomes**

- No wrongdoing found, or not enough evidence
- A process or control is changed to stop a repeat
- Training, coaching or a formal warning
- Disciplinary action under the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone
- A report to a regulator, the police or the client, where required
- Support or adjustments for people affected

**While it is going on.** Keep doing your job. Keep any evidence safe. Do not discuss it with colleagues. Tell your contact straight away if you feel you are being treated badly because of your report.

## 7. Confidentiality and anonymity

These two ideas are different, and both have limits.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Confidential | The receiver knows who you are but keeps it restricted to the people who need to know in order to handle the matter |
| Anonymous | Nobody is told who you are, including the receiver |

**Confidentiality, what we do**

- Share your identity only with those who must know to investigate
- Store the report and notes securely, with restricted access
- Ask everyone involved to keep the matter confidential
- Treat leaking a reporter's identity as a serious breach of conduct

**Limits of confidentiality**

- Your identity may have to be shared if the law requires it, for example with the police, a court or a regulator
- It may be unavoidable in a fair process: the person a concern is about may need enough detail to respond, and context can make the source obvious
- Small teams make guessing easy. If only two people had access to a file, others may work out who raised the concern
- Confidentiality depends on others too. Do not tell colleagues about your report, because this is the most common way identities leak

**Anonymous reports, what we do**

- Accept them through [anonymous channel] and take them seriously
- Assess them on the facts given
- Allow a two way exchange through the channel's reference number, if the channel supports it

**Limits of anonymity**

- Without more information, an anonymous report can be hard or impossible to investigate
- We cannot give you feedback if we cannot contact you
- Protection from retaliation is harder to apply if we do not know who you are
- Writing style, timing, details only you could know and the system you used can reveal you without anyone trying
- Anonymous reports can be abused. They are still assessed fairly, but the person concerned has the same fairness rights

**Tips if you choose to be anonymous**

- Give as much detail as you safely can: dates, places, documents
- Come back to the channel and check for questions
- Do not use a company laptop or account that identifies you, if the channel advises against it
- Consider a confidential report instead if you can: it is often more effective and gives you full protection and feedback

**Your own discretion.** Whatever route you choose, do not post your concern on social media, in a client group chat or in a general team channel. That can expose people unfairly and weaken both your protection and the case.

## 8. Protection from retaliation

**Retaliation means** any harm, threat or unfair treatment because someone raised a concern in good faith, took part in an investigation, or helped someone else to do so.

**What retaliation can look like**

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Formal action | Dismissal, demotion, a poor rating, a pay cut or a lost bonus, being taken off a project or promotion list, an unexpected transfer |
| Informal pressure | Being frozen out of meetings, given only dull work, left off the team chat, micromanaged, or given impossible deadlines |
| Social | Mocking, rumours, "jokes" about being a snitch, exclusion from lunches or events |
| Threats | Hints that your contract may not be renewed, or that "people like you" struggle here |
| Targeting others | Punishing a colleague because they supported you or gave evidence |
| Unmasking | Trying to find out who raised the concern, or revealing it |

**What to do if you think you are being retaliated against**

1. Write down what happened, with dates, names and any evidence
2. Report it to [HR contact] or [ethics contact] as a new concern. Retaliation is treated seriously in its own right, even if the original concern turns out to have no basis
3. Say what has changed since you raised the concern, so the link is clear
4. Ask for interim protection if needed, such as a change of reporting line or working arrangement, without any loss of pay

**What the company will do**

- Take reports of retaliation seriously and investigate them separately
- Treat retaliation as a disciplinary offence, up to and including dismissal
- Check in on you after the case closes, at [30 days and 90 days]
- Take steps to protect you while a matter is being looked at

**What you must do**

- Never retaliate against anyone you suspect of raising a concern
- Never try to find out who raised it
- Tell your manager or [HR contact] if you hear colleagues talking about it unkindly

**Who is protected.** Employees, trainees, interns, contractors and freelancers working with us [confirm against policy]. The protection also covers people who support or give evidence for someone else.

**What protection does not mean.** Raising a concern does not shield you from fair action on unrelated matters, such as genuine poor performance or a policy breach of your own. Managers must be able to show that any action taken is unrelated, fair and consistent. If you are worried that ordinary management is being used as a cover, say so.

## 9. Good faith versus malicious reports

We want people to speak up. We also must be fair to those who are named.

| | Good faith | Malicious or knowingly false |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motive | To put something right | To harm someone, settle a score, or gain an advantage |
| Facts | Honest, even if incomplete or mistaken | Invented, distorted, or key facts hidden |
| Result if not proven | No action against the reporter | May lead to disciplinary action |
| Protection | Full | None for the false part of the report |

**Key points**

- A report that is **mistaken** or **cannot be proved** is not a false report
- Disciplinary action is only for reports that are shown to be **deliberately false or malicious**. That requires evidence of the bad faith and a fair process, never a guess
- The burden is on the company to show bad faith, not on the reporter to prove they were innocent
- Fear of a false accusation must never become a reason to discourage honest reports
- Using the process to harass, as a weapon in a personal feud, is also a misuse

**A worked example.** An account manager reports that a freelancer has been paid twice for the same invoice. Finance checks and finds it was a refund that had been reversed, with a clear ledger trail. No wrongdoing. The account manager acted in good faith, is thanked, and is told the matter is closed. Compare that with a colleague who, after losing a promotion contest, invents a story that the winner copied client data. The invention is shown by the records and messages. The second colleague may face action, after a fair hearing of their own.

**If you have a grudge.** Use the grievance route for personal issues. Do not dress up a personal dispute as a whistleblowing matter.

## 10. The rights of the person a concern is about

Being the subject of a concern is stressful, and many concerns turn out to be unfounded or partly so. Everyone is entitled to a fair process, whatever their seniority.

**The person concerned has the right to**

- **Be told fairly.** They are told that a concern has been raised, what it broadly is, and what is expected of them, at a suitable point. In some cases (for example, where evidence could be destroyed) this may be delayed
- **Be heard.** They have a proper chance to give their side and to name people or documents that support them
- **A presumption of fairness.** No assumption of guilt. The investigation looks for facts, not for a particular result
- **Confidentiality.** Their name is shared only with those who need it
- **An impartial investigator** who has no stake in the outcome
- **To be accompanied** at any formal meeting by a colleague [confirm against policy]
- **To know the outcome** that affects them, and to appeal any disciplinary decision under the HR Policies Handbook for Everyone
- **Support.** Access to [the employee assistance or counselling service] if available

**If you are the subject of a concern**

1. Stay calm and cooperate. Being named is not a finding
2. Do not try to find out who raised it, and do not contact them about it
3. Do not delete, change or hide anything. That makes things much worse
4. Keep doing your work as normal, unless you are told otherwise
5. Keep it confidential, and do not discuss it with colleagues
6. Ask your contact for help if you need it

**Interim steps are not punishment.** Someone may be asked to step back from a project, work from a different place or have access paused while a matter is checked. These are neutral protective steps, taken fairly and for as short a time as possible, with no loss of pay.

**For the investigator**

- Keep records of what was asked and answered
- Test the evidence both ways
- Never reveal the reporter's identity casually
- Write findings in a balanced way

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A team lead hears that a designer has been named in a concern and wants to know "who said it" | Do not ask. Explain that identities are protected, and that the designer will be treated fairly |
| A developer is named for using a client store's admin login after leaving the project | Access is paused neutrally while it is checked. The developer is told what is alleged and invited to respond |
| An account manager is named and then ignored by the team before any finding | Raise it with [HR contact]. Treating someone as guilty beforehand is unfair, and the team should be reminded of confidentiality |

## 11. The formal grievance procedure

This chapter covers personal workplace grievances. Whistleblowing follows the route in chapter 6. The stages and time limits below are proposed defaults.

**Principle.** Most grievances are best solved by a quick, honest conversation. Move to the formal stages only when that does not work, or when the matter is too serious or personal to raise directly.

| Stage | What you do | What the company does | Time limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Informal conversation | Talk to your manager, or [HR contact] if your manager is the issue. Say what is wrong and what would help | Listens, tries to resolve, notes any agreement | Try to resolve within [10 working days] |
| 2. Written grievance | If unresolved, write to [HR contact] describing the grievance, steps taken and the outcome sought | Acknowledges in [3 working days]. A manager not involved in the matter is appointed to look at it | Acknowledgement in [3 working days] |
| 3. Hearing | You are invited to a meeting to explain. You may bring a companion [confirm against policy]. Notes are taken | Reasonable notice is given, enquiries are made, and others may be asked for their account | Hearing within [10 working days] of the written grievance |
| 4. Decision | You receive a written decision with reasons and any action | A decision is made by someone impartial, who explains what will change or why not | Within [5 working days] of the hearing |
| 5. Appeal | If you are unhappy, you may appeal in writing, stating why | A more senior person, not involved earlier, reviews. Their decision is final within the company | Appeal within [5 working days] of the decision, result within [10 working days] |

**Tips for a good written grievance**

- Keep to facts and dates
- Say what you have already tried
- Say what outcome would resolve it
- Be specific about the effect on you
- Do not copy in the whole team

**Mediation.** For disputes between colleagues, a neutral facilitator (such as [HR contact] or an external mediator) can help both sides talk. It is voluntary and often resolves things faster than a formal decision.

**Where the grievance is about pay or leave.** The HR Policies Handbook for Everyone sets out the rules on pay and leave. The grievance route is for when you believe those rules have not been applied fairly or correctly. Raise errors in a payslip with [payroll contact] first, as many are simple corrections.

**If you are on probation, a trainee, an intern or a freelancer.** You can raise a grievance too. The route may be shortened or adapted. Ask [HR contact] how it applies to you.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A copywriter is given a third rush client project in a row while a peer with a lighter load is not | Talk to the manager about workload fairness first. If unresolved, write a short grievance to [HR contact] |
| A designer's overtime payment is missing from the payslip | Raise with [payroll contact]. If not fixed within [10 working days], use the written grievance stage |
| Two colleagues cannot agree on who owns a client relationship and it has become personal | Ask [HR contact] about mediation before a formal hearing |
| An intern is told "this is how it is here" when asking about unpaid extra hours | The intern can go straight to [HR contact]. Do not stay silent because of seniority |

## 12. If you are unhappy with the outcome or the concern is about senior leaders

### If you are unhappy with the outcome

You may not agree with what was decided. That does not make you wrong, and you have options.

1. **Ask for an explanation.** Ask your contact to explain how the decision was reached, as far as confidentiality allows
2. **Check the process.** Was your concern assessed, investigated fairly and answered within the stated times?
3. **Use the appeal step.** For grievances, the appeal in chapter 11 is available. For whistleblowing, ask [ethics contact] for a review by someone who was not involved, if you believe the process was unfair or new evidence exists
4. **Add new information.** If you have new facts, say so. New evidence can reopen a matter
5. **Escalate.** Contact [the audit committee chair or a director] if you believe the process failed

An appeal is about **process and evidence**, not simply about getting the result you wanted. A fair process can still lead to an outcome that disappoints you.

### If the concern is about senior leaders

A concern about a director, founder or senior leader should not go to a person who reports to them.

| If the concern is about | Raise it with |
| --- | --- |
| Your manager | Their manager, [HR contact] or [ethics contact] |
| A department head | [Ethics contact] or [a director not in that department] |
| [HR contact] or [ethics contact] | [The audit committee chair or an independent director] |
| A founder or managing director | [The audit committee chair, an independent director or the board] |
| Everyone you could go to | [Anonymous channel], which should route to the board or an external party |

**Rules for leaders.** Seniority gives no protection from a fair investigation and no right to learn who raised a concern. A leader who is the subject of a concern steps back from decisions about the case, including who investigates it.

**When there is no independent person.** In a small company the owners may be the only senior people. In that case, ask [ethics contact] about using an external adviser or investigator, and see chapter 14 on outside routes.

## 13. A manager's duties when someone raises a concern

If you manage people, you are often the first person to hear a concern. How you respond shapes whether people ever speak up again.

**Do**

- **Listen** calmly and fully. Thank the person. Do not interrupt, argue or defend
- **Take it seriously**, even if it seems small or you disagree
- **Ask what they need**, and what they would like to happen
- **Be honest about confidentiality.** Say clearly: "I will keep this as private as I can, but I cannot promise secrecy. I may have to pass it on to [ethics contact] or [HR contact]"
- **Write a short note** of what you heard, the date and who was involved
- **Pass it on within [1 working day]** for whistleblowing, harassment, discrimination or safety matters, and within [3 working days] for other grievances you cannot resolve yourself
- **Tell the person what you did** and who will contact them
- **Protect them** from reaction by others, and watch for signs of retaliation

**Do not**

- Promise secrecy you cannot keep
- Investigate, interview witnesses or collect evidence yourself
- Confront the person named, or warn them
- Tell the team, or discuss it casually
- Try to talk the person out of it, or hint at consequences
- Change the person's work, rating, pay or team because they raised a concern, unless advised by [HR contact]
- Try to find out who raised an anonymous concern

**A manager's checklist**

- [ ] I listened without judging
- [ ] I did not promise to keep it secret
- [ ] I wrote down what I heard, with the date
- [ ] I passed it on within the set time
- [ ] I told the person what happens next
- [ ] I have not discussed it with anyone who does not need to know
- [ ] I will watch for retaliation and act if I see it

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A developer tells you privately that the lead is skipping security checks before launch, and begs you not to tell anyone | Explain you cannot promise silence on a risk to a client store. Offer to take it to [ethics contact] together, and keep their name restricted |
| A designer says her project manager keeps taking credit for her work and she is considering leaving | Listen, ask what she wants. If it is a personal grievance, support an informal conversation or point her to [HR contact]. Do not tell the project manager what she said |
| You overhear two team members saying someone "reported the invoice thing" and joking about who it was | Stop it now, remind them of confidentiality and that retaliation is a conduct matter, and tell [HR contact] |
| A team member raises a concern about you | Do not respond defensively or ask who else knows. Step back from the matter, tell [HR contact], and let a neutral person handle it |

**Your own wellbeing.** Hearing a serious concern can be hard. You can get support from [HR contact] without revealing details that must stay confidential.

## 14. Going outside the company: regulators, police and media

Some matters are serious enough that authorities need to know. Understand how this fits with raising a concern inside the company.

**The company's expectation.** [Confirm policy.] The company expects staff to raise concerns **internally first**, where it is safe to do so, because most problems can be fixed fastest from inside and the company then has a chance to put things right. Internal reporting does not take away your right to contact an authority where the law allows it.

**Serious matters may also be reported to authorities.** For example:

- A crime, such as fraud, theft or bribery, may be reported to the police
- A breach of a sector or financial rule may be reported to the relevant regulator
- Immediate danger to life or safety may be reported to the emergency services at once
- Data protection and cyber incidents may need to be reported to the relevant authority by the company within set times. See Data Privacy and Confidentiality and Cyber Security Basics for Everyone

**Internal first, but not at any cost.** You do not have to raise a concern internally first if

- You reasonably believe it would put someone in danger
- You reasonably believe evidence would be destroyed
- The concern is about the people who would receive it, and there is no independent route
- You have already raised it internally and it was ignored

**The media and social media.** [Confirm policy.]

- Do not take a concern to journalists, social media, influencers or a public forum before using the internal routes
- Public disclosure can harm clients, colleagues and the person named, and may breach confidentiality duties and contracts
- Public statements for the company are made only by authorised people (see Social Media and Public Communication)
- If you are considering going public, speak to [ethics contact] or take independent legal advice first

**Before going outside, think about**

- Whether you have confidential client or personal data that you are not allowed to share
- Whether you would be breaching a contract or law, even with good intentions
- Whether you can take independent advice, such as from a lawyer or a trade body
- Whether the route you choose gives you legal protection. Protection from the company's policy does not automatically extend to outside disclosures [confirm with a legal adviser]

**Do not**

- Share client store credentials, customer lists or ad account data with anyone outside the company as "proof"
- Threaten to go to the media or a regulator to get a personal outcome

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A finance executive finds evidence that a vendor is receiving payments with no work done and suspects a senior leader is involved | Use [ethics contact] or the audit committee chair. If there is no safe internal route, take independent legal advice before going to the authorities |
| An employee sees a colleague about to leave a client's customer data on a public link | Report to [security contact] immediately. That is a security incident, not a media matter |
| A copywriter posts "my agency is hiding fraud" on social media without raising it first | This breaches the policy and can harm others. Ask them to remove it and raise the concern properly, without punishing the underlying concern |

## 15. The legal background in India, kept simple

This section is general information, not legal advice. Laws change and details vary, so it is only a starting point. [Confirm with a legal adviser.]

**Vigil mechanism under company law.** Under Indian company law, some companies, such as certain listed companies and others meeting size or borrowing conditions, must set up a **vigil mechanism** (a whistleblower mechanism) for directors and employees to report genuine concerns. Whether this applies to our organisation depends on its legal form and size. [Confirm with a legal adviser whether this applies.] Even where it is not required, many organisations set one up as good practice, and this module describes that approach.

**The Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014.** This law deals with disclosures about corruption or misuse of power by **public servants**, made to a competent authority. It does not by itself cover most private sector employees or private companies. Please do not assume that this Act protects a disclosure about a private company. [Confirm with a legal adviser.]

**Where protection comes from for most employees here**

- **Company policy.** This module and the whistleblowing and grievance policy are the main source of protection for our staff
- **Employment contracts and workplace rules.** These may set out duties of fair treatment
- **Other laws** may protect people in particular situations. For example, the POSH Act protects those who complain of sexual harassment, and labour, data protection and sector laws may apply to specific matters. Which ones apply depends on the facts, the state and the person's role. [Confirm with HR or a legal adviser.]
- **Courts and tribunals.** Unfair dismissal and similar claims are decided under the law that applies to the employee's category. [Confirm with a legal adviser.]

**What this means for you**

- Do not assume that the law will automatically protect a private company employee from retaliation. This is why the company's own commitments in chapter 8 matter, and why we treat them as strict
- If you are worried about your legal position, you may speak to an independent lawyer. This is your right
- Contracts often include confidentiality terms. Raising a concern internally under the policy is always allowed. Disclosing client or personal data externally can raise legal issues, so take advice first

**Honest limits.** This module does not set out specific sections, penalties, deadlines or definitions of any law. Where you need those, rely on the organisation's legal adviser and the current text of the law.

## 16. Employee questions and sample answers

These are common questions. Your own policy and your HR contact have the final say.

**1. How do I know whether to use the grievance route or the whistleblowing route?**
Ask whether the problem is mainly about how you are treated, or about something wrong that would matter even if it never touched you. The first is a grievance, the second is whistleblowing. If it is both, pick the nearest route and mention the other angle.

**2. Do I need proof before I raise a concern?**
No. You need an honest belief that something is wrong. Describe what you saw or heard, say what you do not know, and let the trained people check the facts.

**3. Can I raise a concern with my manager instead of a formal channel?**
Yes, that is often the quickest route. Your manager must pass serious concerns to [ethics contact] or [HR contact], so say if you would prefer to go straight there.

**4. What if my manager is the problem?**
Go to their manager, [HR contact] or [ethics contact]. You never need to take a concern to the person it is about.

**5. Will my name be kept secret?**
It will be kept confidential and shared only with people who need it to handle the matter. We cannot promise complete secrecy, because the law may require disclosure or the facts may make the source obvious. We will tell you before sharing your name if we can.

**6. Is it better to report anonymously?**
It is available, and sometimes it is the right choice. But anonymous reports are harder to investigate and we cannot give you feedback. If you can, a confidential report is usually more effective.

**7. What happens if I am wrong?**
If you raised the concern honestly, nothing happens to you. Being mistaken or unable to prove something is not misconduct.

**8. What counts as retaliation?**
Any unfair treatment because you raised a concern, such as dismissal, a poor rating, being left out, worse work, or social pressure. Report it to [HR contact] or [ethics contact] straight away.

**9. How long will it take?**
You should get an acknowledgement within [3 working days] and an initial assessment within [7 working days]. Investigations usually finish within [30 working days], and you will get updates if it takes longer.

**10. Why was I not told what happened to the person I reported?**
Disciplinary details are private to that person, as yours would be to you. You will be told that the matter has been dealt with and, where possible, the general outcome.

**11. Can I report something I heard from someone else?**
Yes. Say it is second hand, and who told you if they are happy for that to be shared. If they are not, tell the receiver that, and they will advise.

**12. Can I record a conversation to keep evidence?**
Please do not record people secretly. It can breach trust and rules, and it may cause legal problems. Keep notes with dates and keep the messages and documents you legitimately have.

**13. Can I forward emails or client files to my personal account as proof?**
No. Moving confidential or client data to personal accounts can itself breach data rules. Tell the receiver where the evidence is, and they will secure it properly.

**14. My colleague told me something serious and asked me to keep it secret. What do I do?**
Be kind and listen, but do not promise secrecy. Encourage them to report, offer to go with them, and if it involves safety, fraud or harassment tell them you may need to pass it on to [HR contact].

**15. Can I bring someone with me to a meeting?**
For grievance hearings, usually yes, a colleague can accompany you [confirm against policy]. For whistleblowing interviews, ask the investigator what is allowed.

**16. What if I am a freelancer or intern?**
You can raise concerns and grievances too. Ask [HR contact] how the process applies to your arrangement. Retaliation protection is intended to apply to you [confirm against policy].

**17. Does the law protect me if I report wrongdoing at a private company?**
It depends on the situation and the law that applies, and the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 is about public servants and does not by itself cover most private employees. Your main protection is company policy. For a view on your own position, speak to an independent lawyer.

**18. Can I go to the police or a regulator?**
Serious matters can be reported to the authorities, and the company expects you to raise concerns internally first where that is safe [confirm policy]. If there is danger or no safe internal route, you may need to act sooner. Take advice if you are unsure.

**19. I raised a concern and nothing seems to have happened. What now?**
Ask your contact for an update. If you are not satisfied, ask [ethics contact] for a review or contact [the audit committee chair or a director].

**20. I am named in a concern. What should I do?**
Stay calm, cooperate and be honest. Do not try to find out who raised it, and do not delete anything. You will be told what it is about and given a fair chance to respond.

**21. Can a manager discipline me while my concern is open?**
Only for unrelated and well documented reasons, handled fairly. If you think it is linked to your report, tell [HR contact] immediately.

**22. Is sexual harassment handled under this process?**
No. It goes to the Internal Committee under POSH Awareness for Everyone. If you are not sure, contact [HR contact] and they will direct you.

**23. What if the issue is a security incident, like a lost laptop?**
Contact [security contact] straight away. Speed matters more than process. Follow Cyber Security Basics for Everyone.

**24. How do I raise a concern about discrimination?**
Use [HR contact] or [ethics contact], or the formal grievance procedure if it affects you personally. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone for the standards of behaviour we expect.

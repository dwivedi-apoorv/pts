# HR Policies Handbook for Everyone

This handbook is for everyone in the organisation: developers, designers, marketers, writers, project managers, finance, admin, sales, leaders, interns and freelancers. It walks through the people policies you will meet from your first day to your last: joining, working hours, leave, pay, performance, learning, conduct, leaving and the paperwork that goes with each. It is written for non-specialists in plain language, with examples from a digital agency and its clients. It is general information and does not replace the organisation's own written HR policies or the laws that apply to us. Where it touches law, it describes the principle only and is not legal advice.

## 1. Why everyone needs an HR handbook

Most people think about HR policies only when something goes wrong: a leave request is refused, a payslip looks odd, a notice period is unclear. A short, friendly handbook means you know the rules before you need them, and you know whom to ask.

**This handbook will help you**

- Understand what to expect at each stage of your time with us
- Know which rules apply to you, whether you are an employee, a contractor, an intern or a freelancer
- Apply for leave, report a late arrival or raise a payroll error without guessing
- Understand how performance reviews and fair discipline work
- Leave well, with your dues and your documents in order

**Where the full policies live.** This handbook is a guide, not the rulebook. The full, signed-off policies sit in [HR policy folder on the intranet]. If this handbook and the written policy ever differ, the written policy applies, and you should tell [hr@company] so the handbook can be corrected.

**The rules to remember**

1. **Ask early.** A short message to HR or your manager before something happens is almost always easier than a long conversation afterwards.
2. **Put it in writing.** Leave approvals, changes to pay details, role changes and agreements should be on [the HR system] or email, not only in a chat or a hallway.
3. **Fair process for everyone.** Every policy here is meant to be applied consistently and respectfully, whatever your role, gender, background or seniority.

**Law sets the floor.** Indian central laws and state rules set minimum standards on matters such as leave, working hours, maternity benefits, gratuity and provident fund. Our policies must meet or beat those minimums. Which rules apply to us depends on the state, the size of the organisation and the type of work, and rules change. Wherever this handbook mentions a legal minimum, treat it as a principle only. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**How to use this handbook.** Read chapters 1 to 3 once. Skim chapters 4 to 9 and come back when you need them, for example before applying for leave or before a review. Read chapters 10 to 12 so you know how conduct, outside work and equipment rules work. Read chapters 13 and 14 before you resign or if you manage someone who is leaving. Use chapter 15 as your quick reference, chapter 16 for common questions and chapter 17 for the glossary and a short knowledge check. If you are unsure, ask [hr@company].

### Induction

Induction is a conversation, not a form. In the first weeks you should meet your manager, a buddy, and the people you will work with most. You should receive access to your tools, read the key policies (this handbook, Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone, Cyber Security Basics for Everyone, POSH Awareness for Everyone) and understand how to reach HR.

### Probation

Probation is a period of [3 months] in which you and the organisation check whether the role is a good fit. Some roles may have a longer or shorter period [as stated in your appointment letter].

**What good probation looks like**

- Clear goals for the first [30, 60 and 90 days]
- A short check-in with your manager every [two weeks]
- Honest feedback, both positive and developmental, well before the end
- A written outcome: confirmed, probation extended for a stated reason and period, or not confirmed

**Notice during probation.** Notice for resigning or ending employment during probation is often shorter than after confirmation. Yours is [as stated in your appointment letter].

### Confirmation

Confirmation is not automatic and not a surprise. Your manager should discuss the outcome with you before the formal letter is issued. If you are not confirmed, you are entitled to hear the reasons and to ask questions. You will receive a confirmation letter stating any changes to terms [within 7 working days of the decision].

## 3. Types of working relationship

People work with us in different ways. The rules, rights and responsibilities differ. This chapter gives a high-level view. Your own letter or contract is the final word.

| Type | Who they are | Key features at a high level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-time employee | Appointed on the payroll for a continuing role | Appointment letter, salary through payroll, statutory benefits as applicable, leave and policy entitlements |
| Contract staff | Engaged for a fixed term or project, often directly or through an agency | Contract states duration, fee, notice and benefits; some policies apply in full, some in part |
| Intern or trainee | Learning on the job for a fixed period | Internship letter, stipend where paid, mentoring, a defined end date, certificate on completion |
| Freelancer or consultant | An independent professional providing a service | Service agreement, invoices instead of payslips, own tools and tax, not an employee |

### Why the difference matters

- **Leave and holidays.** Employees follow the leave policy. Contract staff, interns and freelancers follow what their contract says [for example, interns may get [1 day] of leave per month].
- **Pay.** Employees get payslips with statutory deductions. Freelancers and consultants raise invoices, and tax deducted at source may apply [confirm with HR or a legal adviser].
- **Benefits.** Health cover, provident fund and similar benefits depend on the type of relationship and on law. [HR to confirm eligibility for each group]
- **Reporting line.** Everyone has a person they report to or work with. For freelancers and contractors this is the project lead or [the engagement owner].

### Behaviour rules apply to everyone

Whatever your type of engagement, the rules on respectful conduct, confidentiality, data protection, conflicts of interest and POSH apply to you. Read Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone, Data Privacy and Confidentiality, and POSH Awareness for Everyone.

**A note on classification.** How a person is classified (employee or independent contractor) can have legal consequences. Do not describe an arrangement to a client or to a worker in a way that differs from what is in the contract. If a freelancer works like a full-time team member, tell HR so the arrangement can be reviewed. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

## 5. Late arrivals, early exits, overtime and comp-off

Life is not always on schedule. This chapter explains how we handle it fairly.

### Late arrival and early exit

- Tell your manager before your start time, or as early as you can, if you will be late
- Use the same channel every time, for example [a message on the team chat], and log it in [the HR system]
- Occasional lateness with notice is normal. A repeated pattern is discussed with you kindly first
- Late marks beyond [3 in a month] may be adjusted against leave or treated as part of the conversation about attendance, as set out in [the attendance policy]

**Early exit.** Ask for approval in advance where you can. If the reason is urgent, such as an emergency, tell your manager at once and complete the record afterwards.

### Short-notice cover

If you cannot attend, hand over what is time-sensitive:

1. Tell your manager and the project lead
2. Name who will cover urgent tasks, such as a store launch, an ad budget check or a client reply
3. Leave notes in the shared project tool, not only in a private message

### Overtime

Extra hours happen in agency work, especially around launches and sale seasons. They should be planned, voluntary where possible and recognised.

| Topic | Default rule |
| --- | --- |
| Who can ask you to work extra hours | Your manager, in advance, with a reason |
| How extra hours are recorded | In [the HR system] by [the end of the same week] |
| Recognition | Compensatory time off (comp-off) in most cases, or overtime pay where the law or your contract requires it |
| Limit | Not more than [10 extra hours a week] without a conversation about workload |

Overtime pay rules for employees are set by law, and they vary by state and by type of work and pay band. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

### Comp-off

Comp-off is a day or part of a day off in return for approved work on a holiday, a weekly off or long extra hours.

- Comp-off must be approved before the extra work, where practical
- It must usually be taken within [60 days] of the extra work, or it lapses
- A comp-off day is recorded in [the HR system] like leave
- Comp-off is generally not encashed unless [policy] says so

**Do not** expect to build up unlimited extra hours. If you are regularly working long hours, tell your manager. It is a workload problem to solve, not a badge of honour. See Health, Safety and Wellbeing for Everyone.

### Scenario table

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |

| You worked late every night this week to meet a deadline | Record the hours and talk to your manager about comp-off and about why the workload spiked |
| You will be 30 minutes late because of a delayed train | Message your manager before your start time and log it as a late arrival |

## 8. Performance management

Performance management is how we agree what good looks like, support you in reaching it and recognise results. It is a conversation, not a once-a-year surprise.

### The cycle

| Step | When | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goals | [April, or within 30 days of joining] | You and your manager agree 3 to 5 clear goals |
| Regular feedback | Ongoing, plus a one-to-one every [two weeks] | Short, specific feedback in both directions |
| Mid-year check | [October] | Review progress, adjust goals |
| Year-end review | [March] | You write a self-review, your manager adds theirs, and you discuss |
| Calibration | [April] | Managers check ratings for fairness across the team |
| Outcomes | [May] | Ratings, increments, promotions and development plans communicated |

### Setting good goals

A good goal is specific, measurable, realistic, relevant and time-bound. For example, "Reduce average Shopify product page load time on the main client store to under [2.5 seconds] by [end of September]" is better than "Improve site speed".

Goals should include the work you deliver and also how you work: collaboration, quality, client care and living the values in Company Culture and Values.

### Feedback

- **Ask for it.** Ask "What is one thing I should do more, and one thing I should do less?"
- **Give it fairly.** Describe what you saw, the effect it had, and what would be better
- **Receive it calmly.** You do not have to agree on the spot. Take a day, and then respond

### Ratings

Ratings in a [5-point scale] describe performance against goals in the period. They are not a judgement on you as a person. Your rating should never be a surprise: if your manager expects a low rating, you should have heard it, with examples, in earlier conversations.

### Support and improvement plans

If your performance is below expectations:

1. Your manager explains the gap with examples and listens to your view
2. You agree an improvement plan with clear targets, support and a review date, usually [60 to 90 days]
3. You have regular check-ins and receive written notes of progress
4. At the end, there is a fair outcome: back on track, plan extended with reasons, or next steps under the disciplinary or separation process

An improvement plan is a chance to improve, not the first step to dismissal. If you feel it is unfair, you can speak to HR or use the grievance route.

## 10. Conduct, discipline and grievances

Most workplace issues are solved with a conversation. This chapter explains how we deal with conduct fairly when a conversation is not enough.

**What good conduct is.** Respect, honesty, care for clients, confidentiality and safe working. These are set out in Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone, which applies to every person at every level. Also read POSH Awareness for Everyone and Social Media and Public Communication.

### A fair disciplinary process in stages

| Stage | What happens | Your rights |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Informal discussion | Your manager talks to you about the concern and agrees what should change | You can explain, and ask for support |
| 2. Written notice | If the issue continues or is serious, you receive a written notice stating the concern and the policy | You know exactly what is alleged |
| 3. Written explanation | You are given [3 working days] to respond in writing | You can share your side and any evidence |
| 4. Hearing | A meeting with a manager and HR to hear your explanation | You can be accompanied by [a colleague] as per policy, and can ask for the matter to be explained in a language you understand |
| 5. Decision | A written decision with reasons, based on facts, within [10 working days] of the hearing | The decision is proportionate and consistent |
| 6. Appeal | You can appeal to a [more senior leader] within [7 working days] | Someone not involved in the first decision reviews it |

**Outcomes** depend on the facts and may include: no action, a verbal or written warning, training or coaching, a change of duties, recovery of loss where allowed by law, suspension during investigation on [stated terms], or ending the engagement. Serious matters such as fraud, harassment or major data breaches can skip early stages. Even then, you have the right to hear the allegation and respond.

**Rules of fairness**

- The process should be impartial and free of bias
- The person deciding should not be a person directly involved
- Confidentiality is respected as far as possible
- No one is punished for raising a concern in good faith (see Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling))

Some matters follow their own laws and committees, such as sexual harassment complaints, which go to the Internal Committee under the POSH process. Other matters follow standing orders or labour laws that apply to some establishments. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

### Raising a grievance

If you are unhappy with how you are treated, pay, workload or a decision:

1. Talk to your manager if you can
2. If that does not help, or the issue is with your manager, write to [HR at hr@company]
3. HR acknowledges within [2 working days] and responds with a plan within [10 working days]
4. If not resolved, escalate to [the HR head or a senior leader]
5. For serious or sensitive concerns, use the channels in Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling)

## 11. Outside work, equipment and information

### Outside work and moonlighting

Moonlighting means doing paid or unpaid work for someone else while employed with us. Interests outside work are normal. The concern is conflict, confidentiality and fatigue.

**Do**

- Tell HR and your manager in writing before starting any paid outside work, board role, business or freelance project
- Wait for written approval, which is given within [10 working days]
- Keep outside work outside working hours and off company equipment

**Do not**

- Work for a competitor or a client of ours without approval
- Use our client data, tools, time or contacts for outside work
- Take outside work that affects your performance or availability

## 14. Gratuity, provident fund and changes to policies

### Gratuity, in general terms

Gratuity is a lump sum that an employer pays to an employee as a thank you for long service. In India it is covered by the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, for establishments within its scope. In general terms:

- It is usually payable after the employee has completed a minimum period of continuous service, which is generally [5 years]
- It is based on the last drawn pay and the years of service
- There are exceptions for death or disability, and the formula and limits are set by law and can change
- Where the Act does not apply, the organisation may still offer its own gratuity or similar benefit

Do not rely on this summary for a specific calculation. Ask HR for a written statement. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

### Provident fund, in general terms

The provident fund is a long-term retirement savings scheme. Employee and employer each contribute to your account, and the balance grows with interest set by the government.

- You are identified by a Universal Account Number (UAN) that stays with you across jobs
- If you have a UAN from a previous job, give it to HR on joining, and do not create a new one
- When you change jobs, you can usually transfer your balance to the new account. Do this through the official portal, with HR's help
- Withdrawals, tax treatment and eligibility follow the scheme rules, which change from time to time. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**Do** keep your UAN, PF passbook and KYC details up to date. **Do not** share your PF login or OTP with anyone, including colleagues.

## 16. Employee questions and sample answers

These are questions people often ask. The answers are general and show the proposed defaults. Check the written policy for your exact entitlement.

**1. Where do I find the full HR policies?**
They sit in [HR policy folder on the intranet]. This handbook is a friendly summary. If the two ever differ, the written policy applies, so please tell HR and we will fix the handbook.

**2. Is my probation period fixed for everyone?**
No. The default is [3 months], but your appointment letter is what counts. It can be extended in writing, with reasons and a clear new date, after a conversation with you.

**3. What if I forget to mark my attendance?**
Request a correction in [the HR system] within [2 working days], with your manager's approval. If you forget often, talk to your manager so you can find a habit that works.

**4. Can I work from home whenever I like?**
Not automatically. Your model (office, hybrid or remote) is agreed with your manager and recorded. For one-off needs, ask your manager, and make sure you can be reached and your work and security needs are met.

**5. I will be late tomorrow. Do I need to apply for leave?**
Not for a short, occasional delay. Message your manager before your start time and log it. Repeated lateness or long absence is handled under the attendance rules in [the attendance policy].

**6. Do I get paid for working on a public holiday?**
Usually you either get a comp-off or extra pay, as the policy and the law provide. Get approval before the work and record the hours. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**7. How many days of leave do I have?**
Check your balance in [the HR system]. The proposed defaults are [8 days] casual, [8 days] sick and [15 days] earned leave a year, but your actual entitlement is in your letter and the leave policy.

**8. Can my leave be refused?**
Yes, sometimes, for business reasons such as a critical client launch. Your manager should talk to you and offer other dates. Refusal should be fair and consistent. Sick or emergency leave is handled differently.

**9. What happens to unused leave at year end?**
Under the defaults, up to [10 days] of earned leave carry forward, and casual and sick leave lapse. Some leave can be encashed. The policy and law decide, so ask HR. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**10. I am expecting a baby. When should I tell HR?**
As early as you feel comfortable, and no later than [8 weeks before the expected date]. HR will explain your leave, pay and benefits in writing. The Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 sets minimum protections. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**11. My salary is wrong this month. What do I do?**
Compare your payslip with your leave and attendance record, then write to [payroll@company] by [the 7th] with the details. Payroll replies in [3 working days].

**12. Is a reimbursement part of my salary?**
No. Reimbursements for business expenses are separate and follow the Expense and Travel Policy for Everyone. They appear separately on the payslip.

**13. Why is tax deducted from my salary?**
Employers must deduct income tax at source on salary, based on your estimated annual income and declarations. Submit your declarations on time to avoid a surprise. Ask payroll or a tax adviser about your own situation.

**14. How is my performance rated?**
Against the goals you agreed, and how you worked. Ratings use a [5-point scale] and are checked across teams for fairness. You should get feedback throughout the year, so the final rating is never a shock.

**15. What if I disagree with my rating?**
Ask for the reasons and examples, share your evidence, and request a review within [10 working days]. A reasoned, calm conversation usually works best.

**16. Does an improvement plan mean I will be dismissed?**
No. It is a structured chance to improve, with support and a review date. Most plans end with the person back on track. If the problem remains, further steps follow a fair process.

**17. Who pays for my certification?**
Often the organisation, up to your annual budget of [Rs 15,000], if the course is linked to your role and approved beforehand. Get approval in writing before you pay.

**18. Can I take up a weekend freelance project?**
Only after telling HR and your manager and getting written approval. Work for a competitor or client, or work that uses our time, tools or data, is not allowed. See [the conflict of interest and gifts policy].

**19. Can I use my work laptop for personal things?**
Limited personal use is fine if it is legal, safe and does not affect work. Do not store client data on personal accounts, and do not install unapproved software. See Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools for Everyone.

**20. What happens in a disciplinary process?**
Typically an informal talk, then written notice, your written explanation, a hearing, a reasoned decision and the right to appeal. Serious cases can move faster, but you always have the right to hear the allegation and respond.

**21. How long is my notice period?**
Usually [30 days] after confirmation, and shorter during probation, but your appointment letter is what counts. Notice can sometimes be shortened by agreement. Ask HR in writing.

**22. When will I get my final settlement?**
Usually within [45 days] of your last working day, with a written statement of what is paid and what is deducted. If it is late, write to HR and then to [the HR head]. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**23. Will I get gratuity if I leave after three years?**
Under the general rule, gratuity needs a minimum period of continuous service, which is commonly [5 years], with exceptions. Whether it applies to us, and how it is calculated, depends on the law and policy. Ask HR for a written answer. [confirm with HR or a legal adviser]

**24. Can I keep my old PF account when I change jobs?**
Yes. Your UAN stays with you. Give it to HR when you join, and transfer your balance through the official portal. HR can guide you.

By email and on [the intranet], at least [14 days] before it takes effect, unless the law requires a faster change. Ask HR if you are unsure how it affects you.

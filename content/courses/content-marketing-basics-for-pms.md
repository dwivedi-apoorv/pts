# Content Marketing Basics for Project Managers

This module gives project managers enough knowledge of content marketing and blogging to follow what writers, editors and SEO specialists are saying, review briefs and drafts sensibly, and answer client questions without overpromising. It explains what good content is, how it is planned and produced, and how it is measured. It is written for non-specialists and uses online store examples in USD. It does not describe our internal process; see the main onboarding module for that.

## 1. Why project managers need content marketing basics

Content is one of the slowest services to judge and one of the easiest to get wrong. A project manager who understands it can:

- Tell a useful content plan from a pile of articles
- Review a content brief and see what is missing before writing starts
- Read a draft and notice quality problems that a client will notice
- Explain why content takes months to show results
- Keep approvals, edits and publishing on schedule
- Report content results honestly, including what the numbers cannot show

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 12 once. Use chapter 13 and chapter 14 (client questions) when preparing for client calls, and the glossary in chapter 15 when a term is unfamiliar.

## 2. What content marketing is

**Content marketing** means creating and sharing useful material, such as articles, guides, videos and emails, that attracts the right people, builds trust, and helps them move toward buying. The aim is not to advertise directly, but to be helpful so that people find, trust and choose the brand.

**What content does for an online store**

- **Attracts visitors** who search for answers, ideas and comparisons
- **Builds trust** by showing the brand knows its products and customers
- **Supports SEO** by targeting searches that product pages cannot, and by earning links
- **Helps buyers decide** with guides, comparisons and answers
- **Feeds other channels** with material for email, social media and ads
- **Keeps customers engaged** after they buy

**Content and SEO.** Most blog content is written to be found in search. That is why SEO and content work closely together: the SEO specialist chooses topics and keywords, and the writer produces content that answers them well. Read the SEO basics module for the background.

**Content takes time.** An article does not usually bring much traffic in its first weeks. Results build over months as search engines index it, understand it and trust it, and as the site gains more content on the same subject.

## 3. Audience and the buyer journey

Good content starts with understanding the reader.

**Know the audience.** Who are they, what do they want, what are their questions, and what words do they use? The best sources are customer questions, reviews, support messages, search data and sales conversations.

**The buyer journey.** People move through stages, and each stage needs different content.

| Stage | What the person is doing | Content that helps | Example for a running shoe store |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Realising they have a problem or need | Educational articles, guides, videos | How to start running without injury |
| Consideration | Comparing options | Comparisons, buying guides, reviews | How to choose running shoes for flat feet |
| Decision | Ready to buy | Product pages, collection pages, offers, FAQs, reviews | Best-selling trail shoes, with sizing guide |
| After purchase | Using the product, may buy again | Care guides, emails, tips, community | How to clean and extend the life of your shoes |

**A common mistake.** Many blogs only publish awareness content, which brings visitors who are not ready to buy. A strong plan covers all stages and links readers to the next step.

## 4. Content strategy and topic planning

**A content strategy** answers: what will we publish, for whom, why, how often, and how will we know it works?

**Steps**

1. **Set goals.** Examples are more organic traffic, more email subscribers, more sales from organic search, and stronger trust.
2. **Define the audience** and the questions they ask.
3. **Research topics and keywords.** Find what people search for, and check what already ranks.
4. **Group topics.** Build topic clusters: one main page on a broad subject, supported by detailed articles on related questions, all linked to each other.
5. **Prioritise.** Start with topics that match the business, have real demand, and that the site can realistically rank for.
6. **Plan the calendar.** Decide the cadence, the owners and the due dates.
7. **Decide the formats and the calls to action.**

**Topic clusters in plain words.** A store selling coffee equipment might have a main guide to home espresso, with supporting articles about grinders, milk steaming and cleaning machines. Together they show the site covers the subject in depth, and each article links the reader onward to relevant products.

**Where good topic ideas come from**

- Questions customers ask by email, chat and social media
- Search tools, including the search terms in Search Console
- Competitor content and gaps in it
- Product knowledge and seasonal events
- Reviews and returns, which show confusion about products

**Quality over quantity.** More articles are not better. A smaller number of excellent, well-targeted articles usually beats many thin ones.

## 5. Content types for online stores

| Type | What it is | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blog posts and how-to articles | Articles that answer questions and explain | Awareness, search traffic |
| Buying guides | Help choosing between options | Consideration and decisions |
| Comparison articles | Product A versus product B, or best-of lists | Consideration |
| Collection page copy | Text on category pages | Search rankings and shopper guidance |
| Product descriptions | Details and benefits for each product | Decisions and search |
| FAQs | Short answers to common questions | Trust, support, search |
| Case studies and customer stories | Real examples of use or results | Trust |
| Email newsletters | Regular messages to subscribers | Repeat purchases, engagement |
| Video | Demonstrations, reviews and tutorials | Engagement, trust and YouTube search |
| Lookbooks and inspiration content | Collections of ideas and styled products | Browsing and inspiration |
| Downloadable guides | Longer resources exchanged for an email address | Building an email list |

**Choosing formats.** Match the format to the topic and the audience. A how-to may work best as a video and an article together. Start with the formats the team can produce well.

## 6. Briefs: how good content starts

A **content brief** is a short document that tells the writer exactly what to produce. A good brief saves time, prevents rewrites and improves quality.

**What a brief should include**

- The working title and the main topic
- The goal of the piece and the reader's stage in the buyer journey
- The target audience
- The main keyword and a few related phrases, and the search intent behind them
- A suggested outline with headings
- What the article must cover, and what to avoid
- Length guidance, treated as a guide and not a target for padding
- Examples of strong competitor or reference content
- Facts, sources and product details to use
- Internal links to include, such as relevant products, collections and other articles
- The call to action
- The tone and voice
- The due date and who approves

**Tip for PMs.** Before work starts, check that each brief answers three questions: who is this for, what should they do after reading it, and what makes it better than what already exists?

**Common brief problems**

- No clear reader or goal
- A keyword and nothing else
- No product or source information, so the writer guesses
- Copying a competitor's structure with no added value

## 7. Writing, editing and quality

**What good content looks like**

- It answers the reader's question completely and clearly
- It is accurate and uses reliable sources
- It shows real experience or product knowledge, not generic filler
- It is easy to read, with short paragraphs, clear headings and plain words
- It is original, with a point of view, examples or detail others lack
- It guides the reader to a sensible next step

**Quality and trust.** Google's guidance for search content emphasises helpful, reliable content written for people. A commonly used idea is E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness. In practice this means real knowledge, honest claims, named authors where suitable, and accurate information, especially on topics that affect health, money or safety.

**The editing stages**

1. **Structure check:** does the article follow the brief and answer the question?
2. **Content edit:** accuracy, depth, examples and gaps
3. **Line edit:** clarity, grammar, tone and consistency
4. **SEO check:** title, headings, keyword use, links and metadata
5. **Final review:** facts, links, images and formatting

**Fact checking.** Check claims, numbers, product details and quotes against reliable sources, and keep a list of sources. Wrong information damages trust.

**Using AI tools.** AI can help with research, outlines and drafts. Search engines focus on whether content is helpful and reliable, not on how it was produced. Unreviewed AI text tends to be generic, can contain errors and rarely stands out. Any AI-assisted content needs human review, fact checking, real product knowledge and an original angle. Agree with the client how AI tools may be used.

**Plagiarism and originality.** All content must be original. Copying or lightly rewording other sites is risky, harms quality and can cause legal and search problems.

**Quality checklist before approval**

- [ ] Matches the brief and answers the reader's question
- [ ] Facts and numbers are accurate, with sources noted
- [ ] No copied text, and images are licensed or original
- [ ] Clear headings, short paragraphs, natural language
- [ ] Internal links and the call to action are in place
- [ ] Title, headings and metadata follow SEO guidance
- [ ] Spelling, grammar and tone have been checked

## 8. SEO elements of a blog post

An article can be excellent and still fail to be found if the basics are missed.

| Element | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Title | Clear, includes the main topic naturally, and makes people want to click |
| Headings | A single main heading, with logical sub-headings that organise the content |
| Opening | Gets to the point and confirms the article answers the reader's question |
| Keyword use | Natural wording, with the main phrase and related terms where they fit, and no stuffing |
| Meta description | A short, honest summary that invites the click |
| URL | Short and readable |
| Images | Relevant, compressed for speed, with descriptive alt text |
| Internal links | Links to related articles, collections and products |
| External links | Links to reliable sources where useful |
| Lists, tables and FAQs | Make content easy to scan and can help answer specific questions |
| Calls to action | A clear next step, such as a related collection or an email signup |
| Mobile reading | Readable on a phone, with short blocks of text |

**Internal linking matters.** Links from articles to relevant products and collections send readers and authority to the pages that sell. Links between articles help readers and search engines see how topics connect.

**Avoid cannibalisation.** If two articles target the same search, they compete with each other. Plan one main page per topic.

## 9. Publishing and workflow

**The typical roles**

| Role | What they do |
| --- | --- |
| Content strategist or SEO specialist | Chooses topics and keywords, prepares briefs |
| Writer | Writes the draft |
| Editor | Improves, checks and finalises the draft |
| Designer | Creates images and graphics |
| Publisher | Formats the article, adds links, images and metadata, and publishes |
| Project manager | Keeps the schedule, handles approvals and reports |

One person may do several roles, and the work is the same.

**The typical workflow**

1. Topic and brief approved
2. Draft written
3. Edit and quality check
4. Client review and approval
5. Formatting, images and links added
6. SEO check
7. Publish or schedule
8. Share, track and review

**Publishing in Shopify.** Shopify's blog is used for articles on the store. The theme controls how blog posts look. Check that articles have clear formatting, images, links and metadata, and that the blog appears in the sitemap automatically.

**Keeping approvals moving**

- Agree who approves and the time they have
- Send drafts in batches with a clear deadline
- Ask for consolidated feedback in one place
- Keep a version history and the approved text

**Tip for PMs.** Late client feedback is the main cause of content delays. Build review time into the calendar from the start.

## 10. Distribution and repurposing

Publishing is not the end. Content needs to be shared, linked and reused.

- **Email.** Share new articles with subscribers, and use them in newsletters
- **Social media.** Turn key points into posts, short videos and carousels
- **Internal links.** Link new articles from older, relevant ones and from products and collections
- **Outreach.** Share useful pieces with relevant sites or partners, which can earn links
- **Repurposing.** Turn one article into a video, a checklist, an email series or several social posts
- **Paid promotion.** Promote the strongest pieces with ads to the right audiences

**A simple rule.** Spend time promoting good content, not only creating new content.

## 11. Measuring content

Content results show up slowly and across many metrics.

| Metric | What it tells you | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impressions and clicks | How often the content appears in search and how often it is clicked | Search Console |
| Rankings | Where pages appear for target searches | Search Console, SEO tools |
| Organic sessions | Visits from search to the content | GA4 |
| Engaged sessions and engagement rate | Whether visitors stayed and interacted | GA4 |
| Scroll depth and time on page | How far and how long people read | GA4 or a similar tool |
| Newsletter signups | Interest in hearing more | Email platform, GA4 |
| Conversions and revenue | Orders and sales linked to content visits | GA4, store analytics |
| Assisted conversions | Sales where content was one step in the journey | GA4 |
| Backlinks | Links earned by the content | SEO tools |

**Content often assists, not closes.** Many readers do not buy on their first visit. They come back later through another channel. That is why assisted conversions and trends matter, and why reports should not judge content only by last-click sales.

**Worked example.** A store pays $600 a month for four articles. After several months, the blog sends 3,000 organic sessions a month to the store. Those visits convert at 1 percent, with an average order value of $70, and the store keeps 45 percent of each sale as gross profit.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orders | 3,000 × 1 percent | 30 |
| Revenue | 30 × $70 | $2,100 |
| Gross profit | $2,100 × 45 percent | $945 |
| Content cost | | $600 |
| Profit after content cost | $945 - $600 | $345 |
| Return on content cost | $345 ÷ $600 × 100 | 57.5 percent |

**What this teaches**

- Content can pay for itself, but it needs enough traffic and time to do so
- Older articles keep bringing visitors, which is why content value tends to build
- The numbers above ignore assisted sales, email signups and brand awareness, so they understate the full effect
- Early on, before traffic builds, the same spend will look worse. Judge by trend over several months.

**Reading the numbers**

- Impressions rising, clicks flat: improve titles and descriptions
- Traffic rising, conversions flat: improve links to products and calls to action, or review the intent of the topics
- Good rankings, little traffic: the topics may have low search volume
- High exits and short reading time: the article may not match what the visitor wanted

## 12. Refreshing, pruning and legal basics

**Refreshing older content.** Search results change, products change and information becomes outdated. Updating an older article, by improving it, adding detail and fixing facts, often brings more benefit than writing a new one. Review top articles every few months and refresh ones that are slipping.

**Pruning and consolidating.** Thin, outdated or overlapping articles can be improved, merged into a stronger piece, or removed. If a page is removed or merged, add a redirect to the best replacement so visitors and search value are not lost.

**Legal and ethical basics**

- **Copyright.** Use original writing, and only images, video and music you own or have a licence to use. Credit and link to sources properly.
- **Claims.** Do not publish claims about health, money, results or safety that cannot be supported. Some claims are regulated.
- **Reviews and testimonials.** Use only genuine ones, with permission where needed.
- **Affiliate and sponsored content.** Disclose clearly where required by law and platform rules.
- **Customer information.** Do not publish personal details without permission.
- **Competitor mentions.** Keep comparisons fair and accurate.

**Tip for PMs.** When a client asks for a claim you cannot verify, say you will check, and ask for evidence before it is published.

## 13. Talking to clients: expectations and red flags

**What we can say with confidence**

- Good content builds traffic and trust over months
- We can plan, write, edit and publish content to a high standard
- Quality, relevance and consistency matter more than the number of articles
- We will report on content against goals we agree

**What we must never say**

- A guaranteed ranking, traffic number or revenue from content
- That an article will rank in a set number of weeks
- That volume alone will bring results
- That we can publish copied or rewritten competitor content safely

**Better ways to say it**

- Instead of "This article will rank on page one in a month", say "We have chosen this topic because there is real demand and we can compete. We expect to see early signs in impressions first and traffic after that."
- Instead of "More articles means more traffic", say "A smaller number of strong, well-linked articles usually performs better than many thin ones."

**Red flags**

- No clear audience or goal
- Topics chosen only because competitors have them
- Briefs that are only a keyword
- A demand for many cheap articles
- No subject knowledge available from the client
- Approvals that regularly take weeks
- Reports showing only the number of articles published

**When you are unsure.** It is better to say "I will check with the content team and come back to you by tomorrow" than to guess.

## 14. Client questions and sample answers

These are answers a project manager can give in plain language. Adapt the wording to the client, and check with the content team or SEO specialist for anything specific to the account.

**1. Why do we need a blog?**
A blog lets your store reach people who are searching for answers before they buy, builds trust, and supports your wider search performance. It also gives you material for email and social media.

**2. How long before the blog brings traffic?**
Early signs, such as impressions in search, can appear within weeks. Meaningful traffic usually builds over several months as the content is indexed and the site builds authority. The timing depends on your competition and your site.

**3. Can you guarantee our articles will rank on page one?**
No. Rankings depend on factors we do not control. What we commit to is research-based topics, high-quality content, good on-page work and clear reporting against your starting point.

**4. How many articles do we need?**
It depends on your goals, your market and your budget. A smaller number of excellent articles is better than many weak ones. We start with the topics that match your products and customers, and expand from there.

**5. How do you choose topics?**
We look at what your customers ask, what people search for, what competitors cover and where there are gaps, and we pick topics that fit your products. We share the plan with you for approval.

**6. Why do you need a brief for each article?**
A brief sets the reader, the goal, the keyword, the outline and the facts, so the writer gets the article right the first time. It saves time and reduces rewrites.

**7. Who writes the content, and can it sound like our brand?**
Our writers work to a tone of voice guide that you approve. Sharing examples of content you like and product information helps us match your voice.

**8. Do you use AI to write content?**
We can use AI tools to help with research and outlines, but everything is reviewed, fact-checked and edited by people, and we agree how AI may be used with you. Generic, unchecked AI text rarely performs well.

**9. How do you make sure the content is accurate?**
We check facts, numbers and product details against reliable sources and your own information, and we keep a source list. For sensitive subjects, we ask for your review or an expert's.

**10. Can we reuse text from competitors or suppliers?**
We do not copy content. Copied text is poor for search, can cause legal problems and does not help you stand out. We write original content, using supplier information only as a factual base.

**11. Why does the article need our approval?**
It speaks for your brand and the facts about your products are yours. We send drafts in batches with a clear deadline, so approval is quick and the schedule holds.

**12. Why did an article get no traffic?**
Common reasons include low search demand, strong competition, the page not yet being indexed or trusted, and weak links to it. We review the topic, the page and its links, and adjust.

**13. What is internal linking and why does it matter?**
Internal links connect articles to each other and to your products and collections. They help readers find what they need and help search engines understand the site.

**14. How long should an article be?**
As long as it needs to be to answer the question well, and no longer. We look at what already ranks to understand what is expected, and we do not pad content.

**15. Should we write about products or about general topics?**
Both. Product and collection pages target people ready to buy. General guides and how-tos reach people earlier. A mix that supports your products is the most effective.

**16. Can you update our old articles?**
Yes, and it is often worth doing. Refreshing and improving older articles can bring more benefit than writing new ones. We review which ones to update first.

**17. Why are some articles removed or merged?**
Thin or overlapping articles can weaken the site. We improve them, merge them into a stronger piece, or remove them, and we add redirects so you do not lose visitors.

**18. How do you measure content success?**
We track search impressions and clicks, rankings, organic visits, engagement, signups and orders, and we look at how content helps sales over time. We report against a starting point every month.

**19. Why does content not always produce direct sales?**
Many readers come to learn and buy later, often through another channel. We look at assisted conversions and trends, not only last-click sales, to see the full value of content.

**20. Can we have videos or guides as well as blog posts?**
Yes. Different formats suit different topics and audiences, and we can repurpose one piece of content into several. We will advise what fits your budget and goals.

**21. What do you need from us?**
Information about your products and customers, access to subject experts, examples of your voice, quick approvals and any rules about claims. Delays in these are a common cause of late publishing.

**22. Do we own the content you create?**
Ownership is set out in your agreement. As a general rule, the final published content is yours, and we use only original work or properly licensed material.

**Knowledge check**

1. Name the four stages of the buyer journey and one content type for each.
2. What is a topic cluster, and why do stores use them?
3. List five things a good content brief should include.
4. Why might a blog with many articles still bring little traffic?
5. A blog costs $500 a month and sends 2,000 organic sessions that convert at 1.5 percent with an average order value of $60 and a 40 percent gross profit margin. What is the monthly profit after content cost?
6. Why should AI-assisted content always have human review?
7. A client asks you to promise page one rankings within a month for an article. How do you respond?
8. What is cannibalisation, and how do you avoid it?
9. Why can last-click sales understate the value of content?
10. When an old article is merged into a better one, what must be done to protect traffic?

**Answer guide**

1. Awareness (educational how-tos), consideration (comparisons or buying guides), decision (product and collection pages, FAQs, reviews), and after purchase (care guides and emails).
2. A group of articles on related questions, linked to one main page. They show depth on a subject, help readers move around and strengthen search performance.
3. Any five of: topic and title, goal, audience, main keyword and intent, outline, what to cover and avoid, sources and product facts, internal links, call to action, tone, due date and approver.
4. The topics may have little demand, the articles may be thin or generic, there may be strong competition, or the content may not be linked or promoted well.
5. Orders = 2,000 × 1.5 percent = 30. Revenue = 30 × $60 = $1,800. Gross profit = $1,800 × 40 percent = $720. Profit after content cost = $720 - $500 = $220.
6. AI text can contain errors, be generic and lack real product knowledge. Human review adds accuracy, originality and trust.
7. No honest guarantee is possible. Explain why the topic was chosen, what early signs to expect and that results build over months.
8. It is when two pages target the same search and compete with each other. Avoid it by planning one main page per topic and merging overlapping articles.
9. Many readers use content to learn, then buy later through another channel, so content helps without getting the last click.
10. Add a redirect from the old address to the best replacement, so visitors and search value carry over.

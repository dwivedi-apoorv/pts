# Social Media and Public Communication

This module is for everyone in the organisation, whatever your role or seniority: developers, designers, marketers, writers, project managers, finance, admin, sales, leaders, interns and freelancers. It explains how to use social media and speak in public in a way that protects you, our clients and the company. It covers official and personal accounts, client confidentiality, photos and videos, talks and interviews, media enquiries, online abuse, copyright, advertising rules for the content we publish for clients, and what to do when something goes wrong. It is written for non-specialists in plain language and uses examples from a digital agency. It is general information and not legal advice, and it does not replace the organisation's own social media policy.

## 1. Why everyone needs social media awareness

A post takes ten seconds to write and can be read by thousands of people for years. It can be copied, screenshotted and shared long after you delete it. When you work at an agency, readers often cannot tell where "you" ends and "the company" begins. A comment about a client's product, a photo of your desk with a client dashboard in the background, or a joke about a competitor can be read as the company speaking.

**This module is for all of us because**

- Everyone has a personal account, and everyone is connected to colleagues, clients and recruiters on it
- Our clients trust us with unreleased launches, sales numbers and customer data, and one careless post can break that trust
- Many of us run client accounts, so we also publish on behalf of other brands, which has its own rules
- Most online mistakes are small and quick to fix if they are reported early

**What this module will help you do**

- Know which accounts are official and who may post for the company
- Decide quickly what is safe to say about the company, colleagues, clients and competitors
- Share photos, videos and work achievements without leaking anything
- Handle press calls, public complaints, trolls and abuse calmly
- Follow honest advertising practice in the content we publish for clients
- Report a mistake fast, without fear

**The four rules to remember**

1. **If it is not public, it is not yours to post.** Client work, results, numbers and screenshots stay private until the client has approved them in writing.
2. **Personal views are personal.** Say so, and never imply the company backs them.
3. **Pause before you post.** If you would be uncomfortable seeing it on a client's screen or a news site, do not post it.
4. **Report early.** A wrong post reported in ten minutes is a small problem. The same post found by a client next week is a big one.

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 5 once, as everyone needs them. Read chapters 6 to 9 before you speak at an event, get a press call or face a public complaint. Chapters 10 to 12 cover advocacy, sensitive topics and copyright. Chapters 13 and 14 matter most for people who create or publish content for clients or manage accounts. Use the questions in chapter 15 and the glossary in chapter 16 for quick reference. If you are unsure about anything, ask [social media policy owner].

## 2. Official accounts and personal accounts

There are two kinds of accounts, and the rules differ.

**Official accounts** are accounts that represent the company or a client brand. They include the company's pages on [LinkedIn, Instagram, X, YouTube and other platforms], and the accounts we manage for clients.

**Personal accounts** are yours. They are about your life and your views, even if you mention where you work.

| Type | Examples | Who may post | Key rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Company official account | [Company LinkedIn page], [company Instagram] | Only people named by [marketing lead] | Posts follow the content calendar and approval steps |
| Client brand account we manage | A client's Instagram, Facebook page or YouTube channel | Only the assigned team, with client approval as agreed in writing | Follow the client's brand guide, approvals and access rules |
| Team or project account | A team channel, a hiring page, a community group | Only the named owner and admins | Needs approval before creation, and a named owner |
| Personal account | Your own profiles | You | Your views, but you carry duties to the company and clients |

### Who may speak for the company

Only people authorised by [CEO or communications lead] may post or comment on behalf of the company. Being on the marketing team does not by itself mean you can speak for the company on every topic. If you are asked to speak for the company and you are not sure you are authorised, ask [marketing lead] first.

### Personal accounts: sensible habits

- Add a line like "Views are my own" if you often talk about work or the industry. It helps, but it does not remove your duties
- Check your privacy settings, and decide who can tag you or see your friends list
- Do not use the company logo as your profile picture unless [marketing lead] has approved it
- Do not create accounts that look like the company or a client. If you wish to start a team or community page, ask first
- Keep work conversations with clients on approved channels, not private social media chats [approved client communication tools]

**Do** use your real name and your honest job title on professional profiles.
**Do not** use a personal account to run, post to, or log in to a client's brand account. Use the access given for the job (see chapter 14).

## 3. What you can and cannot say about the company, colleagues, clients and competitors

You are free to be a person online. You are also an employee, and some limits apply. These limits are about protecting people and confidential information, not about silencing normal opinions.

### The company

**You may**
- Share public company news, such as blog posts, awards, job openings and event announcements
- Share your own honest experience of working here, in a respectful way
- Congratulate colleagues on public milestones

**You may not**
- Share confidential company information, such as revenue, pricing, internal plans, the client list (unless a client is publicly named), internal chats or documents
- Present a personal opinion as the company's official position
- Make claims on behalf of the company, such as promising features, results or timelines
- Post screenshots of internal tools, dashboards or chats

### Colleagues

- Do not share a colleague's personal details, photo, health, family news or work problems without their permission
- Do not post comments that insult, mock or target colleagues, even as a joke, even in a group you think is private
- Do not use social media to vent about a manager or a team. Use the channels in Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling) [grievance contact]
- Online behaviour towards colleagues is covered by the same rules as behaviour at work. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone

### Clients

- Never criticise a client, their products or their staff in public
- Never share their confidential information, even in a vague way ("one of our big fashion clients had a 3x spike this week" can identify them)
- Do not name a client as a customer until you have confirmation that this is allowed (chapter 4)

### Competitors

- Do not run down other agencies, brands or their people
- Do not post unverified or exaggerated claims about a competitor's quality, prices or behaviour
- If you know something confidential about a competitor because of a previous job or because a client shared it, do not post it
- Compare fairly if you must. Stick to facts you can prove, and ask [marketing lead] first when the comparison appears in official content

### A simple test before posting

- [ ] Is every fact here true and public?
- [ ] Would I be comfortable if the client, the colleague or the competitor read it?
- [ ] Does it avoid client names, numbers, screenshots and unreleased work?
- [ ] Is it clear that this is my view, not the company's?
- [ ] Would it still look fine if shared out of context?

If any answer is no, do not post. Ask [marketing lead] or your manager.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You are annoyed that a client keeps changing the brief and want to rant on X | Do not post. Talk to your project manager. Venting about a client in public, even without naming them, can be traced |
| A colleague posts a funny photo of you at your desk without asking | Ask them to remove it. If they do not, tell your manager or HR [HR contact] |
| A competitor posts a wrong claim about our work | Do not reply online. Send the link to [marketing lead], who will decide if and how to respond |
| You want to say "we are the best SEO agency in India" | Do not. This is a claim you cannot prove, and it can be misleading. Say what you have done, with approved examples |

## 4. Client confidentiality online

Our clients share launches, sales data, ad spend, customer lists and store logins with us. Keeping that private is part of the service. For the detail of what counts as confidential, see Data Privacy and Confidentiality.

### What you must never post without written permission

- Unreleased work: designs, store builds, campaigns, product launches, collection drops, discount plans
- Results and numbers: revenue, conversion rates, ad spend, traffic, return on ad spend, rankings, even rounded or in percentages, unless the client has approved the exact figure
- Screenshots or screen recordings that show client data, store admin pages, ad accounts, analytics, email lists, orders or customer names
- Store names, domains, product names or brand names, if the client has not agreed to be named
- Passwords, API keys, links with access tokens, staging site links
- Anything marked confidential in a contract, a non-disclosure agreement or a brief

### Portfolio and case-study permission

We often want to show our best work. Do it properly.

1. Check the contract for any rights about naming the client or using the work. [Contract owner] holds the contract
2. Ask the client in writing (email is fine) for approval, and say exactly what you want to publish: the text, images and numbers
3. Wait for a written yes. Silence or a verbal "sounds fine" is not approval
4. Keep the written approval in the client folder [shared drive path]
5. Publish only what was approved. If you change the wording or add a number, ask again
6. If the client later asks to remove the case study, remove it promptly and tell [marketing lead]

**Written permission checklist for a case study**

- [ ] Client's name and logo may be used
- [ ] Specific numbers and screenshots approved
- [ ] Quotes approved by the person quoted
- [ ] Any images, fonts or music in the case study are licensed (chapter 12)
- [ ] Approval saved with a date
- [ ] Review date set in case the client changes their mind [every 12 months]

### Clients who do not wish to be public

Some clients prefer not to be named at all. Respect it. You can describe the work without identifying them: "a mid-size apparel store". Be careful, as with enough detail the client can still be identified.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You are proud of a campaign that doubled sales and want to post the numbers | Ask the client in writing for the exact figures and the wording. Until then, post nothing |
| You share a screenshot of a Google Ads dashboard in a LinkedIn post with the account name blurred, but the budget is visible | Remove it. The figures are still confidential. Check with [marketing lead] |
| A client's CEO mentions your work in their own post | You may reshare their public post. Do not add extra figures or detail |
| You are asked on a webinar "who are your clients?" | Name only clients that are publicly listed on our website or approved for use. For others say "I cannot share client names" |
| A past client's store is on your portfolio page but the contract has ended | Check the contract and ask the client. Past work is not automatically free to display |

## 5. Photos and videos at work

Photos make the workplace look lively. They also carry hidden risks: people's faces, screens, whiteboards and locations.

### Consent for people

- Ask before taking a photo or video of a colleague, and ask again before posting it
- At events and in group photos, say you plan to post, and respect anyone who says no
- Never post photos of anyone at a vulnerable moment (unwell, upset, in a rest area)
- Children, visitors and clients' staff need extra care. Get their consent, and for a child, the parent's consent
- If someone asks you to take down a photo of them, do it quickly and without argument

### Screens, whiteboards and documents

- Check the background before sharing: monitors, whiteboards, sticky notes, printouts, laptop screens, calendars
- A whiteboard with a client's launch plan or a sticky note with a password is a leak
- Use a blank wall, or photograph the team away from desks
- For video calls and screenshots, close extra tabs and notification windows before you capture

### Badges and offices

- ID badges, access cards and lanyards can be copied from photos. Hide them or turn them over
- Do not post views that show the office layout, entry points, server or finance rooms, or security details
- Turn off geotags and location sharing for photos taken at the office, a client site or a private event [follow security guidance]

### Locations and travel

- Do not announce in real time that the office, a client site or an event is where you are, if it shares a client's schedule. Post later
- Remove location data from photos before sharing them if you do not want your place to be known
- Do not post "on my way to meet [client name]" or a photo of a client's office or logo on the wall

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A team lunch photo has a whiteboard behind it with a client roadmap | Crop or retake the photo before it goes anywhere |
| You are filming a "day in the life" reel at the office | Ask everyone in frame, hide screens, remove badge details, and get [marketing lead] to approve before posting |
| A colleague asks you to delete a photo from your phone and your story | Do it at once. Do not ask them to justify it |
| You photograph a client's product shipment at a warehouse visit | Do not post. Ask the client first and check for logos, stock details and addresses |

## 6. Representing the company in public

Whenever you speak, write or appear in public as a professional, people link your words to the company. This covers LinkedIn posts, talks, podcasts, webinars, conferences, panels, interviews and even replies to recruiters.

### LinkedIn posts and thought leadership

- Share what you have learned, not what clients told you in confidence
- Use general lessons instead of client specifics: "stores with slow mobile pages lose shoppers" rather than naming a store
- Do not post about hiring, firing, layoffs, funding, prices or salaries unless [communications lead] approved
- Be honest about results. Do not exaggerate or use data you cannot show
- Tag colleagues or clients only with their agreement

### Talks, podcasts, webinars and conferences

Before you accept an invitation:

1. Tell your manager and [marketing lead]
2. Say whether you will speak as yourself or for the company. If for the company, get approval for the topic, slides and speaker
3. Share your slides or talking points in advance with [reviewer] if client examples are included
4. Ask who will record the session, where it will be posted and for how long
5. After the event, check recordings and posts for anything you should correct

**Before you go on stage**

- [ ] Slides contain no unapproved client names, numbers or screenshots
- [ ] No internal documents, chats or dashboards are shown
- [ ] Images, fonts and music are licensed or free to use
- [ ] I know what I may not say, such as pricing, plans and client issues
- [ ] I have a line ready for questions I cannot answer: "I am not able to share that, but I can point you to..."

### Interviews

If a journalist, blogger or podcaster contacts you about the company or a client, follow chapter 7. If they contact you only about your own field and not about the company, still tell [marketing lead] before you say yes.

### Answering recruiters and surveys

- Recruiters from other companies may message you. You are free to respond about your own career, but do not share our salary bands, client names or internal team details
- Do not give out a colleague's contact details, even to be helpful
- Third-party surveys, "expert networks" and paid research calls often ask about clients and market data. Do not take part if they would require confidential information. Ask your manager first

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You are invited to speak at an e-commerce conference | Tell your manager and [marketing lead]. Agree the topic and what you can show before you accept |
| A podcast host asks about "the biggest store you have ever built" | Say you cannot name clients, and speak about the lessons in general terms |
| A recruiter asks about our pay and client list | Politely decline: "I cannot share that. I am happy to talk about my own experience" |
| You publish an article about SEO tips with a client's screenshot | Do not post it. Remove the screenshot, or get written approval from the client |

## 7. Media and press enquiries

When a journalist, reporter or blogger contacts you about the company, the stakes are higher. A single sentence can become a headline.

### The rule

**Do not answer. Pass it on.** Only [spokesperson name and role] may speak to the media for the company. This applies to calls, emails, direct messages, comments, and approaches at events.

### What to do

1. Stay calm and polite. Do not say "no comment" in a sharp way, as it can read as hiding something
2. Do not confirm or deny anything, including whether a person works here, whether a client is ours, or whether an event happened
3. Take the journalist's name, outlet, phone or email, deadline and question
4. Say a short line (below)
5. Send all details immediately to [spokesperson email] and [your manager]
6. Do not post about the enquiry online

**A short script**

> "Thank you for reaching out. I am not the right person to comment, but I will pass your request to our spokesperson, [name], who will get back to you. May I take your name, outlet and deadline?"

### If you are called at home or at an event

The same script applies. If the journalist keeps pushing, repeat the line and end politely: "I am sorry, I really cannot comment."

### If the enquiry is about a client

Do not discuss the client at all. Tell the journalist it is a matter for the spokesperson, and also let your project manager know so the client can be informed.

### If the enquiry is about you personally

If a journalist asks about your own life or your views, you are free to decide, but tell [marketing lead] first. Make clear you are speaking for yourself, and do not mention the company or clients.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A reporter calls the office and asks for details of a data issue at a client | Use the script, note the details, and alert [spokesperson] and your manager at once |
| A blogger DMs you on Instagram asking for a quote on "Shopify trends" | Pass to [marketing lead]. Do not reply with a quote on behalf of the company |
| A journalist you know personally asks off the record about a layoff rumour | Do not engage. There is no such thing as off the record. Pass it on |
| You are approached by a TV channel for a discount-season comment | Take the details and send to [spokesperson email]. Do not agree to anything |

## 8. When something goes wrong online

Sometimes a customer or client complains publicly, a post goes viral for the wrong reasons, an ad shows an error, or the company is named in a rumour. This chapter helps you act calmly in the first hour.

### First hour: the basics

1. **Stop posting.** Pause scheduled posts and ads on the affected account if you are authorised to do so
2. **Take screenshots and save links** as evidence. Note the time
3. **Do not reply emotionally or delete in panic.** Deleting without a record can look like hiding and cause trouble later
4. **Tell the right people at once:** your manager, [marketing lead] and, for a client matter, the project manager. Use [urgent channel]
5. **Wait for the plan.** One person leads the response, and others do not reply publicly

### Who does what

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| First person to notice | Capture evidence and alert the team |
| Account manager or project manager | Inform the client and agree the response with them |
| [Marketing lead] | Draft the public response, check facts and tone |
| [Spokesperson] | Speak for the company if media gets involved |
| [CEO or senior leader] | Approve serious statements, legal matters, major apologies |
| [Legal adviser] | Advise on defamation, data leaks, or complaints that may be legal |

### A good public reply has

- Acknowledgement: "We are sorry to hear this."
- A fact, only if confirmed
- A next step: "Please DM us your order ID so we can help."
- A calm, human tone, with no blame and no argument

### Client or customer complains publicly

- Respond only if the account is yours to manage, and use the agreed approach
- If it is a client's customer complaining about the client's store, do not answer for the client without their approval
- If a client complains about us in public, do not reply online. Escalate to [account lead] and call the client
- Move the conversation to a private channel as soon as it is safe to do so

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| An ad for a client carried the wrong price and people are commenting | Pause the ad, screenshot, alert the project manager and the client, and agree a correction notice |
| A customer posts angrily on a client's Instagram: "my order never came" | Reply with a polite holding line approved by the client, and ask them to DM. Pass details to the client's support |
| A client posts on LinkedIn that "our agency missed the deadline" | Do not reply. Alert [account lead] and [CEO]. Call the client promptly |
| A rumour spreads that the company is closing | Do not reply. Alert [communications lead]. The spokesperson will respond with facts |

## 9. Negative comments, trolls and online abuse

Not all criticism is a problem, and not all of it is abuse. It helps to tell the difference.

| Type | What it looks like | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fair criticism | "The delivery was late." | Respond politely and fix the issue |
| Negative review | "Bad product." | Acknowledge, ask for details privately |
| Troll | Provocative comments meant to trigger a reaction | Do not feed. Hide or report per platform rules, and keep evidence |
| Spam or fake | Repeated links, bots, fake accounts | Report and block |
| Abuse or harassment | Insults, threats, hate, targeted harassment | Document, report, escalate. Never respond in kind |

### For official or client accounts

- Reply to genuine questions, not to bait
- Keep a short list of approved reply templates [community response guide]
- Hide or delete only comments that break platform rules or the account's published rules (abuse, hate, spam). Do not delete simply because it is negative. Save a screenshot first
- Escalate threats of violence, self-harm or legal action at once to [marketing lead] and your manager

### If you are the target

Abuse is not part of the job. If you are targeted online because of your work or because of who you are:

1. Do not reply. Take screenshots with dates and links
2. Report the content to the platform and block the accounts
3. Tell your manager or [HR contact]. You do not have to cope alone
4. Look after yourself. Take a break from the account, and ask for another person to monitor it [wellbeing support]
5. If there are threats, tell [security or HR contact]. You can also approach the police, and [HR or legal contact] can help. Cyber crime can be reported on the national cyber crime reporting portal and helpline [confirm current details]

### Harassment of colleagues online

Messages, comments, group chats, memes or posts that harass a colleague are workplace conduct matters, even on personal accounts, when they are connected to work. Sexual or sexualised comments, unwanted messages, stalking or sharing intimate images are serious. They are covered by POSH Awareness for Everyone, which explains how to report and how a complaint is handled. If you see it, support the person, keep evidence and tell [IC contact]. Do not forward the material to others.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A stranger sends rude DMs to a colleague who manages a brand page | Do not reply. Screenshot, block, report, and tell the manager |
| A troll on a client's page keeps posting abusive replies | Screenshot, hide or report per platform rules, and inform the client |
| A colleague posts a sexual joke about another colleague in a team group | Tell the colleague to stop if safe, keep evidence, and report to [IC contact]. See POSH Awareness for Everyone |
| A reviewer demands a refund in an angry, abusive tone | Stay polite, reply once with a professional line, offer a private channel, and escalate if it continues |

## 10. Employee advocacy: being a good ambassador

Employee advocacy means sharing the company's news and culture in your own words. Done well, it helps hiring, trust and growth. It is always voluntary.

### Why it helps

- People trust posts from people more than from brand pages
- Real stories attract better candidates
- It shows clients that our team is skilled and engaged

### How to do it well

- Share official posts with a short, honest comment of your own
- Write in your own voice. Avoid copy-pasting corporate lines
- Keep it truthful. Do not exaggerate or claim credit that belongs to the team
- Mention colleagues and clients only with consent
- Follow the sharing guide [employee advocacy guide]
- Do not pressure colleagues to like, share or comment. Taking part is a choice

### What does not count as advocacy

- Posting fake positive reviews or comments about the company
- Asking friends or family to leave reviews as if they were clients
- Using bots, pods or paid likes to boost posts
- Being rewarded by a company in return for glowing posts without saying so (see disclosure in chapter 13)

### Good-citizen habits

- Congratulate others, share useful content and answer questions kindly
- Do not argue with critics under the company's posts. Pass concerns on to [marketing lead]
- Update your profile when your role changes, and remove the company name when you leave

## 11. Politics, religion and sensitive topics

Our company works with clients and colleagues of many beliefs, backgrounds and political views. Everyone has the right to hold opinions. The question is how they are shared.

### Principles

- **Personal views are personal.** You are free to hold and express views in your own time. Make it clear they are yours, and do not use the company name, logo, email or position to give them weight
- **Do not imply the company's backing.** If your profile lists the company, add a clear line that views are your own, and avoid posting about hot topics in a way that links them with us
- **Do not use official accounts.** Company and client accounts never carry political or religious messages, unless the leadership and client have agreed in writing
- **Respect colleagues.** Do not mock, target or attack colleagues because of their religion, caste, region, language, gender, community, sexuality, disability or politics. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone
- **Do not campaign through work channels.** Do not use work email, team chat or client accounts to push political or religious campaigns, or to collect donations
- **Be careful during sensitive events.** In times of tension, elections or disaster, schedule-check client posts. A cheerful sale post can look insensitive at the wrong moment. Ask [marketing lead] to pause or review

### Content for clients on sensitive topics

If a client asks for a post about a festival, political event, social cause or news event, check with [marketing lead] and the client. Jokes, stereotypes, and "trending" hashtags can offend. Check why a hashtag is trending before using it.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You want to post your opinion on an election. Your profile mentions the company | Add a clear "views are my own" line, keep it respectful, do not tag the company or clients, and consider whether a work profile is the right place |
| A colleague argues in a group chat about religion and others are uncomfortable | Ask to move on. If it continues, tell your manager or [HR contact] |
| A client wants a sale post themed on a sensitive national event | Raise the risk with the client and [marketing lead]. Suggest a more suitable idea or timing |
| A trending hashtag looks fun for a scheduled brand post | Check its meaning first. Do not use it until you know why it is trending |

## 12. Copyright, image rights, music and misleading statements

### Copyright

Most images, videos, music, fonts, articles and designs belong to someone. Finding something on the internet does not mean you may use it. Copyright law in India protects creative works. The detail is complex [confirm with a legal adviser], so follow these habits.

- Use images, music and fonts that we have a licence for, that the client supplied with permission, or that come from a source with clear free-to-use terms. Keep the licence details [asset licence folder]
- Do not use "screenshot from Google Images" or "found it on Pinterest" for client work
- Credit creators when the licence requires it
- Check whether the licence allows commercial use, because client posts are commercial
- AI-generated images and text may carry their own rules. Follow Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools for Everyone

### Music in posts

- Music in reels, stories and videos is often licensed only for personal use on the platform
- A popular song used in a brand's or client's promotional post may not be allowed. Platforms usually offer a separate library of music cleared for business accounts [check each platform]
- If you are unsure, use music from a licensed library, or ask [marketing lead]
- Do not rip audio from videos

### Image rights and people

- A person's photo or voice should not be used in promotion without their consent
- Do not use a celebrity's, influencer's or a customer's picture in client ads without a written agreement
- Stock photos of people have usage terms. Read them, for example whether they forbid use for sensitive subjects

### Defamation and misleading statements, in general terms

Defamation is, broadly, a false statement that harms someone's reputation. Both people and businesses can be affected. In India, it can be raised as a civil claim and in some cases criminal law may be relevant [confirm with a legal adviser]. This module does not give legal advice.

**To stay safe**

- State facts you can prove. Avoid guesses, rumours and accusations
- Separate opinion from fact: "I found the checkout confusing" is different from "this store is a scam"
- Do not accuse a named person or business of fraud, theft or illegal conduct online
- If you are not sure, do not post. Ask [marketing lead] or [legal adviser]
- Sharing or retweeting a false claim can carry the same risk as writing it

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You find a perfect image on a blog and want to use it in a client's post | Do not use it. Find the owner for a licence, or use a licensed stock image |
| A teammate wants to use a trending film song for a client's sale reel | Check the platform's business music rules. Use cleared music if unsure |
| You want to post that a rival store sells "fake products" | Do not post. Without proof it could be defamatory. Raise concerns with [marketing lead] |
| A client gives you photos of their customers and says "post them" | Ask for written proof that the customers agreed to be used in promotion |

## 13. Marketing and advertising rules for client content

Much of our work is publishing on behalf of clients: posts, ads, emails, influencer posts, product descriptions and reviews. We must be honest, because misleading consumers harms people and can lead to legal trouble for the client and for us. This is general information. Rules change, so [confirm with a legal adviser] for the current position.

### Core principles

- **Honest claims.** Say only what is true and can be shown. If you say "clinically proven", "number 1", "fastest delivery" or "100% natural", be ready with proof
- **No hiding the catch.** If an offer has conditions, state them clearly, not in tiny text. A "70 percent off" claim that applies to one item is misleading
- **No fake reviews or fake testimonials.** Never write, buy, trade or post reviews pretending to be real customers. Never invent a testimonial or a customer photo. Never delete genuine negative reviews to improve ratings, unless they break platform rules
- **Real before-and-after.** Show results that are real and typical, or label them clearly as examples
- **Clear pricing.** Prices, discounts and delivery fees should not be misleading
- **Respect for the audience.** Take care with content aimed at children, and with claims on health, finance or safety

### Influencer and sponsored content

When someone is paid, given free products, or otherwise rewarded to post, the audience must be told clearly.

- The Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) publishes guidelines for influencer advertising that ask for clear disclosure of paid or gifted content [confirm current ASCI guidance]. Disclosure should be upfront and easy to notice, using words such as "Ad", "Sponsored" or "Paid partnership"
- Do not bury the label in a long list of hashtags or leave it in a place few people will see
- Use the platform's paid-partnership tool where available
- The consumer protection law in India covers misleading advertisements and unfair trade practices, and the regulator can take action [confirm with a legal adviser]
- Both the brand and the person posting can have duties, so our team should brief influencers and check their posts before they go live
- Keep written briefs, agreements and proof of any claims in the client folder

### Before you publish a client ad or sponsored post

- [ ] Every claim is true and we hold proof
- [ ] Offer terms, dates and exclusions are visible
- [ ] No fake or purchased reviews or testimonials
- [ ] Sponsored or gifted content is clearly labelled
- [ ] Images, music and fonts are licensed
- [ ] The client has approved the final version in writing
- [ ] We can pause or correct it quickly if needed

### If a client asks for something misleading

Do not simply comply, and do not simply refuse. Explain the risk calmly, offer an honest alternative, and tell your manager. If the client insists, escalate to [account lead] and [legal adviser]. You are never required to publish something you believe is unlawful or untrue. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A client asks you to post five "customer reviews" you write yourself | Decline politely. Offer to run a genuine review campaign instead |
| An influencer posts a reel about a client's product with the label hidden under 20 hashtags | Ask them to move the disclosure to the top or use the paid-partnership label, and keep a record |
| A client wants "guaranteed results in 7 days" for an SEO service | Say that no one can guarantee search results. Offer realistic wording the client can stand behind |
| A product description says "cures acne" for a cosmetic item | Stop. Health claims are risky. Ask the client for proof and ask [legal adviser] before publishing |
| A client asks you to delete all one-star reviews on their page | Explain that you can report reviews that break the platform rules, but honest negative reviews should stay. Reply to them instead |

## 14. Account security, AI drafting, monitoring and reporting mistakes

### Privacy and security of accounts

Accounts are the front door to a brand. A hacked page can post scams in the client's name. Cyber Security Basics for Everyone covers the detail. For social accounts:

- **Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) on every shared brand account.** No exceptions
- Use an approved password manager [approved password manager], not chats or spreadsheets
- Keep an **admin access list** for each account: who has access, what role, and when it was last checked [shared drive path]. Use the lowest role that does the job
- Never share passwords. Use platform roles, such as business manager or page roles, so each person has their own login
- **Offboarding:** when someone leaves a project or the company, remove their access the same day, change shared passwords and recovery details, and update the list. [Account owner] does a monthly access review
- Do not log in on personal or public devices
- Be careful with links, "copyright violation" warnings, "verify your page" emails and fake support accounts. These are common scams

### Using AI to draft posts

AI tools can help with ideas and first drafts. They can also invent facts, copy phrases and leak data.

- Use only approved tools [approved AI tools list]. See Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools for Everyone
- Never paste confidential client data, unreleased details or personal data into an AI tool unless it is approved for that use
- Check every fact, name, number and claim. AI can sound confident and be wrong
- Rewrite in the client's voice, and check for copied phrases
- Tell the client if they have asked for disclosure of AI use, and follow any contract terms on AI-generated content

### Monitoring, deleting and correcting posts

- Official accounts should be watched for comments and messages during the hours agreed [monitoring hours]
- If a post has an error, tell the owner quickly and choose between correcting, editing or removing it
- For a small error, such as a typo, a short correction or edit is enough. Say what was changed if it affects meaning
- For a serious error, such as a wrong price, a confidential leak or an offensive line, remove it first if you are authorised, then inform the client and your manager
- Keep a screenshot and a short note of what was posted, when it was removed and why. Deleting does not erase screenshots others have taken
- Do not secretly edit or delete in a way that hides a mistake from the client

### Reporting a mistake quickly

Report at once, to [urgent channel] and your manager, if:

- You posted from the wrong account, such as a personal post on a client page
- You posted client information, a password, a screenshot or an unreleased detail
- A post or ad contains a wrong claim, price or offensive content
- Someone impersonates the company or a staff member, or a fake profile appears
- An account is hacked, locked or posting things you did not post
- You received a suspicious message about an account

**Mistakes happen. Hiding them makes them worse.** You will not be blamed for reporting quickly and honestly [confirm with your policy]. See Cyber Security Basics for Everyone for the full incident steps.

### Impersonation and fake profiles

1. Take screenshots, including the profile link and date
2. Do not contact or argue with the fake profile
3. Report it to the platform using its impersonation form
4. Tell [marketing lead] and [IT contact], and warn colleagues and clients if the fake profile contacts them
5. If money or data is at risk, tell [security contact] immediately

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You posted a personal photo to a client's Instagram page by mistake | Delete it, screenshot it, and tell your manager and the client at once |
| A colleague who left the company still has admin rights on a client page | Tell [account owner] now. Remove access and update the list |
| A fake page uses our logo and offers jobs for a fee | Report it to the platform, tell [marketing lead] and [HR contact], and warn candidates on official channels |
| An AI tool wrote a client post with a made-up statistic | Do not publish it. Find a real source or remove the claim |
| You get an email saying the client's page will be deleted unless you click a link | Do not click. Report it to [IT contact] as a phishing attempt |

## 15. Employee questions and sample answers

These are common questions with practical answers. For anything not covered, ask [social media policy owner].

**1. Can I say on LinkedIn that I work at the company?**
Yes. It is normal and helpful. Use your real job title, and keep your posts honest. Remember that things you write may be read as reflecting on the company.

**2. Do I need approval before posting about the company on my personal account?**
Not for sharing public company posts. For anything new, such as announcements, client work, numbers or internal news, check with [marketing lead] first.

**3. Can I post a photo of my desk?**
Yes, once you check the screen, notes, whiteboard, badge and anything with client names. When in doubt, tidy up, clear the screen or take the photo elsewhere.

**4. A client's product launch is tomorrow and I am excited. Can I hint at it?**
No. Even a hint can break the client's plan and our agreement. Wait until the client has launched, and share only their public posts.

**5. Can I show my work as a freelancer or employee in my own portfolio?**
Only with written permission from the client and, where relevant, the company. Many contracts limit it. Ask your manager and [contract owner].

**6. Who can post on the company's official page?**
Only the people named by [marketing lead]. If you have a good idea, send it to them.

**7. A journalist asked me a question about the company. What do I do?**
Do not answer. Use the short script in chapter 7, take their details and send them to [spokesperson email] straight away.

**8. Can I comment on a competitor's post?**
Yes, if you are polite and factual and you make clear you speak for yourself. Never insult them or share anything confidential.

**9. Is it all right to share my political views online?**
You are free to hold views. Keep them personal, respectful and away from official and client accounts, and do not suggest the company agrees.

**10. Can I use a trending song in a client reel?**
Only if the platform allows it for business accounts or you hold a licence. Personal-use music may not be allowed for brands. Ask [marketing lead] if you are unsure.

**11. A customer is shouting at a client's brand in the comments. Should I delete it?**
Not unless it breaks platform rules or the account's published rules. For genuine complaints, reply politely and move to a private channel. Keep a screenshot of anything you hide or delete.

**12. Can I ask friends to leave five-star reviews for a client's store?**
No. Reviews should come from real customers with real experience. Fake or arranged reviews are misleading and can cause legal problems.

**13. What must I do when an influencer posts about a client's product?**
Make sure the post is clearly labelled as paid or gifted, and the claims are true. Keep the brief and approvals in the client folder.

**14. I posted something by mistake on a client's page. What now?**
Remove it if you can, take a screenshot, and tell your manager and the client right away. Quick honesty is respected.

**15. Someone is abusing me online because of my job. Is there support?**
Yes. Keep evidence, do not reply, report and block, and tell your manager or [HR contact]. Abuse that is sexual or targets you at work can also go to [IC contact].

**16. Can I use AI to write social posts?**
Yes, with approved tools and without confidential data, and always check facts and wording yourself. See Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools for Everyone.

**17. Do I have to share company posts on my profile?**
No. Advocacy is voluntary. If you do share, be honest and use your own words.

**18. A past colleague pretends to be our recruiter and messages candidates. What do I do?**
Take screenshots, tell [HR contact] and [marketing lead], and report the profile. Do not engage with it.

**19. Can I invite a client to connect on my personal profile?**
You can, but remember what you post is visible to them. For sensitive discussions, use approved channels and not private messages.

**20. I deleted a post after I realised it was a mistake. Do I still need to tell anyone?**
Yes. Others may have already seen or saved it. Tell your manager so the right people can check for harm.

**21. A client wants me to say their product is "the best in India". May I?**
Only if it can be proven. Otherwise suggest honest claims, such as specific features, awards or customer ratings that are real.

**22. Is it fine to take part in an online poll or expert call with a research firm?**
Only if you can answer without sharing client, pricing or internal information. Tell your manager first.

## 16. Glossary

**Glossary**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Admin access list | A record of who can log in to a shared account, with what role |
| Advocacy | Voluntarily sharing and supporting the company's news in your own words |

| Brand account | An account that represents a company or client brand, not a person |
| Case study | A write-up of client work, published only with written permission |

| Crisis communication | How an organisation responds publicly when something goes seriously wrong |
| Defamation | A false statement that harms a person's or business's reputation |
| Disclosure | A clear label showing that content is paid, gifted or sponsored |
| Geotag | Location data attached to a post or photo |
| Impersonation | Pretending to be a person or company, for example with a fake profile |
| Influencer | A person with an online following who promotes products or brands |
| Multi-factor authentication (MFA) | A second proof of identity, besides a password, when logging in |
| Offboarding | Removing access and handing over duties when someone leaves |
| Official account | An account authorised to speak for the company |
| Paid partnership | A platform label showing that a post has been sponsored |
| Personal account | Your own account, where your views are yours |
| Phishing | A fake message designed to trick you into giving access or data |
| Spokesperson | The person authorised to speak to the media for the company |
| Testimonial | A statement from a real customer about their experience |
| Troll | A person who posts provocative comments to trigger a reaction |
| Unreleased work | Anything not yet public, such as designs, campaigns or launches |
| Written permission | Approval in writing, such as email, which is saved |

# Code of Conduct and Ethics

This module is for everyone in the organisation: developers, designers, marketers, writers, project managers, finance, admin, sales, leaders, interns and freelancers. It explains the standards of behaviour we expect from each other and from ourselves, from respect and honesty to fair dealing, honest reporting, ethical marketing, intellectual property and how to raise a concern. It is written for non-specialists in plain language and uses examples from a digital agency that builds and grows online stores. It is general information and does not replace the organisation's own written Code of Conduct or policies.

## 1. Why a code of conduct matters

A code of conduct is a shared promise about how we treat each other, our clients and the public. It does not try to cover every situation. It gives us a common starting point so that when something is unclear, a new joiner, a founder and a freelancer would all reach a similar answer.

For an agency, trust is the product. Clients hand us their store, their ad budget, their customer data and their brand. They cannot check every line of code or every rupee of spend. They rely on us being honest about what we did and what it achieved. One inflated report or one careless shortcut can cost an account that took years to win.

**This module is for all of us because**

- Everyone represents the company, in meetings, in team chats, on social media and at events
- Most ethical problems are small and everyday: a rounded-up timesheet, a screenshot cropped to look better, a "yes" given to avoid an awkward talk
- Nobody can be expected to guess the rules, so they need to be written in plain words
- Good people sometimes make poor choices under pressure, and a clear code makes the right choice easier

**What this module will help you do**

- Understand the values and standards we hold ourselves to
- Behave respectfully and professionally at work, online and at events
- Report time, work and results honestly, even when the news is not good
- Deal fairly with clients, colleagues, vendors and competitors
- Market honestly and respect other people's intellectual property
- Make a sound decision when no rule fits, using a short set of questions
- Raise a concern safely, and know what happens next

**The three rules to remember**

1. **Be honest, especially when it is uncomfortable.** Bad news told early is a problem. Bad news hidden is a breach of trust.
2. **Treat people the way you would want to be treated in front of your own family.** Respect applies to colleagues, clients, vendors, candidates and strangers online.
3. **When unsure, ask before you act.** Asking is never a mistake. [Your manager or HR contact] would always prefer a question to an apology.

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 4 once, as everyone needs them. Chapters 5 to 12 cover specific areas, so read the ones that match your work first and then the rest. Chapter 13 gives you a method for hard decisions, and chapter 14 explains how to raise concerns and how breaches are handled. Managers should read chapter 15 as well. Use the questions in chapter 16 and the glossary in chapter 17 for quick reference. Where this module points to another course, such as "POSH Awareness for Everyone", go to that course for the full detail.

### Who the code applies to

The code applies to every person working for or with the company:

- Employees at every level, including founders and directors
- Interns, trainees and apprentices
- Freelancers and contractors, whenever they work on our projects or represent us
- Anyone acting on our behalf, such as a reseller or a referral partner [confirm how the contract applies this]

It applies wherever the work happens: in the office, at home, at a client site, in a team chat, on a call, at an event, and on social media where it connects to work or to the company.

### Standards, not just rules

A rule says what you must not do. A standard says what good looks like. The code uses both.

**Rule example:** Do not use pirated software on company laptops.

**Standard example:** If you are not sure that a tool, font or image is properly licensed, check before you use it on a client project.

Rules protect the minimum. Standards are what make people want to work with us.

### Why legal compliance is not enough

Something can be legal and still be wrong for us. Telling a client that "traffic is up" when you know the increase came from a one-off spike is probably not illegal. It is still misleading. Our standard is higher than "can we get away with it": it is "would we be comfortable if the client saw exactly what we did and why".

## 3. Respect and professional behaviour at work, online and at events

Respect is the foundation of every other part of the code. It is simple to describe and harder to practise when deadlines are tight and a client is unhappy.

### Respect in daily work

**Do**

- Greet, listen and give credit to others, whatever their role
- Disagree with ideas, not people, and keep feedback specific and kind
- Respect people's time: arrive on time for calls, give notice if you cannot make it, and respect agreed working hours
- Respond politely to clients even when they are rude, and escalate to your manager if the behaviour continues
- Respect differences of gender, age, caste, religion, region, language, disability, background and belief

**Do not**

- Shout, swear at, belittle or humiliate anyone
- Gossip about colleagues or share private details about them
- Make jokes about someone's accent, appearance, region, religion, community or family
- Exclude people from work conversations because of who they are
- Take credit for someone else's work

### What bullying and harassment look like

Bullying is repeated behaviour that makes a person feel small, afraid or unwelcome. It does not have to be loud. In an agency it often looks like:

- Constant public criticism of one person's work in the team chat
- Giving someone impossible deadlines, or no work at all, as a punishment
- Leaving someone out of calls and then blaming them for missing information
- Sarcastic comments or "jokes" that everyone laughs at except the target
- Messages late at night or on holidays demanding instant replies, as a pattern

Occasional firm feedback is not bullying. A clear, respectful "this needs to change, and here is why" is part of good management. The difference is intent, tone, fairness and pattern.

Sexual harassment has its own law and its own process. This module does not cover it in detail. See **POSH Awareness for Everyone** for what it is, your rights and the Internal Committee process. Discrimination and bullying are covered by the code in this module, and you can raise them through **Speaking Up: Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling for Everyone**.

### Respect online

The workplace now includes team chats, video calls, email, project tools and social media. Our standards apply in all of them.

| Situation | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Team chat | Keep it civil and professional. Think before you post screenshots, memes or reactions that could hurt someone |
| Video calls | Be on time, camera on where agreed, and do not mock or talk over others |
| Email to clients | Clear, polite and factual. Avoid sarcasm and blame |
| Social media | Do not post about clients, colleagues or work disputes in a way that could embarrass anyone. See **Social Media and Public Communication** |
| Private messages | Work-related private messages are still covered. Do not send content you would not say face to face |

A useful test: would you be comfortable if this message were read out in a team meeting, or shown to the client? If not, do not send it.

### Conduct at work events and off-site

Team outings, client dinners, offsites, festival celebrations, conferences and online parties are all "work" for the purposes of this code, even when they feel casual.

**Do**

- Treat colleagues, clients and venue staff with the same respect as in the office
- Make participation optional and inclusive: some people do not drink alcohol, eat certain foods, or are unable to travel late for personal, religious or family reasons
- Arrange safe travel, especially late at night [see the Health, Safety and Wellbeing course and the company travel arrangements]
- Keep it light: no pressure to drink, to join in dares, or to share personal details
- Leave an event or a conversation if you are uncomfortable, and tell [HR contact] if something happened

**Do not**

- Pressure anyone to drink alcohol or consume anything. If alcohol is served [at the company's discretion], moderation is expected
- Use the event as a reason to make unwelcome comments, jokes or advances
- Drive after drinking, or let a colleague do so
- Post photos or videos of colleagues without their consent
- Behave in a way that would embarrass the company in front of a client or the public

**Worked example.** At the annual offsite, a senior colleague keeps urging an intern to have "just one drink" and teases them when they decline. This is peer pressure. The intern may say a polite no, and others who notice can step in ("Leave it, they are fine with juice"). Afterwards, the matter can be raised with [HR contact] or the manager. A senior person's role carries extra responsibility to set the tone.

## 4. Honesty and integrity

Integrity means your words and actions match, even when nobody is checking. In an agency, much of our work cannot be easily verified by clients, which makes personal honesty the real control.

### What honesty means in practice

- Tell the truth about what you did, how long it took, what it cost and what happened as a result
- Do not make promises you do not intend or are unable to keep
- Admit mistakes early. A missed deadline, a broken checkout or a wrongly paused ad is far easier to fix when reported quickly
- Do not blame others for your errors, and do not take credit for theirs
- Do not mislead by omission: leaving out a fact that changes the picture is still misleading

### Small lies add up

Most dishonesty at work does not feel like dishonesty. It looks like these:

| What it looks like | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| "It is almost done" when work has not started | The client plans around false information |
| Saying "I sent it yesterday" when you did not | Trust in your word drops, for everyone |
| Marking a task "complete" when it is only partly done | Hidden gaps appear later, at a worse time |
| Blaming "the client did not reply" when you did not chase | The client is treated unfairly |
| Agreeing in a meeting and quietly doing something else | Colleagues cannot rely on decisions |

### Owning mistakes

Everyone makes mistakes. We expect mistakes to be reported and handled, not hidden. A good response has four steps:

1. **Say it quickly.** Tell your manager or lead as soon as you notice.
2. **State the facts.** What happened, what is affected, what you know and do not know yet.
3. **Contain it.** Stop the harm where you can: pause the ad, roll back the change, restore from backup.
4. **Learn from it.** Agree what will stop it happening again.

Honest reporting of a mistake is treated far more kindly than concealment. Concealing a mistake that harms a client is a serious breach.

### Honesty with the company

- Give accurate information on your CV, application and background documents. Do not claim experience or qualifications you do not have
- Be honest in expense claims, leave requests and reimbursements. See **Expense and Travel Policy**
- Tell the company about relevant matters as required, such as a conflict of interest or a personal interest in a vendor, and tell [your manager or compliance contact]
- Cooperate honestly with any review, audit or investigation

## 5. Honest reporting and records in an agency

Agencies sell hours, expertise and results. That makes our records, timesheets, status updates and reports the most important evidence of what we did. Clients pay based on them, and we make decisions from them. Inaccurate records are not a minor admin issue. They are a core ethical risk.

### Timesheets and time records

- Record the time actually worked, on the right project and task
- Do not move hours between projects to hide an overrun, to stay within a budget, or to look busy
- Do not log time for work you did not do, or for personal tasks, long breaks or idle time
- If you worked on something that overran, say so. Your manager would rather know the true cost of a task than find out at invoicing
- Correct errors promptly and tell [your manager or finance contact] if a past timesheet needs fixing

**Worked example.** A developer spends 14 hours on a Shopify theme fix that was estimated at 6. Logging 6 hours on the project and 8 hours as "internal" hides the problem. The honest approach is to log 14, explain why (an unexpected app conflict), and let the project manager decide how to handle it with the client. The estimate can then be improved next time.

### Status reports

A status report should help the client and the team make good decisions. It should be accurate, balanced and timely.

**Do**

- Report real progress: done, in progress, blocked, at risk
- Flag delays and risks as soon as you know about them, along with a proposed plan
- Distinguish facts from guesses: "I expect to finish by Friday, depending on the client's feedback"
- Say "I do not know yet" when it is true

**Do not**

- Mark things "green" when you know they are not
- Hide a blocker in the hope it will resolve itself
- Use vague language ("working on it") to avoid giving a clear answer
- Describe work as live or tested when it is not

### Ad, SEO and analytics results

Results reporting is where honesty is tested most, because the numbers affect budgets and contracts.

**Never**

- Inflate results, round them favourably beyond reason, or change dates or filters to make a month look better
- Report only the good metrics and leave out those that tell a different story (for example, reporting clicks while hiding a rise in cost per purchase)
- Claim credit for results that came from something else, such as a festival sale, a viral post or a brand campaign running elsewhere
- Present forecasts or targets as achieved results
- Edit screenshots or exports, or fabricate data
- Hide a tracking fault or a billing mistake from the client

**Always**

- Show the source of the data and the date range
- Explain changes honestly, including when the cause is uncertain
- Report negative results and what you plan to do about them
- Correct earlier reports as soon as you find a mistake, and tell the client why

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Sales fell 20 percent this month and the client is nervous | Report it plainly with possible reasons (stock-outs, a competitor's sale, tracking changes) and a plan. Do not reshape the report to hide it |
| You find that conversion tracking was double-counting last month | Tell your manager now, correct the numbers, and let the client know the corrected figures and how it is fixed |
| A manager asks you to leave out a metric that makes the campaign look poor | Politely ask that the full picture be shared. If pressed, raise it with [senior manager or Speaking Up channel] |
| An SEO ranking jumped due to a tracking tool glitch | Check before you celebrate. Do not report a ranking you have not verified |

### Honest estimates and promises

An estimate is our best honest judgement, not a bid to win the job.

- Estimate based on the actual scope, including testing, feedback rounds and handover
- Do not lowball an estimate to win work and plan to recover the cost through change requests
- State assumptions and what could change the estimate
- Do not guarantee outcomes that depend on things we cannot control, such as search rankings, ad performance or customer behaviour
- If the estimate is wrong, say so early and explain what has changed

**Worked example.** A prospective client asks, "Can you guarantee page one for these keywords in three months?" An honest answer explains what we will do, what influences the result, what we can report on, and that nobody can guarantee rankings. A guarantee may win the deal but breaks trust (and may break advertising rules) later.

### Keeping records

- Keep project records (briefs, approvals, change requests, reports) in the [approved project tool or shared drive], not only in personal chats
- Do not alter or delete records to hide an error, a delay or a dispute
- Keep records for the period set by the company and contract [retention period, confirm with finance and legal]. See **Data Privacy and Confidentiality for Everyone** for personal data in records
- If asked to preserve documents because of a dispute or review, do not delete anything until [legal or HR contact] tells you it is fine

## 6. Fair dealing with clients, colleagues, vendors and competitors

Fair dealing means treating the people and organisations we work with honestly and on merit. It protects us from favouritism, unfair advantage and disputes.

### Clients

- Describe our services, team and track record accurately. Do not exaggerate experience or credentials, or list clients as references without permission
- Tell clients early about changes in scope, cost, timeline or team
- Do not take advantage of a client's lack of technical knowledge, for example by recommending services they do not need
- Do not bill for work not done, or for more time than was spent
- Put the client's genuine interest in your advice, even when it means recommending less work
- Handle client complaints politely and on time, and escalate quickly if you cannot resolve them

### Colleagues

- Share credit, information and opportunities fairly
- Do not undermine colleagues in front of clients, or spread rumours about them
- Make hiring, promotion and assignment decisions on merit, not on friendship or family ties
- Respect other teams' time and resources, and do not "borrow" people without asking their manager

### Vendors, freelancers and partners

- Select vendors on quality, price, reliability and fit, not on friendship, personal benefit or gifts
- Compare at least [two or three] quotes for spends above [INR 50,000] where practical [confirm with finance]
- Pay agreed invoices on time and treat vendor staff with respect
- Keep vendor pricing and terms confidential from other vendors
- Do not take commissions, discounts or favours from vendors for yourself

### Competitors

We compete hard and fairly.

**Do**

- Win business on the quality of our work, our price and our relationships
- Speak about competitors factually and respectfully, or not at all
- Use publicly available information to understand the market

**Do not**

- Spread untrue or damaging claims about a competitor
- Obtain a competitor's confidential information improperly, for example by asking a new joiner for their previous employer's client list or pricing
- Pretend to be a customer to obtain private information
- Agree with a competitor on prices, bids or which clients to approach [competition law applies to such agreements, confirm with a legal adviser]
- Hire or approach a competitor's staff using confidential information

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A prospect shares a competitor's proposal and asks you to beat it | You may discuss your own approach and price. Do not use or pass on the competitor's confidential details beyond what the prospect has openly shared, and tell your manager |
| A new joiner offers to bring their former employer's client list | Decline. Do not accept or use confidential material from a previous employer |
| A competitor invites you to "agree" on minimum prices | Refuse, do not continue the conversation, and tell [legal or senior contact] the same day |
| A colleague criticises a rival agency in a client meeting with unproven claims | Redirect to our strengths. Afterwards, mention to your colleague that this can backfire |

## 7. Confidentiality and client trust

Clients trust us with things they do not share widely: sales figures, ad account access, supplier prices, customer lists, unreleased products and strategy. Protecting that trust is a basic condition of working here, and it continues after you leave.

### What counts as confidential

- Client information: store data, analytics, ad spend, margins, plans, passwords, customer details
- Company information: pricing, contracts, financial data, internal strategy, employee records
- Third-party information: a vendor's quotes, a partner's roadmap
- Anything marked confidential, or that a reasonable person would see as private

### Everyday good practice

**Do**

- Share confidential information only with those who need it for the work
- Use approved tools and channels, and keep client access in the [approved password manager]
- Lock your screen, and be careful in public places: do not discuss client matters in cafes, lifts or on public calls
- Ask before you use a client's name, logo or results in a case study or pitch
- Return or delete confidential material when a project ends, as set out in the client contract

**Do not**

- Discuss client business on social media or with friends
- Forward client data to personal email or messaging apps
- Use a client's confidential information for another client, for yourself, or for a future employer
- Use AI tools with confidential client information unless the policy allows it. See **Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools**

### Client trust goes beyond secrecy

Client trust also means:

- Acting in the client's interest, not ours, when we advise them
- Declaring it if we work for two clients who compete with each other, so the conflict can be managed
- Not using access we have been given for anything other than the agreed work. If you have admin access to a store, you do not browse customer orders for curiosity
- Telling the client promptly if their data may have been exposed

Personal data has its own rules. Please read **Data Privacy and Confidentiality for Everyone** for how to handle personal data, and **Cyber Security Basics for Everyone** for safe practices with passwords and devices. If you believe a data incident has happened, report it at once to [security or privacy contact].

Company property and time are given to us to do our work. Using them properly is a matter of fairness to colleagues and to clients who pay for our hours.

### Company property

Company property includes laptops, phones, software licences, accounts, subscriptions, paid tools, the office, stationery, data, and the company's name and brand.

**Do**

- Use company devices and accounts mainly for work
- Look after equipment, and report loss, theft or damage straight away to [IT or admin contact]
- Follow the IT and security policies on software installation and updates
- Return equipment and accounts when you leave

**Do not**

- Install pirated, unlicensed or unapproved software
- Use company tools or paid subscriptions for personal side businesses
- Lend your login or accounts to others
- Take company equipment or data home or to another employer without approval
- Use the company's name or logo for personal purposes

### Client property and accounts

- Use them only for the agreed work
- Do not change ownership, billing details or admin rights without the client's written agreement
- Never connect a client's ad account or store to your personal accounts
- Hand back all access cleanly at the end of the engagement

## 10. Ethical marketing and advertising

As a marketing agency we shape what the public believes about our clients' products. That is a responsibility. The public, platforms and regulators all expect honest advertising, and our clients' reputations depend on it.

India has a self-regulatory body for advertising, the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI), which publishes guidelines on matters such as misleading claims and the disclosure of paid or sponsored content. Consumer protection law also deals with misleading advertisements. The details change over time, so [confirm the current guidelines and rules with a legal adviser or the ASCI website] before relying on them for a specific campaign.

### Core principles

- **Truthful.** Every claim can be supported by evidence
- **Clear.** Terms, conditions, prices and limits are easy to see and understand
- **Fair.** We do not mislead, exploit fears or target vulnerable people unfairly
- **Disclosed.** Paid, sponsored and affiliate content is labelled as such

### What is not acceptable

| Practice | Why it is a problem |
| --- | --- |
| Fake reviews or ratings written by staff, friends or freelancers | Deceives shoppers and breaks platform and consumer rules |
| Paying for or swapping reviews without disclosure | Same, and it damages the client's credibility |
| Deleting genuine negative reviews that are not abusive | Hides the truth from future customers |
| "Only 2 left!" or countdown timers that reset when the page reloads | Fake urgency is misleading |
| "Sale ends tonight" when the offer continues next week | A false deadline |
| Crossed-out "original" prices that were never charged | A fake discount |
| Claims such as "clinically proven", "100 percent safe", "No. 1" without evidence | Unsupported claims |
| Health or beauty claims that promise cures or guaranteed results | May be unlawful and harmful. Ask a legal adviser |
| Before and after images that are staged, edited or unrepresentative | Misleading |
| Hidden charges, auto-renewals or conditions revealed only at checkout | Unfair to the customer |
| Buying fake followers, likes or engagement | Deceives clients and the audience |

### Influencers, creators and sponsored content

When an influencer, creator or staff member promotes a product in return for money, free products, commission or any other benefit, the audience must be able to tell.

**Do**

- Make sure paid or sponsored posts carry a clear disclosure, using the platform's tools and plain labels such as "ad" or "sponsored" placed where people see them (not buried in a long list of hashtags)
- Brief influencers in writing on honest claims and disclosure, and keep a copy
- Make sure the influencer has actually used or understood the product before they endorse it
- Check what ASCI's current influencer guidelines say about disclosure [confirm the current rules]
- Apply the same honesty to affiliate links and gifted products

**Do not**

- Script fake "personal experiences" or testimonials that did not happen
- Tell an influencer to hide that a post is paid
- Use a customer's photo or review without permission
- Make comparative claims about a competitor without proof

### Targeting, data and persuasion

- Respect privacy rules when targeting audiences and using customer data. See **Data Privacy and Confidentiality for Everyone**
- Be extra careful with advertising aimed at children, health, finance and sensitive topics
- Persuasion is fine. Pressure that exploits fear, guilt or confusion is not
- Make unsubscribing and opting out easy

### Pushing back on a client

Clients sometimes ask for claims or tactics that cross the line. A good agency is a trusted adviser here.

1. Explain the risk in simple terms: customer complaints, platform bans, regulatory notices, damage to brand trust
2. Offer an honest alternative that achieves the same goal ("Let us show real reviews with a verified badge and run a genuine limited-time offer")
3. Document the advice
4. If the client insists on something misleading or unlawful, escalate to [senior manager or legal contact] before doing anything. We may decline the work

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A client wants five-star reviews added "to get things started" | Decline politely. Suggest asking real customers by email after purchase, and sample or seeding campaigns with proper disclosure |
| A landing page has a timer that restarts for every visitor | Advise removal, or tie the timer to a genuine end date |
| An influencer's post has "#ad" lost among 20 other hashtags | Ask the creator to add a clear disclosure at the start or in the visible caption |
| A product page says "reduces hair fall by 90 percent" with no source | Ask for the study or test report. Without evidence, do not publish the claim |
| The client says "Everyone does it" about a misleading tactic | That is not a defence. Explain the risk and offer an alternative |

## 11. Respecting intellectual property

Intellectual property (IP) is the legal name for creations such as designs, code, text, photos, music, logos and brands. Using other people's IP without permission is theft, and it exposes the client and us to claims. Respecting IP also protects what we create.

This chapter is general information, not legal advice. Copyright and licence terms vary. [Confirm any uncertain case with a legal adviser.]

### Assets that need a licence

| Asset | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Fonts | Licence covers web, app and commercial use? How many users or sites? A font on your laptop is not licensed for a client site by default |
| Stock images, video and music | Are they from a licensed source, with the right type (commercial, editorial)? Is the licence in the client's name or ours? |
| Shopify themes | Premium themes are licensed per store. Do not reuse one purchased theme on several client stores |
| Apps and plugins | Paid apps are subscribed per store. Do not use cracked or nulled versions |
| Software | Only licensed or free-to-use software on company devices. No pirated copies |
| Icons, templates, UI kits | Check the licence terms and attribution requirements |
| Code libraries | Open-source licences differ: some require attribution or sharing of changes. Check before including them |

### Do and do not

**Do**

- Buy or obtain a proper licence, and keep the receipt or licence file with the project records
- Give credit where the licence or good practice requires it
- Use images that you created, bought or that are free for commercial use with a clear licence
- Tell the project manager if a client sends an asset you are unsure about, and ask the client to confirm they own the rights

**Do not**

- Download images from search results or social media and put them on a client site
- Use "free" fonts or themes from unknown sites
- Copy a competitor's website text, design or code
- Use music in a video without a licence, including in social posts
- Remove a watermark, a licence notice or a copyright line

### Crediting sources

- Quote with attribution, and keep quotes short. Do not paste whole articles
- Use own words for content and cite statistics with their source and date
- If you use AI tools, check the output for accuracy and originality, and follow the AI policy. See **Acceptable Use of IT and AI Tools**
- Respect creators: credit the photographer, writer or designer if their licence requires it

### Who owns the work

Ownership of work depends on the contract and the law. In general:

- Work created for a client under a contract usually becomes the client's property, subject to the contract terms [confirm with the contract]
- Work created by employees in the course of their job usually belongs to the company [confirm with your employment agreement]
- Freelancers' work belongs to the client or company only if the contract says so in writing
- Our reusable tools, templates and know-how may remain ours, if the contract allows

Ask before you reuse a client's work in your portfolio, case studies or social posts. Do not take client files or designs with you when you leave.

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A designer finds a perfect product image on a Google search | Do not use it. Find a licensed image, shoot a new one or ask the brand for rights |
| A client asks you to "make our site look like this competitor's" | Take inspiration from layout and structure, but do not copy their text, images, code or distinctive design |
| A developer wants to install a paid app from a "free download" site | No. Buy the licence or find an alternative, and tell [IT contact] |
| A freelancer delivers a logo with no contract on ownership | Get a signed assignment or licence before using it commercially |
| A team member keeps a past client's design files for their portfolio | Ask the client for permission, and never share confidential parts |

## 12. Outside activities and moonlighting

Your life outside work is your own, but some outside activities can clash with your duties to the company or our clients. The aim is not to stop you from having interests. It is to make sure they do not harm the company, our clients or your own work.

### Examples of outside activities

- A part-time job or freelance projects
- Running your own business or online store
- Directorships, partnerships or investments in other businesses
- Voluntary work, teaching, writing or speaking
- Political or community activities

### General expectations [confirm against your employment contract and policy]

- Tell [your manager or HR contact] before you start paid or business work outside your job
- Do not take on work that competes with the company or with a current client
- Do not do outside work on company time, or with company equipment, accounts or confidential information
- Make sure your outside work does not affect your performance, availability or health
- Do not use the company's name to promote your outside work

Many employment contracts restrict working for others during employment [confirm yours]. Interns and freelancers have their own agreements, which may allow other work. If unsure, ask.

### Personal views and public activity

You have the right to hold personal views. When you speak publicly:

- Make clear that the views are yours, not the company's
- Do not use confidential information
- Be respectful, as our code applies online. See **Social Media and Public Communication**

### Conflicts of interest

When an outside interest could affect your judgement, or look as if it could, it is called a conflict of interest. Examples: a family member's business becomes our vendor, or you run a side business for one of our clients. Conflicts are not always wrong. Hiding them is. Tell [your manager or compliance contact] as soon as you notice one, and follow [the conflict of interest and gifts policy].

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A designer wants to take on logo projects on weekends | Tell your manager first. It is usually fine if it does not compete with the company or a client and uses no company resources |
| A developer builds a Shopify store for a friend's business during office hours | Not allowed on company time. If it is outside hours, disclose it and check that it is not a client or competitor |
| A marketer is offered a paid advisory role with a competitor of our client | Disclose it before accepting. It may not be possible |
| An intern wants to post about their internship on social media | Fine in general. Do not share client names or confidential details without approval |

## 13. Making ethical decisions: a short method

Most situations are covered by a rule. Some are not. For those, ask yourself a few questions. You do not need to be a philosopher. You need a pause.

### The six questions

1. **Is it legal and does it follow our policies and contracts?** If not, stop.
2. **Is it honest?** Would it leave anyone with a false picture?
3. **Is it fair?** Would I be happy if someone did this to me, or to my family?
4. **Who could be affected?** Colleagues, clients, customers, the public, the company.
5. **Would I be comfortable if it were public?** If it appeared in the news, in the team chat or in front of the client, would I stand by it?
6. **Have I asked for help if I am unsure?** A quick conversation can save a lot of trouble.

### Warning signs

Be careful when you hear or think these:

- "Everyone does it"
- "No one will find out"
- "The client will never notice"
- "It is just this once"
- "I was told to do it"
- "We will fix it later"
- A sense that you would not want to explain it to someone you respect

### Pressure and deadlines

Pressure makes shortcuts tempting. It is a good moment to slow down.

- Tell your manager what the pressure is leading you towards ("To hit the deadline I would need to skip testing")
- Offer options (cut scope, extend time, add resources)
- Never let a deadline justify dishonesty

### A worked example

A performance marketer manages a client's festival campaign. Midway, tracking shows that a third of the purchases were not attributed correctly, so the dashboard shows the return on ad spend at 4.2, but the true figure is probably nearer 3. The client is delighted with 4.2 and the monthly review is tomorrow.

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Legal and within contracts? | Misreporting could breach the contract and mislead the client |
| Honest? | Reporting 4.2 knowing it is likely wrong is not honest |
| Fair? | The client would make budget decisions on wrong numbers |
| Who is affected? | The client, the team's credibility, future work |
| Public test? | We would not want the client to learn about it from someone else |
| Ask for help? | Yes: tell the manager today |

**Decision.** Tell the manager, fix or caveat the tracking, and present the figures with an honest explanation and a plan to correct them. The client may be disappointed. They will also trust us more.

### Checklist before a hard call

- [ ] I understand the facts, and have separated facts from assumptions
- [ ] I have checked the relevant rule or contract
- [ ] I have run the six questions
- [ ] I have talked to my manager, HR or another trusted person where unsure
- [ ] I can explain my decision calmly to anyone affected

## 14. Raising concerns, and how breaches are handled

### Raising a concern

If you see or suspect something that breaks the code or the law, please say so. Speaking up helps us fix problems early. It is not disloyal, and it is not "telling tales".

**Options for raising a concern**

1. Your manager or team lead, if you feel comfortable
2. [HR contact, e.g. hr@company]
3. [Senior leader or compliance contact]
4. The confidential channel described in **Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling)**, [channel or email]

Sexual harassment complaints go to the Internal Committee. See **POSH Awareness for Everyone**.

**What to include**

- What happened, when and where
- Who was involved
- Any evidence (messages, screenshots, documents) that you have, without collecting anything you should not access
- Whether you have already raised it

You do not need proof or certainty. Honest concerns raised in good faith are protected, even if they turn out to be mistaken.

**Do not**

- Investigate on your own, confront the person, or gather confidential data you are not entitled to
- Discuss the matter widely or post about it online
- Make a complaint you know to be false. Deliberately false accusations are themselves a breach

### Protection from retaliation

Retaliation means punishing someone for raising a concern or helping with a review. Examples: unfair treatment, exclusion, poor reviews, threats, changed duties, or being made to leave. Retaliation is a serious breach, treated in its own right and even if the original concern is not upheld. If you feel you are being retaliated against, tell [HR contact or Speaking Up channel] straight away.

### What happens after you raise a concern

| Stage | What happens | Typical time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acknowledge | You are told your concern has been received | [within 3 working days] |
| Assess | The matter is assessed, and the right route chosen | [within 7 working days] |
| Review or investigation | Facts are gathered by someone fair and independent of the matter | [timeline depends on the case] |
| Decision | A decision is made and any action taken | [as per policy] |
| Feedback | You are told the matter is closed, as far as confidentiality allows | [on closure] |

### Consequences of breaches

Breaches of the code are taken seriously, in proportion to their seriousness. Outcomes depend on the facts, intent, harm, honesty after the event, and any previous record.

| Level | Examples | Possible outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minor, unintentional | A late timesheet, a careless remark, a forgotten licence check | Informal conversation, coaching, a reminder |
| Moderate | Repeated disrespect, unapproved outside work, misleading a client without serious harm | Written warning, training, a formal improvement plan |
| Serious | Falsifying records, serious bullying, misuse of client data, accepting a bribe | Suspension, termination, ending a contract, reporting to authorities where required |

The precise steps follow the **HR Policies Handbook** [confirm the disciplinary process]. For freelancers and vendors, contract terms apply.

### How breaches are handled fairly

Everyone involved deserves a fair process.

- **Hear both sides.** The person accused is told what is alleged and can respond
- **No presumption of guilt.** An allegation is not a finding
- **Confidentiality.** Details are shared only with those who need to know
- **Independence.** Reviewers should not have a conflict in the matter
- **Proportionality.** The outcome fits the facts
- **Consistency.** Similar cases are handled in a similar way, whatever the seniority of the person
- **Records.** Decisions and reasons are recorded
- **Learning.** Where a system failure contributed (an unclear rule, impossible deadlines), the system is fixed too

Some matters, such as sexual harassment, theft, fraud or data breaches, have legal duties attached, including reporting to authorities. [Confirm with HR or a legal adviser.]

## 15. A manager's extra duties

Managers and team leads shape the culture more than any document. People watch what you do, what you reward and what you ignore.

### What is expected of managers

**Lead by example**

- Follow the code visibly, in small matters as well as large
- Admit your own mistakes openly
- Keep your promises and treat everyone with respect

**Make it safe to speak up**

- Say clearly that questions and concerns are welcome
- Thank people who raise problems, even when the news is awkward
- Never penalise anyone for honest bad news or for refusing to do something unethical

**Set realistic expectations**

- Plan work with honest estimates, so that no one has to cut corners to meet a deadline
- Do not ask for results to be "made to look better"
- Watch workloads, overtime and burnout. See **Health, Safety and Wellbeing**

**Act on concerns**

- Take every concern seriously, listen without judging, and do not promise secrecy you cannot keep
- Pass it on promptly to [HR contact or the Speaking Up channel] as required
- Do not investigate alone or confront the person involved without guidance
- Protect the person who raised the concern from retaliation

**Make fair decisions**

- Hire, assign and promote on merit
- Treat similar cases consistently
- Declare your own conflicts, such as a relative applying to your team

### Warning signs for managers

| Sign | What it may mean |
| --- | --- |
| Reports always look perfect | Bad news may be hidden or softened |
| Timesheets match budgets exactly | Hours may be moved around |
| Team members avoid raising problems | Fear of blame |
| A team member is always "fine" and never takes leave | Possible pressure or concealment |
| Unusual closeness with a vendor | Possible conflict of interest |

### When someone brings you a concern

1. Listen, and thank them
2. Do not make a judgement or promise an outcome
3. Note what they say, in their words
4. Explain what you must do next (pass it on) and who will contact them
5. Report it within [one working day] to [the right contact]
6. Keep it confidential and check in later

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A team member tells you the client report was adjusted before sending | Thank them, do not confront anyone, and report it to [HR or the Speaking Up channel]. Ask the team to hold the report until reviewed |
| You notice a colleague is regularly mocked in the team chat | Speak to the people involved, set expectations, and tell [HR contact]. Do not wait for a complaint |
| A senior leader asks you to overlook a rule for a key client | Politely decline, explain the risk, and escalate if needed |
| An employee's outside work seems to be affecting their performance | Have a private, respectful conversation and refer to [HR] for the policy |

## 16. Employee questions and sample answers

These are questions people often ask about the code of conduct. The answers are general. For your own situation, check the organisation's written policy or ask [HR contact].

**1. Does the code apply to me if I am an intern or freelancer?**
Yes. It applies to everyone who works for or with the company, including interns and freelancers when they work on our projects. Your contract or agreement may add detail. If something is unclear, ask your point of contact.

**2. What if the code does not cover my situation?**
Use the six questions in chapter 13, and talk to your manager or [HR contact]. The code is a guide to judgement, not a complete list.

**3. I made a mistake in a client report. What should I do?**
Tell your manager as soon as you notice, with the facts and a proposed fix. Early honesty is treated much more kindly than hiding it, and it gives us time to correct it before the client is harmed.

**4. My timesheet does not match what I did because I forgot to log last week. Is that a problem?**
Fill it in now as honestly as you can, and tell [your manager or finance contact] it was late. A late, honest timesheet is a minor issue. A made-up one is not.

**5. Can I round my hours up if I think I was underpaid or under-credited?**
No. If you feel your workload or pay is unfair, raise it with your manager or [HR contact]. Adjusting records is not the right way to address it.

**6. My manager asked me to show only the good numbers in a client report. What do I do?**
Ask politely whether the report can include the full picture, and explain the risk. If the request continues, speak to [a senior manager or the Speaking Up channel]. You will be protected for raising it in good faith.

**7. A client wants a guarantee of first-page rankings. Can I give it?**
No. No one controls search engine rankings. Explain what we will do, what we will report on, and what influences the result. A promise we cannot control creates a problem for the client and for us.

**8. Is it wrong to compete with another agency?**
Not at all. We should compete hard and fairly: on quality, price and service. We should not spread false claims, take confidential information, or agree prices with competitors.

**9. Can I use a free font or stock image I found online for a client?**
Only if the licence clearly allows commercial use for that purpose. If you are not sure, do not use it, or ask [your manager or the design lead]. Keep a record of the licence with the project.

**10. A client asked us to add fake reviews to their store. What do I say?**
Politely decline and explain the risks: customer trust, platform and regulatory action, and damage to the brand. Offer an honest alternative such as asking real customers for reviews. Tell your manager so they can support you.

**11. Do I have to disclose an influencer post as an ad?**
Yes. If there is payment, a free product, a commission or any other benefit, the audience should be able to tell clearly. ASCI publishes guidelines on this [confirm the current rules], and our standard is to disclose clearly and early in the post.

**12. Can I do freelance work on weekends?**
Often yes, but tell [your manager or HR contact] first, because your contract may restrict outside work. It should not compete with us or a client, use company resources, or affect your work.

**13. Can I use my work laptop for personal browsing and shopping?**
Light personal use is usually acceptable [confirm in the Acceptable Use policy], as long as it does not affect your work, cost the company, or break rules. Avoid anything unlawful, unsafe or inappropriate.

**14. What if a colleague is rude to me but not in a way that is clearly harassment?**
Rudeness still matters. You can talk to them directly, if you feel safe, or ask your manager or [HR contact] for help. Not every issue needs a formal process, but you do not have to put up with disrespect.

**15. I saw something that might be a breach, but I am not sure. Should I still report?**
Yes. You do not need proof. Raise it in good faith through any of the channels in chapter 14, and let the right people look into it. You are protected from retaliation.

**16. Will my name be shared if I raise a concern?**
We keep it confidential as far as possible, and share it only with people who need to know. In some cases (for example, a formal inquiry or legal duty) confidentiality has limits, and you will be told what to expect.

**17. What if I raise a concern and it turns out to be wrong?**
If you raised it honestly, nothing happens to you. Only deliberately false accusations are a breach.

**18. Can a client ask me to do something against our code?**
A client can ask, but we do not have to agree. Explain the concern, offer an alternative and escalate to your manager. We may decline the work if needed.

**19. Is it okay to take a client's design for my portfolio?**
Not without the client's permission. The work is usually theirs, and some of it is confidential. Ask your manager first and share only what is approved.

**20. How are breaches treated?**
Fairly and in proportion. Minor, unintentional issues usually lead to coaching. Serious ones, such as falsifying records, can lead to termination. Everyone gets a chance to respond and the process is confidential.

**21. Does the code apply at office parties and outings?**
Yes. Social events connected to work are covered. You should be respected, and you should respect others, whether or not alcohol is served.

**22. What should I do if I am under pressure to cut corners to meet a deadline?**
Tell your manager early and offer options such as reducing scope, extending the timeline or adding help. Never let a deadline be the reason for dishonesty.

**23. A vendor offered me a personal discount. Can I accept?**
Not without checking. Personal benefits from vendors can create a conflict of interest. Tell [your manager or compliance contact] before you accept, and follow [the gifts and hospitality policy].

**24. Where do I report sexual harassment?**
To the Internal Committee, as explained in **POSH Awareness for Everyone**. [IC contact]. You can also talk to [HR contact] for support.

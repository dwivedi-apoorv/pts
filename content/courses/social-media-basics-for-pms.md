# Social Media Marketing Basics

This module gives project managers enough knowledge of social media marketing to follow what a social media manager is saying, review calendars and reports with confidence, and answer client questions without overpromising. It explains the platforms, the content, the workflow and the numbers. It is written for non-specialists and uses online store examples in USD. It does not describe our internal process; see the main onboarding module for that.

## 1. Why project managers need social media basics

Social media looks simple from the outside, since everyone uses it, but running it for a business is a steady, detailed job. A project manager who understands it can:

- Tell a realistic social plan from an empty promise
- Review content calendars and spot gaps, risks and missing approvals
- Explain to a client why followers and likes are not the same as sales
- Handle approvals, deadlines and brand questions without delays
- Read a monthly report and ask the right follow-up questions
- Recognise account and policy risks before they cause damage

**How to use this module.** Read chapters 1 to 12 once. Use chapter 13 and chapter 14 (client questions) when preparing for client calls, and the glossary in chapter 15 when a term is unfamiliar.

## 2. How social media works for a business

**Organic and paid.** Organic social means the posts, stories and videos a brand publishes for free to its followers and anyone the platform decides to show them to. Paid social means ads, which are covered in the performance marketing module. This module focuses on organic work, with a short look at how the two fit together.

**What social media does for a store**

- Builds awareness, so people know the brand exists
- Builds trust, since people check a brand's profile before buying
- Shows products in use and in context
- Keeps existing customers engaged and coming back
- Provides a place for customers to ask questions and give feedback
- Sends some traffic and sales to the store

**What social media usually does not do.** It rarely produces a large, steady flow of direct sales on its own from organic posts alone, especially for smaller accounts. Most brands see social as a mix of awareness, trust and community, with sales helped along by ads, email and the store itself.

**How platforms decide what to show.** Each platform has an algorithm that picks what appears in people's feeds. In plain terms, platforms tend to favour content that people watch for longer, share, save and comment on. The exact rules change often and are not published. No one can control them, so no one can guarantee reach.

## 3. The main platforms and what they suit

Platform features and popularity change quickly. Treat this table as a starting point and check current details with the social media manager.

| Platform | Typical strengths | Common formats | Often suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instagram | Visual brands, discovery, shopping features | Photos, carousels, short videos (Reels), Stories | Fashion, beauty, home, food, lifestyle products |
| Facebook | Wide audience, groups, community, local reach, strong ad tools | Posts, videos, groups, events | Broad audiences, local businesses, community brands |
| TikTok | Short video discovery, trends, strong reach for new accounts | Short videos, live | Products that demonstrate well on video, younger audiences |
| YouTube | Search and long-term video views | Long videos, Shorts | Tutorials, reviews, demonstrations |
| Pinterest | People planning and shopping, with long-lasting content | Pins, boards, product pins | Home, fashion, weddings, food, crafts |
| LinkedIn | Professional audiences | Posts, articles, documents | Business-to-business companies, services |
| X (Twitter) | News, conversation, customer service | Short posts, threads | Brands that talk about news and trends |

**Choosing channels.** More platforms do not mean better results. Most small brands do best by doing two or three channels well rather than seven poorly. The choice depends on where the customers are, what the products look like, and how much content the team can create to a good standard.

## 4. Strategy basics

A good social plan starts with questions, not with posting.

1. **What is the goal?** Common goals are awareness, community, traffic to the store, leads, and customer support. Pick one or two main goals.
2. **Who is the audience?** Age, location, interests and what they want from the brand.
3. **What is the brand voice?** Friendly, expert, playful, premium, direct. A written voice guide keeps everyone consistent.
4. **What are the content pillars?** The three to five themes the brand posts about, such as product showcases, tips and how-tos, customer stories, behind the scenes and offers.
5. **Which channels?** Based on audience and the content the team can produce.
6. **How often?** Consistency matters more than volume. A steady, realistic schedule beats bursts followed by silence.
7. **How will we measure?** Agree the numbers up front, and record a starting point.

**Tip for PMs.** Ask to see the written plan: goals, audience, voice, pillars, channels and frequency. If no plan exists, content tends to become random.

## 5. Content types and formats

| Format | What it is | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single image | One photo or graphic | Product highlights, announcements, quotes |
| Carousel | Several images or slides in one post | Step-by-step guides, product ranges, before and after |
| Short video | A video usually under a minute, such as Reels, TikTok or Shorts | Reach, product demonstrations, trends, personality |
| Stories | Temporary posts that disappear after a day | Daily updates, behind the scenes, polls, countdowns |
| Live | A live broadcast | Launches, question sessions, demonstrations |
| User-generated content (UGC) | Photos and videos created by customers | Trust and proof that people use the product |
| Educational posts | Tips, guides and explanations | Authority and saves |
| Promotional posts | Offers, launches, sale announcements | Sales, with care not to overdo |

**A healthy content mix.** Posts that only sell tend to be ignored. Most brands mix useful or entertaining content with product and offer content, so that people have a reason to follow and engage.

**What makes content work**

- A strong first line or first few seconds
- A clear message and one main idea per post
- Good quality images or video, with consistent style
- A call to action, such as "save this", "shop the collection" or "comment your size"
- Captions written in the brand voice
- Accessibility basics, such as readable text and captions on videos

## 6. The content calendar and workflow

A **content calendar** is a plan of what will be posted, where and when. It prevents last-minute scrambling and makes approvals possible.

**What a calendar usually holds**

- Date and time, platform and format
- The topic and the content pillar it belongs to
- The caption and any hashtags
- The creative (image or video) or a link to it
- The call to action and link
- Status: planned, in creation, in review, approved, scheduled, posted

**The typical workflow**

1. **Plan:** agree themes, launches and offers for the coming period
2. **Create:** write captions and produce images and video
3. **Review:** check quality, accuracy, brand voice and links
4. **Approve:** the client approves in writing
5. **Schedule:** posts are loaded into a scheduling tool
6. **Publish and engage:** posts go live and replies are handled
7. **Report:** results are reviewed and used to plan the next period

**Approval rules that save time**

- Agree who approves, and how long they have
- Send the whole calendar at once, not post by post
- Give the client a clear deadline, with what happens if there is no reply
- Keep approved versions on record

**Tools.** Platforms provide their own scheduling tools, and several third-party scheduling apps exist. The choice matters less than keeping the calendar up to date and shared.

**Tip for PMs.** Late approvals are the most common reason social calendars slip. Set the approval deadline early and remind the client before it.

## 7. Community management

**Community management** means replying to comments and messages, welcoming new followers, and handling feedback. It is part of the service, not an optional extra, since customers judge a brand by how it responds.

**Good practice**

- Reply promptly, usually within a working day, and faster for messages that look like sales questions
- Reply in the brand voice, and stay polite even to rude comments
- Take detailed or sensitive issues to private messages or email
- Thank people for positive comments
- Record common questions so they can be answered in content or on the website

**Handling complaints**

1. Acknowledge the problem publicly and politely
2. Move the details to a private channel
3. Pass it to the client's team if it needs their action
4. Follow up when it is resolved

**Moderation.** Decide in advance what to do about spam, abusive comments and misleading posts, and what the client wants hidden or removed.

**When something goes wrong.** A post that causes offence, a product problem or a viral complaint needs a fast, calm response, and the client must decide the wording. Agree an escalation route in advance, so there is a named person to call.

## 8. Captions, hashtags, trends and timing

- **Captions.** Clear, in the brand voice, with a strong opening line since long captions are cut off after a few lines
- **Hashtags.** They help platforms and people classify content. A few relevant ones usually work better than many generic ones. Their effect varies by platform and changes over time.
- **Trends.** Popular sounds, formats and topics can raise reach, but only when they fit the brand. Jumping on a trend that does not suit the brand, or that touches a sensitive subject, can backfire.
- **Posting times.** The best time depends on the audience. Each platform's insights show when followers are active, so use that data instead of generic advice.
- **Consistency.** Posting regularly teaches both the audience and the platform to expect content.

**Tip for PMs.** Be careful with claims such as "post at 7 pm for best results". Test, look at the account's own data and adjust.

## 9. Paid social, influencers and partnerships

**How organic and paid work together.** Organic posts build the brand and give ads a base of content to promote. Paid ads reach more people than organic posts and let you target particular audiences. Posts that perform well organically are often good candidates for ads.

**Influencers and creators.** Brands work with creators who post about their products, in exchange for payment, free products or commission.

- Choose creators whose audience matches your customers, rather than just those with the biggest numbers
- Check the quality of their engagement, not just follower counts
- Agree in writing the deliverables, dates, usage rights and fees
- Provide a brief, and ask for approval of the content before it goes live
- Track results with links or discount codes

**Disclosure rules.** In many countries, including the US, creators must clearly disclose paid or gifted partnerships, and platforms provide labels for it. Brands share responsibility for making sure that happens.

**Giveaways.** They can raise engagement but often attract people who only want the free item. Check each platform's rules for promotions and any local legal rules.

**Fake followers and engagement.** Buying followers, likes or comments is a poor idea. It breaks platform rules, inflates numbers and damages real performance.

## 10. Social metrics and reporting

| Metric | Formula or meaning | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Followers | Number of accounts following | The size of the audience |
| Follower growth rate | (End followers - Start followers) ÷ Start followers × 100 | How quickly the audience grows |
| Reach | Number of unique accounts that saw the content | How many different people were reached |
| Impressions | Total number of times content was shown | How often it appeared, including repeats |
| Engagements | Likes + comments + shares + saves + clicks (definition varies) | How people interacted |
| Engagement rate | Engagements ÷ Reach × 100 (one common version) | How involving the content is |
| Video views and watch time | Views and average time watched | How well video holds attention |
| Shares and saves | Count of each | Strong signs of useful or valued content |
| Profile visits | Visits to the profile after seeing content | Interest in the brand |
| Link clicks | Clicks on links to the store | Traffic intent |
| Social sessions | Visits to the store from social, in analytics | Traffic actually arriving |
| Social conversion rate | Orders from social visits ÷ Social sessions × 100 | How well social visitors buy |
| Response time | Time taken to reply to messages and comments | Quality of community care |

**Agree on definitions.** Engagement rate can be worked out in several ways, such as by reach, by followers or by impressions. Pick one, write it in the report, and use it every month.

**Worked example.** In one month a store's Instagram account posts 12 times. It starts the month with 8,000 followers and ends with 8,400. Total reach is 48,000, and total engagements (likes, comments, shares and saves) are 2,400. The account sends 600 link clicks, and analytics records 520 sessions from social, which produce 13 orders with an average order value of $90.

| Metric | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follower growth rate | (8,400 - 8,000) ÷ 8,000 × 100 | 5 percent |
| Engagement rate (by reach) | 2,400 ÷ 48,000 × 100 | 5 percent |
| Social conversion rate | 13 ÷ 520 × 100 | 2.5 percent |
| Revenue from social sessions | 13 × $90 | $1,170 |

**What this teaches.** The account grew and engaged well, yet the direct revenue is modest. That is normal for organic social. The value also includes awareness and trust that show up later in other channels. The click count (600) and the sessions count (520) differ because platforms and analytics measure differently, and some clicks are not recorded as visits.

**Vanity metrics and useful metrics.** Followers and likes are easy to count but say little about business results. Reach, saves, shares, link clicks, sessions and orders are closer to outcomes. Reports should lead with the numbers connected to the client's goals.

**A good monthly report** shows the starting point, the results against goals, the best and weakest posts and why, the work completed, what was learned, and the plan for next month.

## 11. Social commerce basics

**Social commerce** means selling directly on social platforms, for example through product tags, shops and live shopping.

- Instagram and Facebook offer shop features that show products from a catalog and let people shop in the app or at the store's checkout
- TikTok offers its own shopping features in some countries
- Pinterest lets brands upload product information so that products appear as shoppable pins
- Availability and rules differ by country, and the platforms set eligibility conditions

**What it needs**

- A product catalog with accurate prices, images and stock
- A connection between the store and the platform, often through Shopify's sales channel tools
- Compliance with the platform's commerce policies
- A plan for handling orders and customer messages that arrive from the platform

**Tip for PMs.** Check eligibility and country availability before promising a social shop to a client, and confirm who will manage orders and messages.

## 12. Account setup, access and risks

**Account basics**

- The client should own their social accounts and the business profile on each platform
- Agencies are added as admins or partners with the right level of access, rather than being given passwords
- Use a business or professional account where the platform offers one, since it provides scheduling, insights and ad tools
- Switch on two-factor authentication, and keep recovery details with the client
- Keep a record of who has access, and remove access when someone leaves

**Risks to manage**

- **Account suspension or loss.** Breaking platform rules, suspicious activity and hacked accounts can lead to restrictions. Recovery can be slow.
- **Copyright.** Using images, videos or music without permission can lead to takedowns or claims. Business accounts often face limits on commercial music, and platforms offer licensed libraries for business use.
- **Unproven claims.** Claims about health, results or income can break platform advertising rules and consumer law.
- **Personal data.** Do not share customer details publicly.
- **Brand safety.** Check comments and tagged content for problems, and avoid sensitive topics unless the client has agreed an approach.

**Handover and records.** Keep a list of accounts, access holders, login methods and the content stored in shared folders, so the client is never dependent on a single person.

## 13. Talking to clients: expectations and red flags

**What we can say with confidence**

- We can plan, create, schedule and publish consistent content and manage the community
- Social media builds awareness, trust and community over time
- We will report results against the goals we agree
- Quality and consistency matter more than the number of posts

**What we must never say**

- A guaranteed number of followers, likes, reach or sales
- That a post or video will "go viral"
- That we can beat the platform's algorithm
- That buying followers or engagement is a safe shortcut

**Better ways to say it**

- Instead of "We will get you 10,000 followers", say "We will grow your audience with consistent, useful content and track follower growth and engagement monthly."
- Instead of "Social will bring sales this month", say "Social supports awareness and trust. We will track clicks, visits and orders from it, and ads can add direct sales."

**Red flags**

- No written goals or voice guide
- Slow approvals that leave the calendar empty
- A client who wants to post only offers and discounts
- A request to buy followers or run risky giveaways
- Competitor content being copied directly
- Nobody on the client side with authority to approve and respond

**When you are unsure.** It is better to say "I will check with the social media manager and come back to you by tomorrow" than to guess.

## 14. Client questions and sample answers

These are answers a project manager can give in plain language. Adapt the wording to the client, and check with the social media manager for anything specific to the account.

**1. What does social media management include?**
It usually includes planning the content, creating or sourcing images, video and captions, getting your approval, scheduling and publishing, replying to comments and messages, and reporting on results. The exact scope is set out in your agreement.

**2. How many followers will we get?**
We cannot promise a number. Growth depends on your products, audience, content quality, consistency and, where used, ads. We will track follower growth against a starting point, and we will explain what is driving it.

**3. How long until we see results?**
Awareness and engagement usually start to build within the first few weeks, but a steady audience and clear patterns take a few months. Direct sales from organic posts are slower and smaller than from ads.

**4. Why aren't we getting many sales from social?**
Organic social mostly builds awareness and trust, and it sends some visitors to the store. Sales often come from a mix of channels. We can improve the links, offers and store experience, and ads can add direct sales.

**5. Which platforms should we be on?**
It depends on where your customers spend time and what your products look like. We usually recommend doing two or three platforms well rather than many poorly, and we will suggest the best fit.

**6. How often should we post?**
Consistency matters more than volume. We agree on a frequency that the team can keep up at a good standard, and then adjust based on results.

**7. Why does the content need our approval?**
The posts speak for your brand, so you should see them first. We send the whole calendar at once with a deadline, so approvals stay quick and the schedule stays on track.

**8. Why did a post do badly?**
Reach varies from post to post for many reasons, including timing, format, topic and platform changes. We look at the pattern across many posts rather than any single one, and we adjust the plan.

**9. Why did our reach drop?**
It can come from platform changes, fewer posts, less engaging content, seasonal changes or an audience shift. We check the trend, compare formats and test improvements.

**10. Should we use trends?**
Yes, when they fit your brand and audience. We avoid trends that feel forced or touch sensitive topics. We will suggest ones that are a good match.

**11. Do hashtags matter?**
They help to categorise content, and a small number of relevant ones works best. They are not a magic fix, and their effect varies by platform.

**12. Can we buy followers or likes to look bigger?**
We do not recommend it. Bought followers are not real customers, they break platform rules and they reduce real engagement and trust.

**13. What about influencers?**
Working with creators can build trust and reach, when their audience matches yours. We recommend checking the quality of their engagement, agreeing the details in writing and requiring clear disclosure of paid or gifted partnerships.

**14. Should we run giveaways?**
They can lift engagement, but often attract people who only want the prize. We suggest them for a clear reason, such as a launch, with platform and legal rules checked first.

**15. Can we sell directly on Instagram or Facebook?**
Often yes, depending on your country and the platform's eligibility rules. It needs a product catalog connected to your store and a plan to handle the orders and messages that come in.

**16. What if someone leaves a negative comment?**
We reply promptly and politely, move details to a private channel, pass issues to your team where needed and follow up. We do not delete honest complaints unless they break the rules, and we agree on a moderation approach with you in advance.

**17. Who owns the social accounts?**
You should. We are added as admins or partners with the access we need, so you keep control and history. We will not ask for your passwords.

**18. Why do you need access to our accounts?**
To schedule posts, read insights, reply to messages and run ads. We use access only for the agreed work, and you can remove it at any time.

**19. Can you use music or images we find online?**
Only with permission or a proper licence. Commercial accounts often face limits on music, and unlicensed images can lead to takedowns or legal claims. We use licensed libraries or content you own or create.

**20. How do you measure success?**
We agree goals first, such as audience growth, engagement and visits to your store, and we report on them each month against a starting point. We also share what worked, what did not and what we will try next.

**21. What do you need from us?**
Brand assets, product information and photos, quick approvals, news about launches and offers, and a contact for urgent questions. Delays in these are a common reason the calendar slips.

**22. Why are the follower and click numbers in your report different from our own tools?**
Platforms and analytics tools measure in different ways, so numbers rarely match exactly. We use the same source each month so the trends are reliable, and we explain the definitions in the report.

**Knowledge check**

1. Name three things social media usually does for a store, and one thing it usually does not do well on its own.
2. Why do most small brands do better on two or three platforms than on many?
3. What are content pillars, and why do they matter?
4. A client sends approvals late each month. What would you change?
5. An account has 10,000 followers at the start of the month and 10,500 at the end. What is the follower growth rate?
6. An account has reach of 20,000 and 1,000 engagements. What is the engagement rate by reach?
7. Social sessions are 400, with 8 orders. What is the social conversion rate?
8. A client asks you to guarantee 50,000 followers in three months. How do you respond?
9. Why should clients own their social accounts?
10. What should you check before posting a creator's paid partnership?

**Answer guide**

1. It builds awareness, trust and community, and it shows products. It usually does not produce large, steady direct sales from organic posts alone.
2. Quality and consistency matter more than the number of platforms, and a small team can create better content for fewer channels.
3. They are the main themes a brand posts about. They keep content focused, varied and consistent with the goals.
4. Set the deadline earlier, send the whole calendar at once, remind the client before the deadline and agree what happens if there is no reply.
5. (10,500 - 10,000) ÷ 10,000 × 100 = 5 percent.
6. 1,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 5 percent.
7. 8 ÷ 400 × 100 = 2 percent.
8. No honest guarantee is possible. Explain what we control, track growth against a starting point, and agree goals that connect to business results.
9. So they keep control, history and the audience, even if the agency or a team member changes.
10. That the partnership is clearly disclosed, the content is approved, usage rights and dates are agreed in writing, and the claims in it are accurate.

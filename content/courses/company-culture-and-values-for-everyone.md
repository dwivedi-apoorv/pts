## 1. Why everyone needs to understand our culture

Culture is not a poster. It is what people do when no one is checking: how a developer answers a worried teammate, how a manager reacts to bad news, how we speak to a client who is upset, what we do when we find our own mistake. Every team has a culture, whether or not anyone designed it. This module describes the one we want to build together.

**This module is for all of us because**

- New joiners learn "how things work here" quickly, and it helps if the answer is written down
- Clear shared habits save time, reduce conflict and make good work easier
- Clients feel our culture in every email, deadline and report
- Culture is made by everyone: a freelancer's behaviour matters as much as a director's

**What this module will help you do**

- Understand our mission and values, and what each looks like in practice
- Communicate, meet and give feedback in ways that help the team
- Know how decisions are made and who to ask
- Understand what each service we sell is, and how teams rely on one another
- Plan your first 30, 60 and 90 days
- Keep the culture healthy, and speak up when it slips

**The four rules to remember**

1. **Behaviour counts more than words.** Values are shown by what we do, especially under pressure.
2. **Tell the truth early.** Bad news is easier to fix on day two than on day ten.
3. **Help is part of the job.** Asking for help and giving it are both signs of a strong team.
4. **Everyone keeps the culture.** If you see something that does not fit our values, say so kindly, or tell [People or HR contact].

## 3. What each value looks like

Each value below has a draft name, a short meaning, behaviours you can observe, and a "what this is not" line, so it does not get misread. Edit them to match what the company truly believes.

### Value 1: [Put the client first]

**Meaning:** The client's real business goal guides our work, more than our own convenience.

**Observable behaviours**

- You ask about the goal behind a request ("What are you hoping this will change?") before starting
- You flag a risk to the client before they discover it
- You reply to client messages inside the agreed time [response time]
- You suggest a better option, even if it is less work for us or a smaller sale
- You see the shop through a customer's eyes: can they find the product and pay without trouble?

**What this is not:** It does not mean saying yes to everything, or letting a client treat colleagues badly. A good partner sometimes says no, kindly and with reasons.

### Value 2: [Own the outcome]

**Meaning:** We care about the result, not only the task. If it does not work, we take responsibility until it does.

**Observable behaviours**

- You check the work after delivery: does the page load, does the tracking record, does the campaign spend as planned?
- You say "I will take care of it" and then close the loop in writing
- You tell your manager early if you cannot meet a commitment, with a proposed new plan
- You fix what you find, even if it is outside your own task, or you pass it clearly to the right person
- You do not blame others in front of the client

**What this is not:** It does not mean working alone, taking blame for everything, or working endless hours. Ownership includes asking for help and setting limits.

### Value 3: [Tell the truth early]

**Meaning:** We share facts, risks, delays and mistakes openly and quickly, with respect.

**Observable behaviours**

- You raise a delay the moment you expect it, not the day before the deadline
- You admit mistakes plainly: "I did this, here is the impact, here is my fix"
- You share numbers as they are, including bad ones, with a short explanation
- You say "I do not know yet" instead of guessing
- You give feedback directly and kindly, not through gossip

**What this is not:** It does not mean being blunt or harsh. Truth is shared with care, and about the work, not the person.

### Value 4: [Keep learning]

**Meaning:** The tools, platforms and search rules change constantly. We stay curious and share what we learn.

**Observable behaviours**

- You set aside time each week to learn [learning hours per month]
- You share a useful article, tip or result in the team's channel
- You ask for feedback after a project and act on it
- You try new methods in a safe way and report what happened
- You teach what you know when a colleague is stuck

**What this is not:** It does not mean chasing every trend or neglecting current work. Learning supports the work we do for clients.

### Value 5: [Win as a team]

**Meaning:** Our clients get great results when design, development, search, advertising and content work as one.

**Observable behaviours**

- You hand over work with clear notes so the next person can start at once
- You ask other teams for input early, not at the end
- You credit others by name in public
- You help a colleague whose work is behind, if you have the time
- You disagree in the room and support the decision outside it

**What this is not:** It does not mean groupthink, or going along in silence. A good team argues about ideas, and then moves together.

## 4. Values in daily agency life

Values are tested in ordinary moments. These scenarios show how the draft values may guide a choice. They are examples, not strict rules.

### Scenario table

| Scenario | What living the values looks like |
| --- | --- |
| You will miss a client deadline for a theme update by two days | [Tell the truth early]: tell your project manager today, with the reason and a new date. [Own the outcome]: propose what can go live on time. [Put the client first]: let the client choose between a partial release and a short wait |
| A client changes the scope in the middle of a sprint and wants it for free | [Put the client first]: understand what they need and why. [Tell the truth early]: explain what the change affects. Involve the project manager to agree on time, cost or trade-offs. Do not quietly absorb extra work or refuse in a short email |
| You notice a wrong discount code in a live ad campaign | [Own the outcome]: pause the ad if authorised, tell your manager and the client. [Tell the truth early]: describe what happened and how you will fix it. Learn and add a check afterwards |
| A colleague on another team has fallen behind on product research and looks stressed | [Win as a team]: ask if you can help, offer a concrete task, tell their manager if the overload is serious |
| A teammate's code contains a bug that you find on review | [Tell the truth early] and [Win as a team]: tell them kindly and privately, show the issue, suggest a fix and thank them for fixing it |
| A client praises you for work a junior colleague mostly did | [Win as a team]: pass the credit on in public and by name |
| A client wants a report with numbers that look better than the truth | [Tell the truth early]: share accurate numbers with context, and suggest honest ways to present progress. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone |

### Worked example: a mistake in an ad campaign

A marketing executive launches a festive-season ad on a client's store. After two hours she sees that the ad links to an out-of-stock product page.

1. She pauses the ad immediately, since she has authority to do so [ad account permissions]
2. She tells her manager and the project manager in the team channel in two lines: what happened, how long it ran, estimated spend
3. The project manager informs the client the same hour with a plain note and a plan
4. She fixes the link, tests it, and relaunches after approval
5. In the next team meeting, she shares what she learned. The team adds a final-link check to the launch list

Nobody is shamed. The truth came early, the owner acted, the team learned, and the client trusted the agency more afterwards.

### Worked example: a colleague who needs help

A designer has missed three internal deadlines and seems withdrawn. A developer notices.

- She asks the designer privately, "I noticed you have been busy. Is there anything I can help with?"
- The designer says she is overloaded with two clients' work
- The developer offers to take the mobile checks, and encourages the designer to talk to the manager
- If the designer was ill or distressed, the developer could also share [wellbeing support contact]

Small acts of care like this are the culture.

## 5. How we work together

Good communication saves more time than any tool. This chapter sets out default habits. Each team may add its own.

### Clear communication

- Write so that someone can act on it without asking you again. Say what you need, by when, and why
- Start with the point: "I need approval on the banner by 4 pm today" is better than a long background
- Use plain words. Avoid jargon with clients and with colleagues from other teams
- One topic per message where possible, with a clear subject line
- Confirm important decisions in writing, even after a call

### Written updates

Written updates keep everyone informed without extra meetings.

| Update | Frequency | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily status note | [Every working day by 6 pm] | [Team chat channel] |
| Weekly project summary | [Every Friday] | [Project tool or email] |
| Client report | [Weekly or monthly, as agreed] | [Client report folder] |
| Blockers | As soon as they appear | [Project channel], tagging the person who can help |

A good status note has three lines: done, next, blocked.

### Meetings that earn their time

- Have a purpose and a clear owner. If you cannot say why the meeting exists, cancel it
- Share an agenda in advance, and keep to the time [default meeting length: 30 minutes]
- Invite only people who need to decide or contribute. Others can read the notes
- End with decisions, owners and dates, written down
- Start on time and let people leave if their part is done
- Do not multitask. Cameras on or off is a personal choice unless a client expects otherwise [camera guidance]

**Meeting checklist**

- [ ] Purpose and outcome stated
- [ ] Agenda shared
- [ ] Right people invited
- [ ] Notes and actions shared the same day

### Async, remote and hybrid etiquette

- Write messages so they make sense when read later, without a call
- Share your working hours and holidays in the calendar [shared calendar]
- Set a clear status: available, in focus time, away
- Use video for sensitive or complex conversations, and chat for quick questions
- On hybrid days, include remote people in meetings: speak into the microphone and share your screen
- Keep client information safe at home or in public places. See Cyber Security Basics for Everyone
- Respect other people's off time. Schedule messages for the morning instead of sending late at night [quiet hours]

### Working across time zones with clients

Many of our clients are in other countries. Time zones should help us, not hurt us.

- Agree overlapping hours with each client at the start [client overlap hours]
- Write the time with the time zone, for example "4 pm IST / 11:30 am GMT"
- Send end-of-day updates so that the client's morning starts with progress
- Avoid asking people to join calls at unusual hours too often. Rotate the burden, and offer time off in return [flexible hours policy]
- Check holidays in both countries. Diwali and local festivals in India, and public holidays abroad, affect deadlines

## 6. Ownership and accountability

Ownership means that every task, risk and promise has a named person. Accountability means that person answers for the result, with support from others.

### What ownership looks like

- Every task has one owner, even when many people help
- You say what you will deliver and by when, and you do it, or you say early that you cannot
- You follow work through to the outcome: tested, live, measured, and reported
- You close the loop: when you finish, you tell the people who are waiting
- You take responsibility for your mistakes, and you do not hide them

### When something is stuck

Ask for help in the right order. The escalation order in the onboarding module gives the sequence to follow when you are blocked, so you can move quickly without going straight to the top. In short, begin with the person closest to the issue, then your manager, and then senior leaders, with the facts written down at each step.

**Before you escalate**

- [ ] I have written down what is stuck, what I tried and what I need
- [ ] I have told the person closest to the issue
- [ ] I have given a clear deadline for the answer
- [ ] I am bringing a proposal, not only a problem

### Accountability without blame

- Ask "what happened and what can we change?" not "whose fault is it?"
- If a process failed, fix the process
- If a person made an error, help them to learn, and give feedback privately
- Repeated or deliberate breaches of the Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone are handled under the HR Policies Handbook, not by the team

### Ownership across teams

| Situation | Ownership approach |
| --- | --- |
| A task needs a developer and a designer | Name one owner for the whole outcome. Others are contributors with clear deliverables |
| A client issue touches ads, tracking and the website | The project manager owns the client communication. Specialists own their fixes and update the owner |
| A task has no owner in the meeting | Do not leave without assigning one. If no one is assigned, the meeting owner decides |

## 7. Giving and receiving feedback, and disagreeing well

Feedback is how we improve. It works best when it is regular, specific and kind.

### Giving feedback

Use a simple frame: situation, behaviour, impact, next step.

> "In yesterday's client call (situation), you answered the pricing question before checking with me (behaviour). The client now expects a discount we have not agreed (impact). Next time, could you say 'let me check and come back', and message me in chat (next step)?"

**Do**
- Give feedback soon, privately, and about the work or behaviour, not the person
- Say what was good as well as what to change
- Ask a question: "How did that feel from your side?"
- Offer help, not only criticism

**Do not**
- Give feedback in front of the team or client unless it is praise
- Use words like "always" and "never"
- Save problems for the annual review. Nothing in a review should be a surprise

### Receiving feedback

- Listen to the end. Say "thank you" and ask a clarifying question
- Do not explain or defend straight away. Take time to think
- Decide what you will do and tell the person, so they know you heard
- If feedback seems unfair, say so calmly and ask for examples. If it still feels wrong, talk to your manager or [People contact]

### Disagreeing well

Disagreement is healthy. Unspoken disagreement is not.

1. State the goal you share: "We both want the launch to go well."
2. Say your view and why. Offer data where you have it
3. Listen to the other view and repeat it back in your words
4. Look for a test, a compromise, or a decision-maker
5. When a decision is made, support it, even if it was not your choice
6. If you still have serious concerns, state them once in writing to the decision-maker

| Scenario | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You think a designer's layout will confuse shoppers, but they disagree | Share the concern with reasons, suggest a quick test or an A/B comparison, and accept the owner's decision if the test is not possible |
| A colleague criticises your work harshly in a group chat | Take it offline. Say calmly, "I would like to discuss this privately." If it repeats, tell your manager or [People contact] |
| A manager gives you vague feedback: "It was not good" | Ask for one example and one thing to change. Thank them for making time |
| You and a peer argue about priority | Escalate to your manager together, each explaining your view in two sentences |

## 8. Psychological safety and admitting mistakes quickly

Psychological safety means people feel safe to ask questions, share ideas, say "I do not know", and report mistakes, without fear of ridicule or punishment. Teams with this safety find problems earlier and make fewer big errors.

### What it looks like

- Questions are welcomed, including basic ones
- People say "I was wrong" without embarrassment
- New ideas are heard before they are judged
- Quiet people are invited to speak
- Mistakes are discussed as lessons, not as scandals

### Admitting mistakes quickly

Speed matters more than polish. Use the "what, impact, fix" format:

1. **What happened.** Plain facts
2. **Impact.** Who or what is affected, and how much
3. **Fix.** What you have done and what you propose
4. **Learning.** What we change so it does not repeat (after the fix)

> "I published the wrong price on the summer sale banner at 10 am. It was live for 40 minutes and about [number] visits saw it. I have corrected it and will add a second check before launch."

### What leaders do

- Thank people for raising problems
- Respond to the issue first, and to the person's error later, kindly
- Share their own mistakes openly
- Never use a mistake as a joke or as a weapon in a review

### What is different: honest mistakes versus misconduct

An honest mistake, quickly reported, is a chance to learn. Hiding a mistake, lying about it, or deliberately ignoring a rule is different, and is dealt with under the Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone and the HR Policies Handbook. If you see something that might be wrong or unsafe, see Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling).

## 11. The client mindset and quality standards

### The client mindset

Clients are people who have put their money, time and sometimes their savings into a store. Treat their business as you would your own.

- **Listen for the goal.** What is the business trying to achieve?
- **Be dependable.** Do what you said, when you said
- **Be honest.** Say what we can and cannot do
- **Be proactive.** Offer ideas and flag risks before being asked
- **Be respectful.** Even when a client is frustrated. If a client is abusive, stop and tell your manager. We do not accept mistreatment of our people, see Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone
- **Protect their data.** See Data Privacy and Confidentiality and Cyber Security Basics for Everyone

### Quality standards (defaults)

These standards apply to all work. Teams add their own checklists.

| Area | Standard (draft) |

| Accuracy | Facts, prices, links, names and numbers are checked before sending |

### Before you deliver

## 13. Wellbeing, inclusion and doing the right thing

### Wellbeing and balance

Good work needs rest. We expect high standards, and we do not expect burnout.

- Take your leave and breaks. Plan leave early so handovers are smooth
- Raise workload concerns early with your manager. Saying "I am overloaded" is a professional act
- Respect working hours and time off. Avoid late-night messages unless it is truly urgent
- Look out for signs of stress in yourself and others
- Use the support available [employee assistance or counselling]

Health, Safety and Wellbeing for Everyone covers workplace safety, stress, mental health support and emergency steps in detail.

### Inclusion and respect

Our team is made of people of different genders, regions, languages, faiths, castes, ages, abilities and backgrounds. Everyone deserves respect and fair chances.

- Use people's correct names and pronouns, and ask if unsure
- Make sure quieter and remote voices are heard in meetings
- Avoid jokes, nicknames and stereotypes that single out a person or group
- Make events, food and timings inclusive
- Share opportunities fairly, not only with people you know best
- Challenge unfair behaviour kindly, or report it

Respect for every colleague is part of our code of conduct. Unwelcome behaviour of a sexual nature is covered by POSH Awareness for Everyone, with a formal process and the Internal Committee [IC contact]. Both are part of our culture, not extra rules.

### Doing the right thing

Our values only mean something if we hold to them when it is hard, for example when a deadline is tight, a client pushes, or no one would notice.

- Follow the Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone in all your work
- Do not fake data, copy work without permission, or promise what we cannot deliver
- Be honest about conflicts of interest, gifts and favours. See Code of Conduct and Ethics for Everyone
- Protect client and customer data. See Data Privacy and Confidentiality
- If you see something wrong, say so. See Speaking Up (Whistleblowing and Grievance Handling). Raising a concern in good faith will not be punished [confirm with the organisation's policy]

| You see a colleague copy a competitor's text for a client's page | Talk to them if safe, or tell your manager. Copying without permission harms the client and us |

## 16. Employee questions and sample answers

These are common questions with practical answers. For anything else, ask your manager or [People or HR contact].

**1. Are the values just words, or do they affect my work?**
They affect it. We use them in hiring, feedback, recognition and reviews, and they guide choices when no rule fits. They only matter if people live them, so notice how you and your team act under pressure.

**2. What if I do not agree with one of the values?**
Say so to your manager or [People contact]. Values should be examined, and the team revisits them [every two years]. Disagreement in good faith is welcome. Acting against the values is not.

**3. A client keeps changing the scope. Should I just do it?**
Do not quietly absorb it, and do not refuse flatly. Tell your project manager, write down the change and its impact on time or cost, and let the project manager and the client agree on the way forward.

**4. How fast should I reply to messages?**
Use the defaults in chapter 5, such as [within 4 working hours] for clients and [within 2 working hours] for internal chat. If you cannot give a full answer, send a short acknowledgement with a time.

**5. I made a mistake that nobody has noticed yet. What should I do?**
Tell your manager and, if a client is affected, the project manager, now. Use what, impact, fix. Early honesty is almost always rewarded, and hidden mistakes grow.

**6. How do I give feedback to someone more senior?**
Be respectful, specific and private. Describe the situation and its impact and offer a suggestion. Most leaders want honest input. If you feel unsafe, speak to [People contact].

**7. I disagree with a decision. What now?**
State your view once, with reasons, to the decision owner. If the decision stands, support it. If you have serious ethical or legal concerns, use Speaking Up.

**8. What does "own the outcome" mean for a junior person?**
It means you care whether your work actually works, you finish what you start, and you ask early when stuck. It does not mean you are alone. Your manager shares responsibility.

**9. Who decides the priority if two managers ask me for different things?**
Tell both managers about the clash and ask them to agree the priority. Do not choose silently. If they cannot agree, ask your own manager to help.

**10. Can I turn off my notifications after work?**
Yes, in normal times. Unless you are on an agreed on-call rota, you are not expected to respond outside working hours. For genuine emergencies, there is [urgent channel].

**11. How do I handle a rude client?**
Stay calm and professional, do not argue, and tell your manager. We value clients, and we also protect our people. Repeated abuse is escalated to [account lead].

**12. I am not in a client-facing role. Why does culture matter to me?**
Because clients feel the whole company. A late invoice, a slow laptop fix or a missed leave approval can slow client work. Your reliability supports the people who talk to clients.

**13. Do remote and office staff get equal treatment?**
They should. Meetings, decisions and recognition must include remote people. If you feel left out, tell your manager or [People contact].

**14. How do I ask for help without looking weak?**
Describe what you tried, what you need and by when. That shows ownership. Most colleagues are glad to help, and you will help others later.

**15. What is the difference between feedback and criticism?**
Feedback is specific, timely and aimed at helping. Criticism is vague or personal. If feedback sounds personal, ask for a specific example and the change wanted.

**16. How do I find a mentor?**
Ask your manager or [People contact] for options, or approach someone whose work you admire. Keep the request small, such as a monthly 30-minute chat.

**17. Is there a budget for my learning?**
The draft default is [Rs. amount per year] and [protected learning time], but check the current figure with your manager or [People contact].

**18. What if I see a colleague being treated unfairly?**
Support the person, and if it is safe, say something at the time. Then tell your manager or [People contact]. If it involves sexual harassment, tell [IC contact].

**19. Am I expected to join every team celebration?**
No. Celebrations are for connecting, not a duty. Join what suits you, and tell the organiser if something excludes you for reasons of health, faith, diet or family.

**20. A teammate is struggling. Is it my place to say something?**
Yes, kindly. Ask privately if you can help. If you are worried about their wellbeing, let their manager or [wellbeing support contact] know, without gossip.

**21. How do I know which team to ask about a client issue?**
Start with the project manager, who knows who should help. The who-is-who table in chapter 9 also lists the leads.

**22. Will the culture change over time?**
Yes, as the company grows. We use surveys, retrospectives and town halls to adjust, and your input matters.
